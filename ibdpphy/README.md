## Reference pages

The equations are data: in `assets/equations.js` each topic lists its equations and a `syms` dictionary, and every symbol found in the dictionary gets an explanation on hover, focus or tap. Write a symbol in `syms` exactly as it appears in the equation (`'F_\\mathrm{f}'`), and give an equation its own `syms` where a letter means something different there. The booklet shows every topic; the course summary, otherwise plain HTML, shows a topic's equations wherever it has `<div data-eqs="A.1 sl"></div>` (`hl` for the additional HL ones, placed inside the terracotta block), so an equation is edited once for both pages.

No equation scrolls sideways. Each is typeset in parts, split before every top-level `=`, and the parts wrap; a part still wider than its column is scaled down. Keep a row to one relation where you can: a long chain or a “where” clause reads better as two rows. The booklet follows version 1.2 (updated November 2023), the latest the author holds; when the IB revises the booklet or the guide, update the page, its version line and the course summary together. Both are the IB's copyright, so these pages explain and summarise and do not reproduce their text.

## Marking higher level content

Terracotta (`--hl`, with `--hl-text` where it is text on paper) means higher level only and is used for nothing else. The other colours come from the same palette: slate `#2C4654`, teal `#4F9C8F`, sage `#96B083`, sand `#E2C678`, apricot `#E8A66C` and terracotta `#D87759`.

- A slide that is additional HL has the class `hl` on its `<section>`. That gives it the terracotta edge, the "HL only" flag and a terracotta footer, and tags its thumbnail.
- Write the footer of such a slide as `… · ADDITIONAL HL` (or `… · HL ONLY` in a topic with no SL content) and of every other slide as `… · SL + HL`.
- One HL item on a mixed slide takes `<span class="hl-tag">HL only</span>`; a summary card takes `class="term hl"` or `class="card hl"`.
- On the hub, a resource that is HL only uses `class="card t-deck hl"` and `<span class="level hl">`.

## Editing a deck

Each slide is a `<section class="slide">` in the deck's `index.html`; a diagram is `<div class="fig" data-fig="name">`, drawn by the function of that name in `decks/assets/figures.js`. A student note goes inside the slide as `<aside class="revision-note" hidden>`. Maths is LaTeX between `\(` and `\)`, typeset by MathJax as in PanPhy Markdown (`decks/assets/deck-math.js` holds the setup); `\class{res}{…}` colours a result. Write a division as a fraction (`\frac`, or `\dfrac` in worked examples), never with ÷. A one-line bullet point that contains a `\dfrac` takes the class `frac`, which keeps its dot level with the text. In a worked example, write the equation in symbols first, rearrange it for the unknown, then substitute the numbers: the arithmetic is always the last step (`a = \dfrac{v - u}{t} = \dfrac{30 - 12}{6.0} = …`, never a line that opens with numbers). A long line widens its column and shrinks the figure beside it, so put the symbols and the numbers on two lines when one line is too wide. Labels inside the SVG diagrams are plain text, not LaTeX. MathJax comes from the same CDN address as PanPhy Markdown; if it cannot load, the deck still opens and shows the LaTeX source.

The seven newer decks keep their diagrams in a `figures.js` of their own, which calls `DeckFigures.add(parts => ({ 'd1-name': el => svg }))`; `parts` is the set of drawing helpers from the shared file. Prefix every name with the topic, because all figures share one list and a repeated name silently replaces the earlier figure. A new deck can do the same or add to the shared file.

A lesson map does not move: give each of its figures `data-still`, which keeps the still picture and drops anything that plays or waits for a press. Moving pictures in those decks are SMIL animations inside the SVG, made with the `track`, `anim` and `spin` helpers. Draw the figure twice with `twoState(still, mover)`: `.still` is what thumbnails, print and reduced motion show, `.mover` is what plays on screen. A looping animation runs as soon as the slide opens. One that should play once on a press of → is made with `{ loop: false }` and the empty `PLAY` step in the same SVG; `deck.js` starts every such animation when that step is shown and swaps in a fresh copy of the figure when it is hidden again. A panel holds a figure about 550 to 800 px wide and 540 px high, so keep a `viewBox` near 800 × 500 and labels at `.lbl` size or above.

Give a deck story slides whenever a suitable story and a freely licensed picture exist: a short piece of history tied to the physics on the slides around it, as a `class="slide history"` slide with the “Background story · not assessed” label, photographs in the deck’s `images/` folder, a caption, and a credit linking to the source. Check every date and number against a named source and record it below.

A.2 has three story slides (10, 23 and 33) with photographs in `decks/a2-forces-and-momentum/images/`, all from Wikimedia Commons and public domain. Their facts were checked on 13 October 2026 against the sources named here. Newton: the title page itself gives Pepys’s imprimatur of 5 July 1686 and the year 1687, and Halley’s financial risk is from the Wikipedia article on the *Principia*, citing Westfall (1980). Stokes: the drag formula is equation 126 of his 1851 paper “On the effect of the internal friction of fluids on the motion of pendulums” (*Transactions of the Cambridge Philosophical Society* 9), as cited in the Wikipedia article on Stokes’s law, and he was Lucasian Professor from 1849 to 1903. Goddard: the date, the fuels, the 2.5 s flight, 41 feet (12.5 m) of height, 184 feet (56 m) of range and the 1920 editorial are from NASA’s “95 Years Ago: Goddard’s First Liquid-Fueled Rocket” (2021); Commons dates the photograph itself to 8 March 1926, so the caption says only March.

The guide gives A.2 no additional HL content, but its guidance makes calculations on two-dimensional collisions HL only, so those two slides have the `hl` class. Its worked examples use `g` = 9.8 and the symbols of the data booklet (`F_N`, `F_f`, `F_H`, `F_d`, `F_b`, `F_g`); no example needs momentum and energy solved together, banked tracks are in a note in words only, and the vertical circle is treated at its top and bottom only, all as the guidance requires. Its diagrams are the `// ---- A.2: forces and momentum ----` block of `figures.js`: forces are teal, velocities and components apricot, every force arrow starts where the force acts (weight at the centre of mass, a normal force or friction at the surface in contact, a tension where the string is attached, an applied force where it is applied) and no body is treated as a point, and `data-plain` gives the unlabelled copies used on the title, lesson-map and section slides. The collision pictures are one figure, `collide`, whose bodies for each `data-kind` are listed in `COLLISIONS` with their positions before, at contact and after; change a worked example and its entry there together. Each body has its own colour (`BODY_COLOURS`). On screen one press of → plays the figure: the bodies come together and move apart without a pause, at speeds in proportion to the values on the slide, so its panel comes first in the slide (`split fig-first`) and plays before the working is revealed. Thumbnails, print and reduced motion show the still before-and-after rows instead (`.still` and `.mover` in each figure; the motion is the `.cbody` rules in `deck.css`). `sphere-fall` (viscous drag) is played the same way: the sphere is released near the surface and sinks, while its drag and velocity arrows grow with a speed that rises as 1 − e^(−t/τ), from the `sink`, `speed-grow` and `speed-head` keyframes in `deck.css`; a speed–time graph beside it is drawn in step, from the same time constant (0.9 s of a 5 s fall), so change the curve in `figures.js` and those keyframes together; its arrow lengths are chosen so that buoyancy and drag together equal the weight at the end. `collide-2d` works the same way; its collision is off-centre, with the line joining the two centres at contact drawn along the struck puck’s final velocity, so keep that geometry if its numbers or radii change.

Notes on those decks:

- **A.3** never uses terracotta. Its fuel table gives both specific energy (J kg⁻¹) and energy density (J m⁻³), with rounded textbook values. The roller-coaster cart moves at the speed energy conservation gives it, and its two bars are driven by the same list of positions (`RIDE` in the deck’s `figures.js`).
- **B.4** writes the first law as `ΔU = Q − W`, the author’s form, where the data booklet and the reference pages have `Q = ΔU + W`; a note on the first-law slide tells students the two are the same. The sign convention is the booklet’s (`Q` supplied to the gas, `W` done by it), and calculations use monatomic ideal gases only. The four processes are drawn as lines through one state with no arrowheads, because each can go either way; arrows are kept for a named expansion and for cycles. The gas atoms in the piston and free-expansion figures never stop (`gas` in the deck’s `figures.js`), and the piston on the lesson map takes `data-still` so that it does not move. Its Carnot loop is drawn from `PV` and `PV^(5/3)` curves through the corner states listed at the top of its `figures.js`.
- **Field lines** in D.1 to D.4 are unbroken lines that end on the mass, charge, plate or pole, with the arrowhead part of the way along and never at the tip (`fline` in the shared `figures.js` for a straight one). An arrow with its head at the tip is kept for a vector at a point: a force, a velocity or a field strength.
- **D.1** works with circular orbits only, finds a resultant field only on the line joining two bodies and treats atmospheric drag in words, as the guidance requires. Its numbers use `G` = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², an Earth of 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg and radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m, a Moon of 7.35 × 10²² kg at 3.84 × 10⁸ m. The planet in the Kepler figure moves by Kepler’s equation, so its swept areas really are equal.
- **D.2** draws the field lines of point charges, the bar magnet, the coil and the solenoid by tracing the field numerically (`follow` in its `figures.js`), and equipotentials by tracing at right angles to it. The coil and solenoid are traced as long straight wires seen end on, which gives the right pattern but not the exact field of a real coil.
- **D.3** never uses terracotta. It teaches the left-hand rule as “FBI”: thumb `F`, index finger `B`, middle finger `I`. The fingers are always called thumb, index finger and middle finger, never first and second finger, which confuses students whose first language is not English; a note says that any correct rule is accepted. The hand on slide 11 is a Three.js model (`hand.js`): each finger, the palm and the forearm is a skin lofted along a curve through a chain of joints listed at the top of the file, swaying slowly. The figure’s `data-hand` picks the look: `facet`, the one in use (few, flat faces), `smooth` or `toon` (flat tones with an outline). It is drawn with HTML labels placed at the arrow tips each frame. The slide’s thumbnail shows a still of the model, taken by `hand.js` once it is built and again when the theme changes. The flat `d3-rule` picture is what prints and what shows if Three.js cannot load.
- **D.4** gives the peak emf of a rotating coil, `NBAω`, as a derived result, since it is not in the data booklet. Self-induction is in words only.
- **E.3** began six diagrams as copies from `gcsephy/decks/atoms-and-radiation/figures.js` (isotopes, alpha, gamma, penetration, half-life and random decay); the two decks are separate. Its binding energy curve is plotted from measured values listed in the figure. SL slides use whole numbers of half-lives; the exponential law is in the HL part only.

Constants are the values in the 2025 data booklet (checked 12 October 2026): `h` = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, `hc` = 1.24 × 10⁻⁶ eV m, `k` = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻², `e` = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, `u` = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and Fermi radius `R₀` = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. Worked examples carry rounded values forward exactly as shown on the slide, so recheck every line of an example after changing one number.

Slides are drawn on a 1600 × 900 frame and must be readable from the back of a classroom. Keep anything a student has to read at 22 px or more on that frame: body text is 26 to 35 px, and the given-data line of a worked example (`.say`), table and panel headings and photo captions are 22 to 24 px. Diagram labels (`.lbl` 28 px, `.lbl.mono` 26 px, `.lbl.small` 24 px in `deck.css`) are scaled with their diagram, so a wide `viewBox` in a narrow panel shrinks them: check a new diagram on its slide, and trim the `viewBox` or move labels inward if they come out under 22 px. Only the footer, photo credits and the “not assessed” tag are smaller on purpose.
