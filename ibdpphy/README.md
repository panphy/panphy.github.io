## Reference pages

The equations are data: in `assets/equations.js` each topic lists its equations and a `syms` dictionary, and every symbol found in the dictionary gets an explanation on hover, focus or tap. Write a symbol in `syms` exactly as it appears in the equation (`'F_\\mathrm{f}'`), and give an equation its own `syms` where a letter means something different there. The booklet shows every topic; the course summary, otherwise plain HTML, shows a topic's equations wherever it has `<div data-eqs="A.1 sl"></div>` (`hl` for the additional HL ones, placed inside the terracotta block), so an equation is edited once for both pages.

No equation scrolls sideways. Each is typeset in parts, split before every top-level `=`, and the parts wrap; a part still wider than its column is scaled down. Keep a row to one relation where you can: a long chain or a “where” clause reads better as two rows. The booklet follows version 1.2 (updated November 2023), the latest the author holds; when the IB revises the booklet or the guide, update the page, its version line and the course summary together. Both are the IB's copyright, so these pages explain and summarise and do not reproduce their text.

## Marking higher level content

Terracotta (`--hl`, with `--hl-text` where it is text on paper) means higher level only and is used for nothing else. The other colours come from the same palette: slate `#2C4654`, teal `#4F9C8F`, sage `#96B083`, sand `#E2C678`, apricot `#E8A66C` and terracotta `#D87759`.

- A slide that is additional HL has the class `hl` on its `<section>`. That gives it the terracotta edge, the "HL only" flag and a terracotta footer, and tags its thumbnail.
- Write the footer of such a slide as `… · ADDITIONAL HL` (or `… · HL ONLY` in a topic with no SL content) and of every other slide as `… · SL + HL`.
- One HL item on a mixed slide takes `<span class="hl-tag">HL only</span>`; a summary card takes `class="term hl"` or `class="card hl"`.
- On the hub, a resource that is HL only uses `class="card t-deck hl"` and `<span class="level hl">`.

## Editing a deck

Each slide is a `<section class="slide">` in the deck's `index.html`; a diagram is `<div class="fig" data-fig="name">`, drawn by the function of that name in `decks/assets/figures.js`. A student note goes inside the slide as `<aside class="revision-note" hidden>`. Maths is LaTeX between `\(` and `\)`, typeset by MathJax as in PanPhy Markdown (`decks/assets/deck-math.js` holds the setup); `\class{res}{…}` colours a result. Write a division as a fraction (`\frac`, or `\dfrac` in worked examples), never with ÷. Symbols in the slide text are LaTeX as well: Greek letters (`\(\theta\)`), fractions, powers, arithmetic, scientific notation and nuclide symbols (`\({}^{235}\mathrm{U}\)`, not Unicode superscripts). Units in running text and word equations may stay as plain text. A one-line bullet point that contains a `\dfrac` takes the class `frac`, which keeps its dot level with the text. In a worked example, write the equation in symbols first, rearrange it for the unknown, then substitute the numbers: the arithmetic is always the last step (`a = \dfrac{v - u}{t} = \dfrac{30 - 12}{6.0} = …`, never a line that opens with numbers). A long line widens its column and shrinks the figure beside it, so put the symbols and the numbers on two lines when one line is too wide. Labels inside the SVG diagrams are plain text, not LaTeX; the labels and captions of the Three.js models (`model3d.js`) are HTML and may hold LaTeX, which MathJax typesets. MathJax comes from the same CDN address as PanPhy Markdown; if it cannot load, the deck still opens and shows the LaTeX source.

Every deck except A.1, A.2, E.1 and E.2 keeps its diagrams in a `figures.js` of its own, which calls `DeckFigures.add(parts => ({ 'd1-name': el => svg }))`; `parts` is the set of drawing helpers from the shared file. Prefix every name with the topic, because all figures share one list and a repeated name silently replaces the earlier figure. A new deck can do the same or add to the shared file.

A lesson map does not move: give each of its figures `data-still`, which keeps the still picture and drops anything that plays, draws itself in, pulses or waits for a press. Moving pictures in those decks are SMIL animations inside the SVG, made with the `track`, `anim` and `spin` helpers. Draw the figure twice with `twoState(still, mover)`: `.still` is what thumbnails, print and reduced motion show, `.mover` is what plays on screen. A looping animation runs as soon as the slide opens. One that should play once on a press of → is made with `{ loop: false }` and the empty `PLAY` step in the same SVG; `deck.js` starts every such animation when that step is shown and swaps in a fresh copy of the figure when it is hidden again. A panel holds a figure about 550 to 800 px wide and 540 px high, so keep a `viewBox` near 800 × 500 and labels at `.lbl` size or above.

Give a deck story slides whenever a suitable story and a freely licensed picture exist: a short piece of history tied to the physics on the slides around it, as a `class="slide history"` slide with the “Background story · not assessed” label, photographs in the deck’s `images/` folder, a caption, and a credit linking to the source. Check every date and number against a named source and record it below.

Thumbnails, print and reduced motion show the still before-and-after rows instead (`.still` and `.mover` in each figure; the motion is the `.cbody` rules in `deck.css`). `sphere-fall` (viscous drag) is played the same way: the sphere is released near the surface and sinks, while its drag and velocity arrows grow with a speed that rises as 1 − e^(−t/τ), from the `sink`, `speed-grow` and `speed-head` keyframes in `deck.css`; a speed–time graph beside it is drawn in step, from the same time constant (0.9 s of a 5 s fall), so change the curve in `figures.js` and those keyframes together; its arrow lengths are chosen so that buoyancy and drag together equal the weight at the end. `collide-2d` works the same way; its collision is off-centre, with the line joining the two centres at contact drawn along the struck puck’s final velocity, so keep that geometry if its numbers or radii change.

- **B.1, B.2, B.3 and B.5** never use terracotta. B.1’s black-body curves are drawn from Planck’s law, so their peaks obey Wien’s law, with wavelengths in nm; its conduction figure shows the strong vibration spreading from the hot end until the bar reaches a steady gradient; slide 34 is a Three.js model (`brightness.js`), built like the D.3 hand: see-through spheres of radius d and 2d round the source, with the patch one cone lights on each (area A, then 4A, glowing a quarter as brightly) and a wavefront spreading out, so that the power is seen to cover the whole 4πr²; it can be dragged to turn and scrolled or pinched to zoom, and a double-click restores the view; its tables of specific heat capacity, conductivity and so on hold rounded textbook values.
- **B.2** writes albedo as `α` and emissivity as `ϵ` (`\epsilon`, distinct from the `ε` of emf); the guide and data booklet give both in words only, with no symbol. Its slide 8 is a Three.js model (`quarter.js`) of sunlight meeting a turning planet, showing the disc it intercepts and the sphere that shares the power. The Sun’s spectrum is gold and the Earth’s a thin pink line. On slide 17 the infrared arrows appear one after another, absorbed and re-emitted by the molecules. B.2 takes an albedo of 0.30 and the data booklet’s solar constant, which give 238 W m⁻² absorbed and 255 K; its one-layer model gives 303 K, and 288 K when the layer absorbs 78% of the infrared. The guide defines emissivity only in words, so the deck writes `e` for it. The graph of carbon dioxide is a smooth fit to the Mauna Loa record with a yearly ripple added, not the data, and says so on the slide.
- **B.3** calls the three laws by what is held constant and gives Boyle’s and Charles’s names only in passing; “the pressure law” has no agreed name. `gas` in its `figures.js` is a copy of the always-moving gas of B.4.
- **B.5** draws its circuits with `branch` in its `figures.js`, which lays the data booklet’s symbols along a wire; the longer plate of a cell is positive. Conventional current is apricot and electrons are blue. The potential divider equation is derived on the slide because it is not in the data booklet.
- **B.4** writes the first law as `ΔU = Q − W`, the author’s form, where the data booklet and the reference pages have `Q = ΔU + W`; a note on the first-law slide tells students the two are the same. The sign convention is the booklet’s (`Q` supplied to the gas, `W` done by it), and calculations use monatomic ideal gases only. The four processes are drawn as lines through one state with no arrowheads, because each can go either way; arrows are kept for a named expansion and for cycles. The gas atoms in the piston and free-expansion figures never stop (`gas` in the deck’s `figures.js`), and the piston on the lesson map takes `data-still` so that it does not move. Its Carnot loop is drawn from `PV` and `PV^(5/3)` curves through the corner states listed at the top of its `figures.js`.
- **C.1 to C.5** use terracotta only for HL: the phase curve of C.1 and the single-slit, modulated and many-slit patterns and grating beams of C.3. C.2 and C.4 never use it.
- **C.1** draws displacement in teal, velocity in apricot and acceleration in slate, and kinetic energy in apricot against potential in teal. Its HL examples share one oscillator (0.20 kg, 0.80 s, 0.050 m), so change them together.
- **C.2** takes the spectrum’s boundaries from the data booklet page. Its longitudinal graphs count displacement to the right as positive, which puts a compression where the graph falls through zero.
- **C.3** works at normal incidence only. Its intensity patterns are computed (`sinc2` and `slits` in its `figures.js`); the modulated pattern has a slit separation of four slit widths, as the slide says. Figures that are clipped (`clipped`) mark the clip rectangle visible so that they still show in a thumbnail.
- **C.4** says “first harmonic”, never “fundamental” or “overtone”, calls pipes open or closed, uses displacement nodes only and needs no end corrections, as the guidance requires. The closed pipe’s patterns are labelled first, third and fifth harmonic.
- **C.5** never has the source and the observer both moving. Its four figures are not SMIL: each is one model drawn at a time t (`sourceAt`, `observerAt`, `radarAt` and `linesAt` in its `figures.js`), which gives the still and is redrawn every frame on the visible slide from `data-live` groups, so the wavefronts, the observers’ flashes and the traces of what each hears always agree. The moving source runs at half the wave speed, the walking observer at half and the radar’s car at a quarter (much exaggerated), so the traces show 2f and ⅔f, 1.5f, and ⁵⁄₃f; change a speed and its label together. The moving source crosses the picture and starts again as its last wavefronts fade, because a moving source has no smooth loop. The galaxy’s lines are shifted by z = 0.08 of their own wavelengths. The doubled shift of a reflected wave is in a note only.
- **Field lines** in D.1 to D.4 are unbroken lines that end on the mass, charge, plate or pole, with the arrowhead part of the way along and never at the tip (`fline` in the shared `figures.js` for a straight one). An arrow with its head at the tip is kept for a vector at a point: a force, a velocity or a field strength.
- **D.1** works with circular orbits only, finds a resultant field only on the line joining two bodies and treats atmospheric drag in words, as the guidance requires. Its numbers use `G` = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², an Earth of 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg and radius 6.37 × 10⁶ m, a Moon of 7.35 × 10²² kg at 3.84 × 10⁸ m. The planet in the Kepler figure moves by Kepler’s equation, so its swept areas really are equal.
- **D.2** draws the field lines of point charges, the bar magnet, the coil and the solenoid by tracing the field numerically (`follow` in its `figures.js`), and equipotentials by tracing at right angles to it. The coil and solenoid are traced as long straight wires seen end on, which gives the right pattern but not the exact field of a real coil.
- **D.3** never uses terracotta. It teaches the left-hand rule as “FBI”: thumb `F`, index finger `B`, middle finger `I`. The fingers are always called thumb, index finger and middle finger, never first and second finger, which confuses students whose first language is not English; a note says that any correct rule is accepted. The hand on slide 11 is a Three.js model (`hand.js`): each finger, the palm and the forearm is a skin lofted along a curve through a chain of joints listed at the top of the file, swaying slowly. The figure’s `data-hand` picks the look: `facet`, the one in use (few, flat faces), `smooth` or `toon` (flat tones with an outline). It is drawn with HTML labels placed at the arrow tips each frame. The slide’s thumbnail shows a still of the model, taken by `hand.js` once it is built and again when the theme changes. The flat `d3-rule` picture is what prints and what shows if Three.js cannot load.
- **D.4** gives the peak emf of a rotating coil, `NBAω`, as a derived result, since it is not in the data booklet. Self-induction is in words only.
- **E.4 and E.5** never use terracotta. Their nuclei follow E.3: one drawn nucleon by nucleon (`cluster`) has exactly the numbers in its label, and the chain reaction uses plain spheres. E.4’s example reaction is n + ²³⁵U → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n throughout, worked both from binding energies per nucleon (7.59, 8.33 and 8.51 MeV, giving 174 MeV) and from atomic masses (173 MeV); later slides carry 173 MeV forward. Its binding energy curve is drawn from the semi-empirical mass formula and its waste curve is a sketch, as the slides say.
- **E.5** uses `L☉` = 3.85 × 10²⁶ W, which is not in the data booklet and is given on the slide, with 5800 K for the Sun. Its binding energy curve joins measured values, with the horizontal scale stretched below `A` = 60. The stars of its HR diagram are generated (`stars` in its `figures.js`), not real data, and the diagram is labelled illustrative; temperature decreases to the right and both axes are logarithmic, as the guide requires. Cepheid variables are left out.
- **E.3** began six diagrams as copies from `gcsephy/decks/atoms-and-radiation/figures.js` (isotopes, alpha, gamma, penetration, half-life and random decay); the two decks are separate. Its binding energy curve is plotted from measured values listed in the figure. SL slides use whole numbers of half-lives; the exponential law is in the HL part only. Its N–Z graph puts both axes on one scale, so that N = Z is at 45°. The strong-force graph is a sketch, and the slide says the curves are not required: the guide asks only that the force is attractive and short range.

Constants are the values in the 2025 data booklet (checked 12 October 2026): `h` = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, `hc` = 1.24 × 10⁻⁶ eV m, `k` = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻², `e` = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, `u` = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and Fermi radius `R₀` = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. Worked examples carry rounded values forward exactly as shown on the slide, so recheck every line of an example after changing one number.

Slides are drawn on a 1600 × 900 frame and must be readable from the back of a classroom. Keep anything a student has to read at 22 px or more on that frame: body text is 26 to 35 px, and the given-data line of a worked example (`.say`), table and panel headings and photo captions are 22 to 24 px. Diagram labels (`.lbl` 28 px, `.lbl.mono` 26 px, `.lbl.small` 24 px in `deck.css`) are scaled with their diagram, so a wide `viewBox` in a narrow panel shrinks them: check a new diagram on its slide, and trim the `viewBox` or move labels inward if they come out under 22 px. Only the footer, photo credits and the “not assessed” tag are smaller on purpose.
