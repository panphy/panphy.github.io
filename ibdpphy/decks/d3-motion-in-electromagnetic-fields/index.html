Along the field

A charge in a uniform electric field.

AN ELECTRON BETWEEN TWO PLATESPRESS → TO RELEASE
  • Constant force \(F = qE\), so constant acceleration
  • \(a = \dfrac{qE}{m}\)
  • Work done by the field becomes kinetic energy
\(qV = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}\)
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Two ways to find the final speed. The force is constant, so the equations of motion from A.1 work: \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\) with \(a = \frac{qE}{m}\). But energy is quicker. The field does work \(qV\) on the charge whatever the distance between the plates, and all of it becomes kinetic energy. The speed depends only on the potential difference crossed, not on how far apart the plates are.

Why gravity is ignored. For an electron in a typical laboratory field, the electric force is more than \(10^{13}\) times its weight. The electron falls by far less than the width of an atom while it crosses the apparatus.

An electron accelerated from rest through \(V\) volts has a kinetic energy of \(V\) electronvolts. A “2 keV electron” has been through 2000 V.

Sending a negative charge along the field. An electron accelerates towards the positive plate, against the field lines.

Worked example

An electron accelerated through 2000 V.

From rest · \(V = 2000 \,\mathrm{V}\) \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{C}\) · \(m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \,\mathrm{kg}\) \(eV = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}\)  so  \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2eV}{m}}\) \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2000}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}}\) \(v = \class{res}{2.65 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
  • Kinetic energy gained: \(2000 \,\mathrm{eV} = 3.2 \times 10^{-16} \,\mathrm{J}\)
  • About 9% of the speed of light
  • Four times the voltage gives twice the speed
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Why speed goes as the square root of voltage. The energy gained is proportional to \(V\), and kinetic energy is proportional to \(v^{2}\). So \(v \propto \sqrt{V}\): to double the speed you need four times the accelerating voltage.

A different particle. A proton through the same 2000 V gains the same energy, 2000 eV, because its charge has the same size. But its mass is 1836 times larger, so its speed is \(\sqrt{1836} = 43\) times smaller: \(6.2 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\).

Above about 10% of the speed of light this equation starts to overestimate the speed, and relativity (A.5, HL) is needed. Exam questions stay below that.

Across the field

Entering an electric field at right angles.

AN ELECTRON BETWEEN CHARGED PLATESPRESS → TO FIRE
  • Along the plates: no force, constant velocity
  • Across the plates: constant force, constant acceleration
  • The path is a parabola, like a projectile
  • Outside the field: a straight line
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Projectile motion with a different force. In A.1 a ball thrown horizontally keeps its horizontal velocity while gravity accelerates it downwards, and it follows a parabola. Here the constant force is \(qE\) and the acceleration is \(\frac{qE}{m}\) instead of \(g\). Everything else is the same: treat the two directions separately and link them through the time.

The method. Find the time inside the field from the motion along the plates, \(t = \frac{L}{v}\). Use that time for the motion across: deflection \(\frac{1}{2} a t^{2}\) and sideways velocity \(at\). After the plates, both components of velocity are constant, so the path is straight, along the tangent to the parabola at the exit.

The kinetic energy of the particle increases in an electric field: the field does work on it. Compare this with a magnetic field, in Part 2.

Worked example

How far is the electron deflected?.

\(v = 2.0 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) along plates of length \(L = 5.0 \,\mathrm{cm}\) Field \(E = 2.0 \times 10^{4} \,\mathrm{V} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\) \(t = \dfrac{L}{v} = \dfrac{0.050}{2.0 \times 10^{7}} = 2.5 \times 10^{-9} \,\mathrm{s}\) \(a = \dfrac{eE}{m} = \dfrac{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{4}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}\) \(a = 3.5 \times 10^{15} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\)
Deflection across the plates \(y = \dfrac{1}{2} a t^{2}\) \(y = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 3.5 \times 10^{15} \times (2.5 \times 10^{-9})^{2}\) \(y = \class{res}{1.1 \times 10^{-2} \,\mathrm{m}}\)
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

What sets the deflection. Putting the steps together, \(y = \frac{eEL^{2}}{2mv^{2}}\). A faster electron spends less time in the field and is deflected less, by the square of its speed. A stronger field or longer plates deflect it more.

The velocity on leaving. The sideways velocity is \(at = 3.5 \times 10^{15} \times 2.5 \times 10^{-9} = 8.8 \times 10^{6} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). The electron leaves at an angle \(\theta\) to its original direction with \(\tan \theta = \frac{8.8 \times 10^{6}}{2.0 \times 10^{7}}\), so \(\theta = 24^{\circ}\). Its speed is now \(\sqrt{(2.0 \times 10^{7})^{2} + (8.8 \times 10^{6})^{2}} = 2.2 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\).

Check that the particle does not hit a plate: here the plates would need to be more than 2.2 cm apart for an electron entering midway between them.

Charges in magnetic fields.

A force at right angles to the motion: it changes the direction, never the speed

D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

The magnetic force

The force on a moving charge.

\(F = qvB \sin \theta\)
  • \(B\): magnetic field strength, in tesla (T)
  • \(\theta\): the angle between the velocity and the field
  • Greatest at 90°. Zero when moving along the field, and zero at rest
A POSITIVE CHARGE, AT RIGHT ANGLES TO B
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

How this differs from the electric force. An electric field pushes any charge, moving or not, along the field lines. A magnetic field pushes only a moving charge, and the push is sideways: at right angles to the field and at right angles to the velocity. A charge at rest in a magnetic field feels nothing at all.

The tesla. Rearranging, \(B = \frac{F}{qv}\) for motion at right angles to the field. A field of 1 T gives a force of 1 N on a charge of 1 C moving across it at 1 m s⁻¹. One tesla is a strong field: the Earth’s field is about \(5 \times 10^{-5} \,\mathrm{T}\), a small bar magnet gives about 0.01 T close to a pole, and a hospital MRI scanner uses 1.5 T or more.

A numerical case: an electron crossing a 2.0 mT field at \(2.0 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) feels \(1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{7} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} = 6.4 \times 10^{-15} \,\mathrm{N}\).

Which way?

The direction of the magnetic force.

  • At right angles to both the field and the velocity
  • Left-hand rule: First finger Field, seCond finger Current, thuMb Motion
  • “Current” is the way a positive charge moves
For an electron, point the second finger opposite to its velocity.
THE LEFT HAND
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Using the rule. Hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of your left hand at right angles to one another. Point the first finger along the field and the second finger along the conventional current. The thumb then shows the force. Check it on the last slide: field into the page, positive charge moving right, force up the page.

Negative charges. An electron moving to the right is a conventional current to the left. Either point the second finger opposite to the electron’s velocity, or use the rule as for a positive charge and reverse the answer.

Any correct rule earns the marks. Some books use a right-hand rule with the palm: fingers along the field, thumb along the current, and the palm pushes in the direction of the force. Choose one and always use it.

Mixing this up with the right-hand grip rule from D.2. The grip rule gives the direction of the field made by a current. This rule gives the force on a current in a field.

At right angles to the field

Circular motion in a magnetic field.

Magnetic force = centripetal force \(qvB = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{r}\) \(r = \dfrac{mv}{qB}\)
  • The force does no work: the speed and kinetic energy stay constant
A POSITIVE PARTICLE, FIELD INTO THE PAGE
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Why a circle. The force is always at right angles to the velocity, so it can never speed the particle up or slow it down. It only turns it. A force of constant size, always at right angles to the motion, is exactly the condition for uniform circular motion from A.2.

Why no work is done. Work is force times the distance moved in the direction of the force. The particle never moves in the direction of the magnetic force, not even slightly, so the work done is zero and the kinetic energy cannot change.

Reading \(r = \frac{mv}{qB}\). The top line is the momentum. A particle with more momentum is harder to turn, so its circle is larger. A stronger field or a larger charge gives a tighter circle. A negative particle circles the opposite way round.

Writing that the magnetic field accelerates the particle “to a higher speed”. It does accelerate it, because the direction of the velocity changes, but the speed is unchanged.

Worked example

An electron in a 2.0 mT field.

\(v = 2.0 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\), at right angles to \(B\) \(r = \dfrac{mv}{eB}\) \(r = \dfrac{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.0 \times 10^{7}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3}}\) \(r = \class{res}{5.7 \times 10^{-2} \,\mathrm{m}}\)
Time for one circle \(T = \dfrac{2 \pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2 \pi m}{eB}\) \(T = \dfrac{2 \pi \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3}}\) \(T = \class{res}{1.8 \times 10^{-8} \,\mathrm{s}}\)
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

The surprising thing about the period. Substitute \(r = \frac{mv}{eB}\) into \(\frac{2 \pi r}{v}\) and the speed cancels: \(T = \frac{2 \pi m}{eB}\). A faster particle moves round a bigger circle, and the two effects cancel exactly, so fast and slow particles of the same kind take the same time for one orbit. The period depends only on the field and on the charge-to-mass ratio of the particle.

Why that matters. A voltage that switches direction at this fixed rate can give a particle a push twice in every orbit, however fast the particle is already going. That is the idea behind the cyclotron, on the next slide.

A proton at the same speed in the same field has a radius 1836 times larger: 105 m. Heavy particles need strong fields or large machines.

Identifying a particle from its path

The charge-to-mass ratio.

Accelerate through \(V\), then bend in \(B\) \(qV = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}\)  and  \(qvB = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{r}\) \(v = \dfrac{qBr}{m}\) \(\dfrac{q}{m} = \dfrac{2V}{B^{2} r^{2}}\)
Measure \(V\), \(B\) and \(r\): the path gives \(\dfrac{q}{m}\).
A FINE BEAM TUBE
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

The algebra. From the circular motion, \(v = \frac{qBr}{m}\). Put this into the energy equation: \(qV = \frac{1}{2} m \left(\frac{qBr}{m}\right)^{2} = \frac{q^{2} B^{2} r^{2}}{2m}\). Divide both sides by \(q\) and rearrange to get \(\frac{q}{m} = \frac{2V}{B^{2} r^{2}}\). If the speed is known directly, the shorter form \(\frac{q}{m} = \frac{v}{Br}\) does the same job.

Why only the ratio. Charge and mass always appear together as \(\frac{q}{m}\) in the motion of a particle in electric and magnetic fields. No experiment of this kind can find either one separately. J. J. Thomson measured the ratio for cathode rays in 1897; it took Millikan’s oil drops to find \(e\) on its own, and then the mass of the electron followed.

How the tube works. The bulb holds gas at low pressure. Electrons that hit gas atoms make them glow, so the path shows as a thin bright ring whose diameter can be measured with a ruler. The uniform field comes from a pair of large coils, one on each side of the bulb.

A mass spectrometer uses the same physics to sort ions: for the same charge and speed, heavier ions follow wider circles.

Worked example

Measuring \(\frac{e}{m}\) for the electron.

\(V = 250 \,\mathrm{V}\) · \(B = 1.0 \,\mathrm{mT}\) Diameter of the ring 10.6 cm, so \(r = 5.3 \,\mathrm{cm}\) \(\dfrac{e}{m} = \dfrac{2V}{B^{2} r^{2}}\) \(\dfrac{e}{m} = \dfrac{2 \times 250}{(1.0 \times 10^{-3})^{2} \times (5.3 \times 10^{-2})^{2}}\) \(\dfrac{e}{m} = \class{res}{1.8 \times 10^{11} \,\mathrm{C} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}}\)
  • Accepted value: \(1.76 \times 10^{11} \,\mathrm{C} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\)
  • Measure the diameter and halve it
  • \(r\) is squared: its uncertainty counts twice
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

How large this number is. One kilogram of electrons would carry a charge of \(1.76 \times 10^{11} \,\mathrm{C}\). For a hydrogen ion, a single proton, the ratio is \(9.6 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{C} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\), 1836 times smaller. When Thomson found such an enormous ratio for cathode rays, he concluded that the particles must be far lighter than any atom.

Improving the experiment. A single reading gives one value. A better method is to change the voltage, measure the radius each time and plot \(r^{2}\) against \(V\). The graph is a straight line through the origin with gradient \(\frac{2m}{eB^{2}}\), and the gradient uses all the readings at once.

Leaving the field in millitesla or the radius in centimetres. Both are squared, so the error is a factor of a million or ten thousand.

Neither along nor across

Velocity at an angle to the field.

  • Component along \(B\): no force, carries on at constant velocity
  • Component across \(B\): circular motion
  • Together: a helix round the field lines
Only \(v \sin \theta\), across the field, appears in the force.
A HELICAL PATH
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Where the \(\sin \theta\) comes from. Resolve the velocity into a part along the field, \(v \cos \theta\), and a part at right angles to it, \(v \sin \theta\). Only the second part gives a force, so \(F = q (v \sin \theta) B\). The radius of the helix is \(\frac{m v \sin \theta}{qB}\).

Why charged particles follow field lines. The particle cannot easily cross the field, because any motion across is bent back into a circle. It can slide freely along it. Charged particles are therefore guided along magnetic field lines, like beads on a wire. This is used to hold the hot plasma in a fusion reactor away from the walls.

Both fields at once

Crossed electric and magnetic fields.

\(v = \dfrac{E}{B}\)
The charge cancels: one speed passes, whatever the particle.
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Why only one speed gets through. The electric force \(qE\) is the same for every speed. The magnetic force \(qvB\) grows with speed. For a particle that is too fast, the magnetic force wins and it curves one way. For one that is too slow, the electric force wins and it curves the other way. Only at \(v = \frac{E}{B}\) do they balance, and the particle obeys Newton’s first law.

A worked case. With \(E = 2.0 \times 10^{4} \,\mathrm{V} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\) and \(B = 1.0 \,\mathrm{mT}\): \(v = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{4}}{1.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 2.0 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). Changing either field selects a different speed.

Negative particles. For an electron both forces reverse, so they still oppose each other, and the same speed passes straight through.

Thomson used crossed fields to find the speed of his cathode rays, then switched one field off and measured the deflection to get \(\frac{e}{m}\).

D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

A wire in a magnetic field

The force on a current-carrying conductor.

\(F = BIL \sin \theta\)
  • \(L\): the length of wire in the field
  • \(\theta\): the angle between the current and the field
  • Direction: the same left-hand rule
A WIRE BETWEEN THE POLES OF A MAGNET
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Where it comes from. The force on the wire is the total of the forces on all the moving charges inside it. In a time \(t\) a charge \(q = It\) passes along a length \(L = vt\) of wire. The force on that charge is \(qvB = (It) \left(\frac{L}{t}\right) B = BIL\). The two equations describe the same force, once for a single charge and once for a current.

Checking the direction in the diagram. First finger along the field, from N to S: to the right. Second finger along the current: out of the page, towards you. The thumb points up the page.

What it is used for. This force turns every electric motor and moves the cone of every loudspeaker. A coil carrying a current in a magnetic field feels forces on its two sides in opposite directions, and so it turns.

A wire lying along the field feels no force: \(\sin 0^{\circ} = 0\). The force is greatest when the wire is at right angles to the field.

Worked example

A wire in a 0.050 T field.

\(I = 3.0 \,\mathrm{A}\) · \(L = 0.20 \,\mathrm{m}\) of wire in the field \(F = BIL \sin \theta\) At right angles to the field: \(\theta = 90^{\circ}\) \(F = 0.050 \times 3.0 \times 0.20 \times \sin 90^{\circ} = \class{res}{0.030 \,\mathrm{N}}\)
The wire is turned to \(\theta = 30^{\circ}\) \(F = 0.050 \times 3.0 \times 0.20 \times \sin 30^{\circ}\) \(F = \class{res}{0.015 \,\mathrm{N}}\) Still at right angles to the wire and to the field
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Measuring a field with a balance. Place a magnet on a top-pan balance and clamp a wire between its poles. When a current flows, the magnet pushes the wire up, so by Newton’s third law the wire pushes the magnet down and the reading rises. A change in reading of 3.1 g means a force of \(3.1 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.8 = 0.030 \,\mathrm{N}\). Knowing the current and the length of wire in the field, \(B = \frac{F}{IL}\).

A better version. Change the current, record the force each time and plot force against current. The line passes through the origin and its gradient is \(BL\).

Using the whole length of the wire. Only the part inside the field counts.

Currents act on each other

The force between two parallel wires.

LONG STRAIGHT WIRESPRESS → TO SWITCH ON
\(\dfrac{F}{L} = \mu_{0} \dfrac{I_{1} I_{2}}{2 \pi r}\)
  • \(\dfrac{F}{L}\): the force on each metre of wire
  • \(r\): the separation of the wires
  • \(\mu_{0} = 4 \pi \times 10^{-7} \,\mathrm{T} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{A}^{-1}\)
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

The opposite of charges. Like charges repel, but like currents attract: two wires carrying current in the same direction pull together. Currents in opposite directions push apart.

Equal forces. The force on each metre of wire 1 is the same size as the force on each metre of wire 2, even if the currents are different. They are a Newton’s third law pair, and the equation is unchanged if \(I_{1}\) and \(I_{2}\) are swapped.

The constant. \(\mu_{0}\) is the permeability of free space. It does for magnetic fields what \(\varepsilon_{0}\) does for electric fields: it sets how strong a field a given current produces in a vacuum.

The two wires of a mains cable carry equal currents in opposite directions, so they repel. At ordinary currents the force is far too small to notice.

One wire in the field of the other

Why parallel currents attract.

  • Wire 1 makes a field at wire 2: \(B_{1} = \dfrac{\mu_{0} I_{1}}{2 \pi r}\)
  • Wire 2 carries a current in that field: \(F = B_{1} I_{2} L\)
  • So \(\dfrac{F}{L} = \mu_{0} \dfrac{I_{1} I_{2}}{2 \pi r}\)
Grip rule for the field, left-hand rule for the force.
SEEN END ONBOTH CURRENTS OUT OF THE PAGE
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Following the two rules. Wire 1 carries current out of the page, so by the right-hand grip rule its field circles anticlockwise. At wire 2, to the right of wire 1, that field points up the page. Now the left-hand rule at wire 2: first finger up the page, second finger out of the page, and the thumb points to the left, towards wire 1.

Reversing one current. If the current in wire 2 goes into the page, the second finger reverses and so does the thumb: wire 2 is pushed away. You only need to know the field of a straight wire as a pattern; the expression for \(B_{1}\) is not in the data booklet, but the final equation is.

Apply the same steps the other way round, with wire 1 in the field of wire 2, and the force on wire 1 comes out equal and opposite.

Worked example

Two wires 2.0 cm apart.

\(I_{1} = 5.0 \,\mathrm{A}\) · \(I_{2} = 8.0 \,\mathrm{A}\), in the same direction \(\dfrac{F}{L} = \mu_{0} \dfrac{I_{1} I_{2}}{2 \pi r}\) \(\dfrac{F}{L} = \dfrac{4 \pi \times 10^{-7} \times 5.0 \times 8.0}{2 \pi \times 0.020}\) \(\dfrac{F}{L} = \class{res}{4.0 \times 10^{-4} \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}}\), attractive
  • The \(\pi\) cancels: \(\dfrac{4 \pi}{2 \pi} = 2\)
  • On 1.5 m of either wire: \(4.0 \times 10^{-4} \times 1.5 = 6.0 \times 10^{-4} \,\mathrm{N}\)
  • Halve the separation: double the force
D.3 MOTION IN ELECTROMAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Not an inverse square law. The force per metre is proportional to \(\frac{1}{r}\), not \(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\), because the field of a long straight wire falls off as \(\frac{1}{r}\). Doubling the separation halves the force.

When the force becomes large. The answer here is tiny, about the weight of a few grains of rice on every metre. But the force goes as the product of the currents. In a short circuit at a power station, where currents of tens of thousands of amperes flow in conductors a few centimetres apart, the forces can bend thick copper bars, and the supports have to be built to resist them.

Leaving the separation in centimetres. It must be in metres to match \(\mu_{0}\).

The story · Paris, 1820

Background story · not assessed

Ampère and the ampere.

  • In September 1820 Ørsted’s discovery was shown in Paris
  • Ampère soon found that two parallel wires attract or repel, with no magnet involved
  • Until 2019 this force defined the ampere: \(2 \times 10^{-7} \,\mathrm{N}\) on each metre of two wires 1 m apart
