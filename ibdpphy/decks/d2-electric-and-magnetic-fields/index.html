Electric charge.

Two types of charge, how bodies become charged, and the force between them

Positive and negative

Two types of charge.

  • Like charges repel. Unlike charges attract
  • Charge is measured in coulombs, C
  • Protons are positive, electrons negative, with the same size of charge:
    \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{C}\)
  • A neutral body has equal amounts of both
FORCES BETWEEN CHARGES
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Why only two types. Every electrical experiment ever done can be explained with two kinds of charge that cancel when they are combined. Which one is called positive is a historical accident: Benjamin Franklin chose the names long before the electron was found, and the electron ended up with the negative sign.

Neutral does not mean empty. A copper coin contains about \(10^{24}\) electrons and exactly as many protons, so its total charge is zero. Charging an object means moving a tiny fraction of its electrons on or off. One coulomb is a huge charge by everyday standards: a rubbed balloon carries less than a millionth of a coulomb.

In solids only electrons move. A body becomes positive by losing electrons, never by gaining protons.

A conservation law

Charge is conserved.

  • The total charge of an isolated system never changes
  • Charge can be moved from one body to another, but not created or destroyed

Rod and cloth start neutral and end with equal and opposite charges

Touching

\((+6) + (-2) = +4 \,\mathrm{nC}\)

Two identical spheres touch and share the total equally: +2 nC each

The spheres, step by step. The total charge is \(+6 + (-2) = +4 \,\mathrm{nC}\). When identical conducting spheres touch, they end with equal charges, so each takes \(+2 \,\mathrm{nC}\). The total is still \(+4 \,\mathrm{nC}\). If the spheres were different sizes the charge would not split equally, but the total would still be +4 nC.

It holds even when particles are created. In beta-minus decay a neutron (charge 0) becomes a proton (+e), an electron (−e) and an antineutrino (0). New particles appear, but the total charge is zero before and after. No process has ever been found that changes the total charge.

Add charges with their signs. A common slip is to add the sizes: +6 nC and −2 nC do not make 8 nC.

Moving electrons

Charging by friction and by contact.

  • Friction: rubbing moves electrons from one insulator to the other
  • The two end with equal and opposite charges
  • Contact: touch a charged body to a conductor and charge is shared
  • An insulator holds its charge in place. In a conductor it spreads
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Which way the electrons go. It depends on the pair of materials. Polythene holds electrons more strongly than a woollen cloth, so it takes electrons and becomes negative. Acetate or glass rubbed with the same cloth gives up electrons and becomes positive. Rubbing only brings the surfaces into close contact over a large area; it is not the rubbing itself that creates the charge.

Why you cannot charge a metal rod held in your hand. Metals and your body are conductors. Any charge that the rod gains flows through you to the ground at once. Hold the metal with an insulating handle and it charges just as well as plastic.

Writing that “positive charges move onto the rod”. The positive charges are protons, fixed in the nuclei. Only electrons transfer.

Charging without touching

Charging by induction.

A METAL SPHERE ON AN INSULATING STANDPRESS → FOR EACH STAGE
The sphere ends with the opposite charge to the rod, and the rod loses none of its own. Earthing (grounding) gives electrons a path to or from the ground.
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

What happens at each stage. The negative rod repels the free electrons in the metal to the far side: the sphere is still neutral overall but its charge is separated. Connecting it to earth lets those repelled electrons escape further, into the ground. Breaking the earth connection first traps the shortage of electrons on the sphere. Only then is the rod taken away, and the positive charge spreads evenly over the surface.

Why the order matters. If the rod is removed before the earth connection is broken, the electrons simply flow back up from the ground and the sphere ends neutral.

What earth is. The Earth is so large that it can supply or accept electrons without its own potential changing. Touching the sphere with a finger works as an earth connection.

With a positive rod everything reverses: electrons flow up from the earth onto the sphere, and it ends negative.

The force between two point charges

Coulomb’s law.

\(F = k \dfrac{q_{1} q_{2}}{r^{2}}\)
  • Along the line joining the charges
  • \(k = 8.99 \times 10^{9} \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{m}^{2} \,\mathrm{C}^{-2}\) in a vacuum
  • An inverse square law, for point charges
TWO POINT CHARGESEQUAL AND OPPOSITE FORCES
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Reading the sign. Put the charges in with their signs. A positive answer (two like charges) means repulsion; a negative answer (unlike charges) means attraction. Many people prefer to calculate the size of the force from the sizes of the charges and then decide the direction by thinking: both methods are accepted.

How strong it is. Compare it with gravity for the electron and proton in a hydrogen atom. The electric attraction is \(8.2 \times 10^{-8} \,\mathrm{N}\). The gravitational attraction between the same two particles is \(3.6 \times 10^{-47} \,\mathrm{N}\), smaller by a factor of about \(10^{39}\). Gravity wins on the scale of planets only because matter is almost exactly neutral, so the electric forces cancel.

Charges are usually given in μC or nC, and distances in cm. Convert to C and m before you start.

A charged sphere acts as a point charge at its centre, for anything outside it, as a uniform sphere does for gravity.

Worked example

The force between two small charged spheres.

\(q_{1} = +3.0 \,\mathrm{\mu C}\) · \(q_{2} = -5.0 \,\mathrm{\mu C}\) Centres \(r = 0.20 \,\mathrm{m}\) apart, in air \(F = k \dfrac{q_{1} q_{2}}{r^{2}}\) \(F = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 5.0 \times 10^{-6}}{0.20^{2}}\) \(F = \class{res}{3.4 \,\mathrm{N}}\), attractive
  • Sizes of the charges in the equation; direction from the signs
  • \(\mathrm{\mu C} = 10^{-6} \,\mathrm{C}\) and \(\mathrm{nC} = 10^{-9} \,\mathrm{C}\)
  • Each sphere feels 3.4 N, towards the other
Air is so close to a vacuum that the same \(k\) is used.

Ratios again. If the spheres are moved to 0.40 m apart, the distance has doubled and the force falls to a quarter: 0.84 N. If instead one charge is doubled, the force doubles. Many multiple-choice questions on Coulomb’s law need only this kind of reasoning and no calculation.

A sense of scale. A few microcoulombs on objects a hand’s width apart give a force of a few newtons, easy to feel. This is why charged clothes cling and a rubbed balloon sticks to a wall.

Forgetting to square the distance, or squaring it in centimetres. \(r\) must be in metres before it is squared.

Charges inside a material

The Coulomb constant and permittivity.

\(k = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}}\)
  • \(\varepsilon_{0} = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \,\mathrm{C}^{2} \,\mathrm{N}^{-1} \,\mathrm{m}^{-2}\): the permittivity of free space
  • In a material, replace \(\varepsilon_{0}\) by its permittivity \(\varepsilon\)
  • Larger \(\varepsilon\): smaller force
The same two spheres, now in water \(\varepsilon = 80 \varepsilon_{0}\) \(F = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon} \dfrac{q_{1} q_{2}}{r^{2}}\) \(F = \dfrac{F_\mathrm{air}}{80} = \dfrac{3.4}{80} = \class{res}{0.042 \,\mathrm{N}}\)
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Why a material weakens the force. The molecules of an insulating material are distorted or turned by the field of the charges, so that their own positive and negative ends line up against it. Their fields partly cancel the original one. Water molecules do this particularly well, which is why water has such a high permittivity.

Why this matters in chemistry. In a crystal of salt the sodium and chloride ions are held together by electric attraction. In water that attraction is 80 times weaker, and thermal motion is enough to pull the ions apart. That is why salt dissolves in water but not in oil.

Check that \(\frac{1}{4 \pi \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12}}\) gives \(8.99 \times 10^{9}\) on your calculator. Both constants are in the data booklet.

Evidence that charge is quantized

Millikan’s oil drop experiment.

ONE CHARGED OIL DROPPRESS → TO SWITCH ON THE FIELD
  • Adjust the voltage until the drop hangs at rest: \(qE = mg\)
  • Measure \(q\) for many drops
  • Every charge is a whole-number multiple of one value, \(e\)
Charge is quantized: \(q = ne\).
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

What “quantized” means. Charge comes in lumps. A drop can carry 1, 2, 3 or more extra electrons, but never 2.5. Millikan’s drops gave charges such as \(3.2\), \(4.8\), \(8.0\) and \(1.6 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{C}\). No drop ever had a charge between these steps, and the size of one step is the charge of a single electron.

How the mass was found. A drop is far too small to weigh. With the field switched off it falls at a steady terminal velocity, where its weight is balanced by viscous drag and buoyancy. Measuring that velocity through a microscope gives the radius of the drop, and so its mass. The drops picked up their charge from friction in the spray nozzle, or from air ionized by X-rays.

To find \(e\) from data: look for the largest value that divides exactly into every measured charge.

E.2 tells the story of Millikan’s other famous experiment, on the photoelectric effect.

Worked example

How many extra electrons on the drop?.

Mass \(4.9 \times 10^{-15} \,\mathrm{kg}\), at rest between plates \(V = 500 \,\mathrm{V}\) · plates \(d = 5.0 \,\mathrm{mm}\) apart \(qE = mg\)  with  \(E = \dfrac{V}{d}\) \(q = \dfrac{mgd}{V} = \dfrac{4.9 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.8 \times 5.0 \times 10^{-3}}{500}\) \(q = \class{res}{4.8 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{C}}\)
Number of extra electrons \(n = \dfrac{q}{e}\) \(n = \dfrac{4.8 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = \class{res}{3}\)
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Which plate is positive? The electric force must point up to balance the weight. The drop is negative, so it is attracted to the positive plate: the top plate must be positive. For a positive drop the plates would have to be connected the other way round.

What happens if the voltage changes. Raise the voltage and the electric force exceeds the weight, so the drop accelerates upwards. Lower it and the drop falls. If the drop suddenly gains or loses an electron while you watch, it starts to move, and a new voltage is needed to hold it: direct evidence of charge changing in steps.

Leaving \(d\) in millimetres. The field strength \(\frac{V}{d}\) needs the separation in metres.

D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

The same idea as in D.1. Gravitational field strength is force per unit mass; electric field strength is force per unit charge. Both describe the place, not the object you put there. The one new feature is the sign: mass is always positive, but charge is not, so the force on a charge can be along the field or against it.

A worked case. At a point 0.30 m from a charge of +4.0 nC, \(E = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 4.0 \times 10^{-9}}{0.30^{2}} = 400 \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{C}^{-1}\), pointing away from the charge. An electron placed there feels \(1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 400 = 6.4 \times 10^{-17} \,\mathrm{N}\), towards the charge.

Using the test charge in \(E = k \frac{Q}{r^{2}}\). \(Q\) is the charge that makes the field. The test charge cancelled.

Drawing a field

  • Show the direction of the force on a positive charge
  • Start on positive charge, end on negative charge
  • Never cross

RADIAL FIELDS OF POINT CHARGES
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Why density means strength. Draw 12 lines from a point charge. At any distance, all 12 lines pass through a sphere centred on the charge. The area of that sphere grows as \(r^{2}\), so the number of lines through each square metre falls as \(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\), exactly as the field strength does. The picture of lines spreading out is the inverse square law in visual form.

What field lines are not. They are not the path a charge would follow. A charge released from rest starts off along the line, but once it is moving its momentum carries it across curved lines. A field line shows the direction of the force at each point, and nothing more.

Sketching: equal angles between lines from a point charge, arrows on every line, and the number of lines in proportion to the size of the charge.

Fields add as vectors

The field of two point charges.

UNLIKE CHARGESLINES JOIN THEM
LIKE CHARGESZERO FIELD MIDWAY
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

How the patterns arise. At every point, the field of each charge is worked out as if the other were not there, and the two are added as vectors. Close to either charge its own field is much the stronger, so the lines there look radial. Further away the two fields are of similar size and the lines bend.

The midpoint. Between two equal like charges the fields are equal and opposite at the centre, so the field there is zero: no line passes through that point. Between unlike charges the two fields at the centre point the same way, from the positive charge to the negative one, and they add. For +4.0 nC and −4.0 nC placed 0.60 m apart, each gives 400 N C⁻¹ at the midpoint, so the field there is 800 N C⁻¹.

Lines must leave and meet each charge evenly spaced and at right angles to its surface. Far from two unlike charges of equal size, the field becomes very weak.

Inside and outside

The field of a charged conducting sphere.

  • The charge spreads over the surface
  • Inside: no field at all
  • Outside: the field of a point charge at the centre
  • Lines meet the surface at right angles
A POSITIVE SPHERE
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Why the charge goes to the surface. The extra charges repel one another and are free to move through the metal, so they get as far apart as they can: onto the outside surface, evenly spread on a sphere.

Why there is no field inside. If there were a field inside the metal, the free electrons would feel a force and move. They would keep moving until their new arrangement cancelled the field. Within a tiny fraction of a second the charges settle, and when nothing is moving the field inside must be zero. This is true for a solid sphere and for a hollow one.

Why the lines are at right angles. Any component of the field along the surface would push charge along the surface. At rest, there can be none.

A hollow metal box shields its inside from outside electric fields in the same way. A car body protects its passengers in a lightning strike.

Parallel plates

The uniform field between parallel plates.

\(E = \dfrac{V}{d}\)
  • \(V\): potential difference between the plates. \(d\): their separation
  • Same strength and direction everywhere between the plates
  • \(\mathrm{V} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\) is the same unit as \(\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{C}^{-1}\)
OPPOSITELY CHARGED PLATESEDGE EFFECTS
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

Where the equation comes from. Move a charge \(q\) from one plate to the other. The force on it is \(qE\) all the way, so the work done is \(qEd\). By the definition of potential difference the work is also \(qV\). Setting them equal gives \(E = \frac{V}{d}\).

Why the field is uniform. Each plate carries an even sheet of charge. Move towards one plate and its pull grows a little, but you are also moving away from the other, and the two changes cancel. The force on a charge is the same at every point between the plates, so a charge there has a constant acceleration, like a mass in the uniform gravitational field near the ground.

The edges. Near the edges the lines bulge outwards and are further apart: the field is weaker and no longer uniform. Calculations assume plates that are large compared with their separation, so that the edges can be ignored.

Draw the lines between the plates with a ruler, evenly spaced, starting and ending exactly on the plates, with one curved line at each edge.

Why the weight is ignored. The weight of an electron is \(9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 9.8 = 8.9 \times 10^{-30} \,\mathrm{N}\). The electric force here is larger by a factor of \(10^{14}\). For electrons, protons and ions in electric fields, gravity can always be left out. For an oil drop, which has billions of times more mass, it cannot.

The force is the same everywhere. Because the field is uniform, the answer does not depend on where the electron is between the plates. Its acceleration is constant, so the equations of motion from A.1 apply. D.3 uses this to follow a charged particle through the plates.

D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

What is the same. Both forces act at a distance, both obey an inverse square law for point bodies, and both are described by a field strength that is a force per unit of the property the force acts on. A uniform sphere behaves as a point at its centre in both cases. Learn one and you have most of the other.

What is different. There is only one kind of mass, so gravity always attracts and can never be shielded. There are two kinds of charge, so electric forces can attract or repel, and equal amounts of both kinds cancel. The constants differ enormously: \(G\) is about \(10^{-10}\) and \(k\) about \(10^{10}\) in SI units.

Using the same words for both (field strength, field line, and at HL potential and equipotential) is deliberate: the mathematics is identical.

THE FIELD OF A BAR MAGNET
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · SL + HL

How this differs from an electric field. Electric field lines begin on positive charges and end on negative ones. Nobody has ever found a separate north or south pole: cut a magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with both poles. So magnetic field lines have nowhere to begin or end. They run round in closed loops, out of the north pole, round to the south pole and back through the magnet.

The Earth. A compass needle is a small magnet, and its north pole points towards geographic north. Since unlike poles attract, the magnetic pole near the Earth’s geographic north is a magnetic south pole.

Magnetic field strength is also called magnetic flux density. Its symbol is \(B\) and its unit the tesla, T. D.3 defines it from the force on a current.

The coil. Each short piece of the loop makes circles of field round itself. Inside the loop all these circles point the same way, through the coil, so they add up to a strong field there. Outside the loop they partly cancel.

The solenoid. A solenoid is many loops side by side. Their fields add inside to give a field that is strong, parallel to the axis and nearly uniform, which is why solenoids are used wherever a uniform magnetic field is needed. Outside, the pattern is the same as that of a bar magnet. The field is stronger for a larger current and for more turns on each metre of length.

Which end is north? Grip the solenoid in your right hand with the fingers curling the way the current flows round the turns. Your thumb points to the north pole, the end the field lines leave from.

Looking at one end of a solenoid: current anticlockwise means that end is a north pole; clockwise means south.

Only these four patterns are required: bar magnet, straight wire, circular coil and air-core solenoid.

They are bound: work must be done to pull them apart

D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · ADDITIONAL HL

Compare with gravity. Gravitational potential energy, \(-G \frac{m_{1} m_{2}}{r}\), is always negative because gravity always attracts. Two unlike charges also attract, and putting one positive and one negative charge into \(k \frac{q_{1} q_{2}}{r}\) gives a negative answer in the same way. Two like charges repel, which has no parallel in gravity: their potential energy is positive.

A worked case: the hydrogen atom. For a proton and an electron \(5.3 \times 10^{-11} \,\mathrm{m}\) apart: \(E_\mathrm{p} = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19}) \times (-1.60 \times 10^{-19})}{5.3 \times 10^{-11}} = -4.3 \times 10^{-18} \,\mathrm{J}\), which is −27 eV. The negative sign says the atom is bound.

Squaring \(r\), as in Coulomb’s law. Force has \(r^{2}\) underneath; energy has \(r\).

Energy per unit charge

Electric potential.

\(V_\mathrm{e} = \dfrac{kQ}{r}\)
  • The work done per unit charge in bringing a small positive test charge from infinity to the point
  • A scalar, in volts. Zero at infinity
  • Potentials of several charges add as numbers
POTENTIAL ROUND A POINT CHARGE
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · ADDITIONAL HL

Reading the two curves. To bring a positive test charge towards a positive charge you must push against a repulsion, so you do positive work: the potential is positive and rises as you get closer. Towards a negative charge the test charge is pulled in and the field does the work: the potential is negative.

The volt. One volt is one joule per coulomb. The potential difference across a cell in a circuit is the same quantity as here: the difference in the electric potential between two points. A point at 180 V is one where each coulomb of positive charge would have 180 J of potential energy.

Dropping the sign of \(Q\). Potential is a scalar but it does have a sign, and it matters when you add the potentials of several charges.

Worked example

The potential near two charges.

P is 0.10 m from \(Q_{1} = +2.0 \,\mathrm{nC}\) and 0.20 m from \(Q_{2} = -3.0 \,\mathrm{nC}\) \(V_\mathrm{e} = \dfrac{k Q_{1}}{r_{1}} + \dfrac{k Q_{2}}{r_{2}}\) \(V_{1} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9}}{0.10} = +180 \,\mathrm{V}\) \(V_{2} = \dfrac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times (-3.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.20} = -135 \,\mathrm{V}\) \(V_\mathrm{e} = 180 - 135 = \class{res}{+45 \,\mathrm{V}}\)
  • No directions, no components: just add
  • It does not matter where P is, only how far it is from each charge
Field strengths at P would have to be added as vectors.

Zero potential is not zero field. Move P until it is 0.15 m from the negative charge and the two potentials are +180 V and −180 V: the total is zero. But the field there is certainly not zero, because a test charge at that point would still be pushed by one charge and pulled by the other. A potential of zero only means that no net work is needed to bring a charge there from infinity.

And zero field is not zero potential. Midway between two equal positive charges the fields cancel, but the potentials, both positive, add.

Up to four point charges can appear in a question. The method is the same: one term for each charge, each with its own sign and distance.

Joules and electronvolts

Work done in moving a charge.

\(W = q \Delta V_\mathrm{e}\)
  • Depends only on the potentials at the start and end, not on the path
  • Electronvolt: the work done when a charge \(e\) moves through 1 V
  • \(1 \,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{J}\)
An electron, from rest, moves through +500 V Energy gained \(= e \Delta V_\mathrm{e} = \class{res}{500 \,\mathrm{eV}}\) \(= 500 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 8.0 \times 10^{-17} \,\mathrm{J}\) \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 E_\mathrm{k}}{m}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-17}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}}\) \(v = \class{res}{1.3 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · ADDITIONAL HL

Who does the work? \(W = q \Delta V_\mathrm{e}\) is the work an outside agent must do to move the charge without changing its speed. For the electron, \(q\) is negative and \(\Delta V_\mathrm{e}\) is positive, so \(W\) is negative: no one has to push. The field does the work, the potential energy falls by 500 eV and the kinetic energy rises by 500 eV.

Why the electronvolt is convenient. An electron accelerated through 500 V gains 500 eV. A particle with charge \(2e\), such as an alpha particle, gains 1000 eV through the same voltage. The number of electronvolts is the number of volts times the charge in units of \(e\), with no powers of ten to carry.

Putting an energy in eV straight into \(\frac{1}{2} m v^{2}\). Convert to joules first.

Linking the two descriptions

Field strength is the potential gradient.

\(E = -\dfrac{\Delta V_\mathrm{e}}{\Delta r}\)
  • The field points from high potential to low potential
  • Potential changing quickly with distance: a strong field
  • Between plates: \(E = \dfrac{400}{0.040} = 1.0 \times 10^{4} \,\mathrm{V} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\)
PLATES 4.0 cm APARTEQUIPOTENTIALS EVERY 1.0 cm
D.2 ELECTRIC AND MAGNETIC FIELDS · ADDITIONAL HL

The parallel plates are the simplest case. The potential falls steadily from 400 V at the positive plate to 0 V at the negative plate, by 100 V for every centimetre. The gradient is the same everywhere, so the field is uniform, and \(E = \frac{V}{d}\) from the SL part is this equation for a constant gradient.

Why the minus sign. The potential decreases in the direction of the field, so \(\Delta V_\mathrm{e}\) is negative when \(\Delta r\) is taken along the field. The minus sign makes \(E\) come out positive in that direction. A positive charge is pushed “downhill” in potential, as a ball rolls downhill in height.

On a graph of potential against distance, the field strength is minus the gradient. On a graph of field strength against distance, the area under the curve is the change in potential.

Mapping a field with potential

Equipotential surfaces.

  • Join points at the same potential
  • No work is done moving a charge along one
  • Always at right angles to the field lines
  • Round a point charge: spheres. Between plates: planes
A POSITIVE POINT CHARGESTEPS OF 100 V

Why the circles spread out. The potential is \(\frac{kQ}{r}\), so halving the potential means doubling the distance. The surfaces at 300 V and 200 V are close together; those at 200 V and 100 V are three times as far apart. Closely spaced equipotentials mean a large potential gradient, and so a strong field: near the charge.

Why no work is done. \(W = q \Delta V_\mathrm{e}\), and along an equipotential \(\Delta V_\mathrm{e} = 0\). In terms of force: the field, and so the force on the charge, is at right angles to the surface, and a force at right angles to the motion does no work.

To turn a map of equipotentials into field lines, draw curves that cross every equipotential at right angles, with arrows pointing from higher potential to lower.

How to read the two-charge map. Close to each charge the equipotentials are nearly circles, positive round the positive charge and negative round the negative one. Exactly halfway between, every point is the same distance from both charges, so the two potentials cancel: the mid-plane is the 0 V equipotential. Every field line crosses every dashed curve at a right angle.

The four charges. The same reasoning gives two planes of zero potential, between each positive charge and its negative neighbours. You are expected to recognize patterns like these and to sketch field lines on them, not to calculate them.

Two equipotentials can never cross, for the same reason that two field lines cannot: a point cannot have two different potentials.

Solid or hollow

Field and potential of a charged sphere.

  • Outside: as a point charge at the centre
  • Inside: the field is zero, so the potential does not change
  • The whole sphere is at the surface potential \(\dfrac{kQ}{R}\)
\(Q = 2.0 \,\mathrm{nC}\) · \(R = 0.10 \,\mathrm{m}\) \(V_\mathrm{e} = \dfrac{kQ}{R} = \class{res}{180 \,\mathrm{V}}\) throughout

Why the potential is constant inside. \(E = -\frac{\Delta V_\mathrm{e}}{\Delta r}\). Inside the conductor \(E = 0\), so the potential gradient is zero and the potential cannot change from one point to another. No work is needed to move a charge around inside. The whole conductor, surface and interior, is one equipotential.

Solid and hollow spheres behave alike. In both, the charge is on the outer surface and the field inside is zero, so the graphs are the same. Outside, the equipotentials are spheres centred on the conductor, as for a point charge.

The field at the surface. \(E = \frac{kQ}{R^{2}} = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9}}{0.10^{2}} = 1800 \,\mathrm{V} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\). This is the gradient of the lower graph just outside the surface.
