What is a force?

Forces are interactions.

  • A force is a push or a pull that one body exerts on another. A vector, measured in newtons
  • Every force needs two bodies: one that exerts it and one that it acts on

Contact forces

The bodies touch

Normal force, friction, tension, the force of a spring, viscous drag, buoyancy

Field forces

They act at a distance

Gravitational force (weight), electric force, magnetic force

A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

A test for any force. Finish the sentence “the force of … on …”. The weight of a book is the force of the Earth on the book. The tension is the force of the string on the load. If you cannot name the body that exerts a force, the force is not real: there is no “force of motion” that keeps a moving body going.

Why the two families are not so different. Contact forces only look like touching. When a book rests on a table, the electrons in the two surfaces repel each other without ever meeting. Every contact force in this topic is the electric force acting between a very large number of atoms.

One newton is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻²: about the weight of a small apple.

Representing forces

Free-body diagrams.

  • One body only, drawn on its own
  • Every force that acts on it, as an arrow from where it acts
  • Length shows the size; label each with its name or symbol
  • Leave out the forces the body exerts on other things
A BLOCK PULLED ALONG A TABLE
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

A way to find every force. Start with the weight: anything with mass near the Earth has one. Then go round the outline of the body and stop at each thing that touches it. Each contact can supply a force: a surface gives a normal force and perhaps friction, a string gives a tension, a fluid gives buoyancy and drag. When you have been all the way round, you have them all.

Where each arrow starts. Weight acts at the centre of mass, so its arrow starts at the centre of the body. A contact force starts where the contact is: the normal force and friction at the surface the body rests on, a tension where the string is attached, a push or pull where it is applied.

Reading this diagram. The two vertical arrows are the same length, so there is no resultant force up or down and the block stays on the table. The applied force is longer than the friction, so there is a resultant force forwards and the block speeds up.

Adding an arrow for “the force of the motion” in the direction the body is moving. Velocity is not a force and never appears on a free-body diagram.

Adding forces

The resultant force.

  • The one force that has the same effect as all the forces together
  • Along a line: add with signs
  • In two dimensions: add as vectors
\(F = \sqrt{F_{x}^{2} + F_{y}^{2}} = \sqrt{4.0^{2} + 3.0^{2}} = \class{res}{5.0 \,\mathrm{N}}\) \(\tan \theta = \dfrac{F_{y}}{F_{x}} = \dfrac{3.0}{4.0}\)  so  \(\theta = \class{res}{37^{\circ}}\)
TWO FORCES AT RIGHT ANGLES
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

When the forces are not at right angles. Resolve each one into two components along directions you choose, usually horizontal and vertical. Add all the horizontal components with their signs, then all the vertical ones. That leaves two perpendicular totals, which combine by Pythagoras exactly as on the slide.

Choosing the directions. Pick the pair that leaves the fewest forces to resolve. On a slope, take directions along the slope and at right angles to it: then only the weight needs resolving, and the acceleration lies along one of your directions.

A resultant needs a size and a direction. “5.0 N” alone is half an answer: say “5.0 N at 37° to the 4.0 N force”.

No resultant force

Newton’s first law.

  • With no resultant force, a body stays at rest or keeps moving at constant velocity
  • Such a body is in translational equilibrium
Vertical forces balance \(2T \sin \theta = mg\) \(T = \dfrac{mg}{2 \sin \theta} = \dfrac{49}{2 \sin 40^{\circ}} = \class{res}{38 \,\mathrm{N}}\)
A 5.0 kg SIGN HANGING AT REST
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Equilibrium does not mean at rest. A parachutist falling at a steady speed and a car cruising along a straight motorway are both in equilibrium: the forces on each add to zero. A force is needed to change a velocity, not to keep one.

Why each string pulls with more than half the weight. Only the vertical part of each tension, \(T \sin 40^{\circ}\), holds the sign up. The horizontal parts pull against each other and cancel. The flatter the strings, the smaller the vertical part of each tension, so the larger the tension has to be. A washing line pulled almost straight can snap under a small load.

For any equilibrium problem: resolve, then write “forces up = forces down” and “forces left = forces right”.

A resultant force

Newton’s second law.

\(F = ma\)
  • \(F\) is the resultant force
  • The acceleration is in the direction of the resultant force
  • In this form the mass must be constant
A 1200 kg car · driving force 3000 N Resistive forces 600 N Resultant \(F\) = driving force − resistive forces \(F = 3000 - 600 = 2400 \,\mathrm{N}\) \(a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{2400}{1200} = \class{res}{2.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\)
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

What mass means here. Mass measures how hard a body is to accelerate. The same 2400 N on a 2400 kg van gives only 1.0 m s⁻². This reluctance to change velocity is called inertia.

The first law is a special case. Put \(F = 0\) into \(F = ma\) and the acceleration is zero: the velocity does not change. The first law says what happens without a resultant force; the second says how much happens with one.

Using one of the forces instead of the resultant. Dividing the 3000 N driving force by the mass gives 2.5 m s⁻², which ignores the forces holding the car back.

Part 3 gives the full form of the law, which also works when the mass changes.

Forces come in pairs

Newton’s third law.

  • If A exerts a force on B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A
  • The two forces act on different bodies
  • They are the same type of force
A BOOK ON A TABLETWO PAIRS
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Why the pair never cancels. Forces cancel only when they act on the same body. The two forces of a pair act on two different bodies, so each appears on a different free-body diagram. A horse can pull a cart forwards even though the cart pulls back equally hard on the horse: the cart’s motion depends only on the forces on the cart.

Finding the partner of a force. Swap the two bodies in its description. The partner of “the force of the Earth on the book” is “the force of the book on the Earth”. It is still gravitational, and it is the same size, but the Earth is so massive that its acceleration is far too small to notice.

Calling the weight of the book and the normal force on it a third-law pair. They are equal here, but both act on the book and they are different types of force. They balance because of the first law, not the third.

Worked example

A 70 kg person in a lift.

Up is positive · \(m = 70 \,\mathrm{kg}\) \(F_\mathrm{N} - mg = ma\)  so  \(F_\mathrm{N} = m(g + a)\) Accelerating upwards: \(a = +1.5 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\) \(F_\mathrm{N} = 70(9.8 + 1.5) = \class{res}{791 \,\mathrm{N}}\) Accelerating downwards: \(a = -1.5 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\) \(F_\mathrm{N} = 70(9.8 - 1.5) = \class{res}{581 \,\mathrm{N}}\)
ACCELERATING UPWARDS
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

What you feel is the normal force. You have no sense of your weight itself: what you notice is the floor pushing up on your feet. When the lift accelerates upwards the floor pushes harder than your weight and you feel heavier. When it accelerates downwards the floor pushes less and you feel lighter. Bathroom scales read the normal force too.

Steady speed. With \(a = 0\) the normal force is 686 N, the same as at rest, whether the lift is going up or down. It is the acceleration that matters, not the direction of travel. A lift slowing down on its way up has a downward acceleration, so the person feels lighter.

If the cable broke, the lift and the person would both fall at 9.8 m s⁻². Then \(F_\mathrm{N} = 70(9.8 - 9.8) = 0\): the person would feel weightless, although their weight is still 686 N.

Six contact forces and three field forces

A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

A field force and a contact force

Weight and the normal force.

\(F_\mathrm{g} = mg\)
  • Weight: the gravitational force on a body. Straight down
  • \(g = 9.8 \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\) near the Earth’s surface
  • Normal force \(F_\mathrm{N}\): the part of a contact force at right angles to the surface
  • A surface can only push, never pull
\(F_\mathrm{N}\) is not always equal to \(mg\). Find it from the other forces.
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

How a table knows how hard to push. A surface behaves like a very stiff spring. A book placed on a table squashes it by an amount far too small to see, and the squashed surface pushes back. The heavier the load, the more it is squashed and the harder it pushes: the normal force adjusts itself to whatever is needed to stop the body sinking in.

Three cases where it is not \(mg\). In a lift accelerating upwards it is more than \(mg\). On a slope it is \(mg \cos \theta\), less than \(mg\). If you press down on a box with an extra 20 N, it is \(mg + 20\). In each case, write the second law at right angles to the surface and solve for \(F_\mathrm{N}\).

Mass is the amount of matter, in kg, and is the same everywhere. Weight is a force, in N, and depends on where the body is: \(g\) on the Moon is about a sixth of its value here.

\(\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\) and \(\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\) are the same unit, so \(g\) is both the gravitational field strength and the acceleration of free fall.

Resolving the weight

A body on a slope.

  • Resolve the weight along the slope and at right angles to it
  • Along: \(mg \sin \theta\), down the slope
  • At right angles: \(F_\mathrm{N} = mg \cos \theta\)
FORCES ON A BLOCK ON A SLOPE
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Checking sin and cos with extreme cases. Flatten the slope to \(\theta = 0\): nothing pulls the block along it, and \(\sin 0 = 0\) agrees. Tip it up to \(\theta = 90^{\circ}\): the whole weight acts along it, and \(\sin 90^{\circ} = 1\). So the component along the slope must be the one with sin.

Why the normal force is \(mg \cos \theta\). The block does not leave the slope or sink into it, so it has no acceleration at right angles to the slope. The forces in that direction must balance: the normal force equals the component of the weight pressing into the slope.

The dashed arrows replace the weight. Use either the weight or its two components, never all three.

Contact force 2

Friction.

  • Acts along the surface, against sliding
  • Static, not sliding: \(F_\mathrm{f} \le \mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N}\)
  • Dynamic, sliding: \(F_\mathrm{f} = \mu_\mathrm{d} F_\mathrm{N}\)
  • \(\mu_\mathrm{d}\) is less than \(\mu_\mathrm{s}\)
PUSHING HARDER ON A BODY AT REST
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Why static friction is an inequality. Static friction is only as large as it needs to be to stop the body sliding. Push a heavy crate with 5 N and friction is 5 N; push with 12 N and it is 12 N. \(\mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N}\) is the most it can reach, not its value. Only at the moment the body is about to slip does \(F_\mathrm{f} = \mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N}\).

What the coefficients depend on. Each is a number with no unit that belongs to a pair of surfaces: rubber on dry road is about 0.8, steel on ice about 0.03. In this model friction does not depend on the area of contact or on the speed of sliding.

Friction is what lets you walk. Your foot pushes backwards on the ground, and static friction from the ground pushes you forwards.

Writing \(F_\mathrm{f} = \mu mg\) for every problem. The equation uses the normal force, which equals \(mg\) only on a level surface with no other vertical forces.

Worked example

Pushing a 5.0 kg crate.

Level floor · \(\mu_\mathrm{s} = 0.40\) · \(\mu_\mathrm{d} = 0.30\) \(F_\mathrm{N} = mg = 5.0 \times 9.8 = 49 \,\mathrm{N}\) Most static friction: \(\mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N} = 0.40 \times 49 = \class{res}{19.6 \,\mathrm{N}}\) A 15 N push: it stays at rest. Friction \(= \class{res}{15 \,\mathrm{N}}\) A 25 N push: it slides \(F_\mathrm{f} = \mu_\mathrm{d} F_\mathrm{N} = 0.30 \times 49 = 14.7 \,\mathrm{N}\) \(a = \dfrac{F - F_\mathrm{f}}{m} = \dfrac{25 - 14.7}{5.0} = \class{res}{2.1 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\)
FORCES ON THE CRATE
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Always test for sliding first. Compare the push with the largest static friction available. 15 N is less than 19.6 N, so the crate does not move and friction simply matches the push. Using \(0.40 \times 49\) there would give a friction force larger than the push, which would mean the crate accelerating backwards.

The jolt when it starts. At 19.6 N the crate breaks free and friction falls at once to 14.7 N. If you keep pushing just as hard there is suddenly a resultant force of 4.9 N, so the crate lurches forwards. That is why a stuck object tends to shoot away when it finally gives.

Worked example

Sliding down a 20° slope.

\(\mu_\mathrm{d} = 0.25\) · \(\theta = 20^{\circ}\) · find the acceleration \(F_\mathrm{N} = mg \cos \theta\) \(F_\mathrm{f} = \mu_\mathrm{d} F_\mathrm{N} = \mu_\mathrm{d} \, mg \cos \theta\) \(ma = mg \sin \theta - \mu_\mathrm{d} \, mg \cos \theta\) The mass cancels \(a = g(\sin \theta - \mu_\mathrm{d} \cos \theta)\) \(a = 9.8(\sin 20^{\circ} - 0.25 \cos 20^{\circ}) = \class{res}{1.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\)
FORCES ON THE BLOCKNOT TO SCALE
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Why the mass does not matter. A heavier block is pulled down the slope harder, presses on it harder and so meets more friction, and is harder to accelerate. All three effects are proportional to the mass, so it divides out. A full sledge and an empty one slide down the same slope with the same acceleration.

The angle at which it would just start to slip. A block at rest is about to slide when the component of its weight down the slope equals the largest static friction: \(mg \sin \theta = \mu_\mathrm{s} mg \cos \theta\), so \(\tan \theta = \mu_\mathrm{s}\). Tilting a surface until a block slips is a quick way to measure \(\mu_\mathrm{s}\).

Without friction the acceleration would be \(9.8 \sin 20^{\circ} = 3.4 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\). Friction has removed more than two thirds of it.

Contact force 3

Tension.

  • The pull of a stretched string or rope, along its length
  • A light string has the same tension all the way along
\(m_{1} = 3.0 \,\mathrm{kg}\) · \(m_{2} = 2.0 \,\mathrm{kg}\) \(a = \dfrac{(m_{1} - m_{2})g}{m_{1} + m_{2}} = \dfrac{1.0 \times 9.8}{5.0} = \class{res}{1.96 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\) \(T - m_{2}g = m_{2}a\) \(T = m_{2}(g + a) = 2.0(9.8 + 1.96) = \class{res}{23.5 \,\mathrm{N}}\)
TWO MASSES OVER A PULLEY
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Where the first line comes from. The string keeps the two masses moving together, so they share one acceleration. Treat them as a single 5.0 kg system: the 29.4 N weight drives it one way round the pulley and the 19.6 N weight opposes it. The tension pulls equally on both and is internal to the system, so it does not appear.

Why the tension lies between the two weights. The 2.0 kg mass accelerates upwards, so the string must pull up on it with more than its weight of 19.6 N. The 3.0 kg mass accelerates downwards, so the string must pull up on it with less than its weight of 29.4 N. Check with that mass: \(29.4 - 23.5 = 5.9 \,\mathrm{N}\), and \(3.0 \times 1.96 = 5.9 \,\mathrm{N}\).

A string can only pull. On a free-body diagram, tension always points away from the body, along the string.

“Light” means the string’s own mass can be ignored. A heavy rope hanging down has a larger tension at the top than at the bottom, because the top holds up the rope beneath it.

Contact force 4

Hooke’s law.

\(F_\mathrm{H} = -kx\)
  • \(x\): extension or compression
  • \(k\): spring constant, in N m⁻¹
  • The minus sign: the force is opposite to \(x\)
STRETCHING A SPRING
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

A worked case. A 0.30 kg mass hung from a spring stretches it by 6.0 cm. At rest the spring force equals the weight: \(k \times 0.060 = 0.30 \times 9.8\), so \(k = \frac{2.94}{0.060} = 49 \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\). A 0.50 kg mass would stretch the same spring by \(\frac{4.9}{49} = 0.10 \,\mathrm{m}\).

Why it is called a restoring force. Stretch the spring and it pulls back; squash it and it pushes out. Either way the force points towards the natural length, against the displacement, and that is what the minus sign says. In a calculation of size only, use \(F = kx\).

A large \(k\) means a stiff spring: a car’s suspension spring is about 20 000 N m⁻¹, the spring in a ballpoint pen about 200 N m⁻¹.

The law holds only up to the limit of proportionality. Beyond it the graph curves, and the spring may not return to its natural length.

Contact force 5

Buoyancy.

\(F_\mathrm{b} = \rho V g\)
  • The upward force of a fluid on a body in it
  • \(\rho\): density of the fluid
  • \(V\): volume of fluid displaced
It equals the weight of the fluid displaced.
A BODY HELD UNDER THE SURFACE
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Where the force comes from. Pressure in a fluid increases with depth. The bottom of a body is deeper than its top, so the fluid pushes up on the bottom harder than it pushes down on the top. The difference is the buoyancy force.

A worked case. A ball of mass 1.2 kg and volume \(2.0 \times 10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) is held under water of density 1000 kg m⁻³. Buoyancy: \(1000 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.8 = 19.6 \,\mathrm{N}\). Weight: \(1.2 \times 9.8 = 11.8 \,\mathrm{N}\). On release the resultant is 7.8 N upwards, so it rises.

Floating. The ball comes to rest at the surface when the buoyancy has fallen to equal its weight: it then displaces only \(1.2 \times 10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) of water, so 60% of it is under the surface. A body floats if its average density is less than the fluid’s.

Using the density or the mass of the body in \(\rho V g\). The equation is about the fluid that has been pushed aside.

Contact force 6

Viscous drag.

\(F_\mathrm{d} = 6 \pi \eta r v\)
  • On a small sphere of radius \(r\) moving at speed \(v\)
  • \(\eta\): viscosity of the fluid, in Pa s
  • Always against the velocity
Terminal speed: \(F_\mathrm{g} = F_\mathrm{b} + F_\mathrm{d}\)
A SPHERE FALLING THROUGH A FLUID
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Viscosity is how much a fluid resists flowing. Layers of fluid drag on each other as they slide past. Water has a viscosity of about 0.001 Pa s; honey is several thousand times more viscous, which is why a spoon sinks through it so slowly.

How this explains terminal speed. The weight and the buoyancy do not change as the sphere falls, but the drag is proportional to the speed. At the start there is no drag and the sphere accelerates. As it speeds up the drag grows, the resultant force shrinks, and the acceleration falls. When the drag has grown enough to make the three forces balance, the speed stops changing. This is the force behind the speed–time curve met in A.1.

The equation is for a small sphere moving slowly enough for the fluid to flow smoothly round it. It does not describe a skydiver or a car.

Worked example

A steel ball falling through oil.

\(r = 1.0 \,\mathrm{mm}\) · \(\rho_\mathrm{s} = 7800\), \(\rho_\mathrm{o} = 900 \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m}^{-3}\) · \(\eta = 0.50 \,\mathrm{Pa} \,\mathrm{s}\) \(V = \tfrac{4}{3} \pi r^{3} = 4.19 \times 10^{-9} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) \(F_\mathrm{g} = \rho_\mathrm{s} V g = 7800 \times V \times 9.8 = 3.20 \times 10^{-4} \,\mathrm{N}\) \(F_\mathrm{b} = \rho_\mathrm{o} V g = 900 \times V \times 9.8 = 0.37 \times 10^{-4} \,\mathrm{N}\)
At terminal speed \(F_\mathrm{d} = F_\mathrm{g} - F_\mathrm{b} = 2.83 \times 10^{-4} \,\mathrm{N}\) \(v = \dfrac{F_\mathrm{d}}{6 \pi \eta r} = \dfrac{2.83 \times 10^{-4}}{6 \pi \times 0.50 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-3}}\) \(v = \class{res}{0.030 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Two densities, two jobs. The steel’s density gives the mass of the ball and so its weight. The oil’s density gives the buoyancy. Both use the same volume because the ball is completely under the surface, so it displaces its own volume of oil.

How the radius changes the answer. Weight and buoyancy depend on the volume, so on \(r^{3}\); the drag depends on \(r\). Dividing one by the other, the terminal speed depends on \(r^{2}\): a ball of twice the radius falls four times as fast. This is why fine drizzle drifts down while large raindrops fall quickly.

Convert the radius to metres before anything else. A radius left in millimetres makes the volume wrong by a factor of a thousand million.

Timing a ball between two marks on a tall tube of liquid, once it has reached terminal speed, is a standard way to measure viscosity.

What a field is. A body with mass or charge changes the space around it, and a second body placed in that space feels a force without being touched. The region where this happens is the field. The idea is developed properly in theme D; in this topic you only need to recognise these forces, name them and include them in a resultant.

A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Mass in motion

Linear momentum.

\(p = mv\)
  • A vector, in the direction of the velocity
  • Unit: kg m s⁻¹, the same as N s
A 1500 kg car at 20 m s⁻¹ \(p = mv = 1500 \times 20 = \class{res}{3.0 \times 10^{4} \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\) A 0.050 kg ball at 40 m s⁻¹, the other way \(p = mv = 0.050 \times (-40) = \class{res}{-2.0 \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

What momentum tells you. It measures how hard a moving body is to stop. A 0.16 kg cricket ball at 40 m s⁻¹ and a 1600 kg car creeping at 0.40 m s⁻¹ have the same kinetic energy, 128 J. But the ball’s momentum is 6.4 kg m s⁻¹ and the car’s is 640 kg m s⁻¹: stopping the car in the same time takes a force a hundred times larger.

Why the sign matters. Two identical trolleys moving towards each other at the same speed have momenta of \(+mv\) and \(-mv\). Their total momentum is zero, although both are moving. Every momentum calculation starts by choosing a positive direction.

“Linear” separates this from angular momentum, the momentum of rotation, which HL students meet in A.4.

The full second law

Force changes momentum.

\(F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\)resultant force = rate of change of momentum

\(F = ma\)

This is the law as Newton stated it. \(F = ma\) is the special case for a body whose mass stays the same. It fails for a rocket, which may burn most of its mass as fuel during a launch: the same thrust produces a larger and larger acceleration as the rocket gets lighter.

Using it for a stream of matter. When matter arrives or leaves steadily, look at the momentum that changes in one second. A hose that delivers 12 kg of water each second at 15 m s⁻¹ onto a wall, where the water stops, destroys \(12 \times 15 = 180 \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) of momentum every second. The wall therefore pushes on the water with 180 N, and by the third law the water pushes on the wall with 180 N.

Why the area. A constant force gives a rectangle on this graph, and its area, height × width, is \(F \Delta t\). A real collision force rises and falls, so cut the time into thin strips: each strip’s area is the small change in momentum in that short time, and together they make the whole area. The average force is the height of the rectangle that has the same area over the same time.

Why a longer stop is a gentler stop. A passenger in a crash loses a fixed amount of momentum, so the impulse is fixed. A seat belt and an airbag stretch the stop from a few thousandths of a second to a few tenths, so the average force is tens of times smaller. Crumple zones, crash helmets, bending your knees on landing and drawing your hands back to catch a ball all work in the same way.

For a curved graph, find the area by counting squares, as for a velocity–time graph in A.1. One square is worth its height in N times its width in s.

A rocket does not push on the air. It pushes on its own exhaust, and the exhaust pushes back. That is why a rocket works in the vacuum of space, where there is nothing else to push against. Seen as a whole, the rocket and its gas keep the same total momentum: the gas gains momentum backwards and the rocket gains the same amount forwards.

Will it lift off? A thrust of 5000 N can raise a rocket only if it weighs less than that: a mass below \(\frac{5000}{9.8} = 510 \,\mathrm{kg}\). For a 400 kg rocket the resultant is \(5000 - 3920 = 1080 \,\mathrm{N}\) upwards and the first acceleration is \(\frac{1080}{400} = 2.7 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\). As fuel burns, the weight falls and the acceleration rises.

The general pattern for a steady stream: force = mass per second × change in velocity.

The principle

Conservation of momentum.

TWO BODIES COLLIDEPRESS → TO PLAY
  • The total momentum of a system stays constant…
  • …unless a resultant external force acts on it
\(m_{1}u_{1} + m_{2}u_{2} = m_{1}v_{1} + m_{2}v_{2}\)
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

It follows from the third law. While the bodies touch, each pushes on the other with an equal and opposite force, for exactly the same time. So they receive equal and opposite impulses: whatever momentum one gains, the other loses. The total cannot change.

Internal and external forces. The forces the two bodies exert on each other are internal to the system and always cancel in this way. Friction from the track or a push from outside is external and does change the total. Collisions are so brief that external forces usually have no time to matter, which is why the principle works so well for them.

Where the 54 kJ went. Total energy is always conserved. The missing kinetic energy did the work of crumpling the metal and was transferred to thermal energy and sound. None of that requires momentum to be lost: momentum has no other form to change into.

A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Elastic collisions are rare at everyday sizes. Any collision you can hear or that leaves a dent has transferred some kinetic energy elsewhere. Steel balls and snooker balls come close. Collisions between the molecules of a gas are perfectly elastic, which is one of the assumptions of the ideal gas model in B.3.

How kinetic energy can change when momentum cannot. Momentum is a vector, so gains in one direction cancel losses in the other. Kinetic energy is a scalar that is never negative, so nothing cancels: it can only be shared out differently or transferred to another form. In an explosion it comes from a store, such as chemical energy or a compressed spring.

A collision in which the bodies stick together loses the most kinetic energy that conservation of momentum allows.

What counts as an explosion. Any event in which bodies that were together fly apart because of forces between them: skaters pushing off, a gun firing a bullet, a nucleus emitting an alpha particle, a firework bursting. The forces are internal, so the total momentum stays what it was. From rest that is zero, so the momenta of the pieces must cancel.

Who gets the energy. The two skaters leave with equal sizes of momentum, 120 kg m s⁻¹ each, so the lighter one moves faster, and has more of the kinetic energy: 180 J against 120 J. This is why a bullet carries almost all the energy of a shot and the heavy rifle very little. The 300 J came from chemical energy in the skaters’ muscles.

Equal and opposite momenta mean the speeds are in the inverse ratio of the masses: the masses are 3 : 2, so the speeds are 2 : 3.

Why each direction is separate. The force between the two pucks has an \(x\) part and a \(y\) part. Each part acts equally and oppositely on the two pucks, so the argument from the third law works for each direction on its own. It is the same independence of perpendicular directions that you used for projectiles in A.1.

Reading the diagram before calculating. Before the collision there is no momentum along \(y\) at all. Afterwards the 2.0 kg puck moves along \(+y\), so the 3.0 kg puck must have exactly as much momentum along \(-y\). And since the 2.0 kg puck now has no momentum along \(x\), the 3.0 kg puck must carry all of the original \(x\) momentum.

Choose the \(x\) direction along the velocity of the incoming body. Then the momentum before the collision has only one component.

The pucks do not meet head-on: the first strikes the second off-centre. The force on each acts along the line joining their centres at the moment of contact, so the puck that was at rest leaves along that line. A head-on collision would keep both on the original line.

Describing motion in a circle

Angular velocity.

\(v = \dfrac{2 \pi r}{T} = \omega r\)
  • \(T\): the period, the time for one revolution
  • \(\omega\): the angle turned through each second, in rad s⁻¹
\(r = 2.0 \,\mathrm{m}\) · \(T = 5.0 \,\mathrm{s}\) \(\omega = \dfrac{2 \pi}{T} = \dfrac{2 \pi}{5.0} = \class{res}{1.26 \,\mathrm{rad} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\) \(v = \omega r = 1.26 \times 2.0 = \class{res}{2.5 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
MOVING ROUND A CIRCLE
A.2 FORCES AND MOMENTUM · SL + HL

Why radians. An angle in radians is the arc length divided by the radius. So a body that turns through \(\Delta \theta\) moves a distance \(r \Delta \theta\) along the arc, with no conversion factor. Divide both sides by the time taken and you have \(v = \omega r\). One full turn is an arc of \(2 \pi r\), so it is \(2 \pi\) rad, and turning through that in one period gives \(\omega = \frac{2 \pi}{T}\).

One angular velocity, many speeds. Every point on a spinning roundabout turns through the same angle each second, so all share one \(\omega\). But a child at the rim travels round a bigger circle in the same time than a child near the middle, and so moves faster: \(v\) is proportional to \(r\).

Given a rate of rotation in revolutions per minute, divide by 60 for revolutions per second, then multiply by \(2 \pi\) for rad s⁻¹.

Why towards the centre. Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time. Take the velocity arrows at two points close together on the circle: they are the same length but turned through a small angle. The arrow you must add to the first to get the second points inwards, along the radius. An acceleration at right angles to the velocity changes its direction and leaves its size alone.

A worked case. For the body on the last slide, \(a = \omega^{2} r = 1.26^{2} \times 2.0 = 3.2 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\), about a third of \(g\). The three forms of the equation are the same thing with \(v = \omega r\) or \(\omega = \frac{2 \pi}{T}\) put in: use whichever matches the data you have.

Doubling the speed on the same circle makes the acceleration four times as large. Doubling the radius at the same speed halves it.

What happens if it disappears. Cut the string and the ball does not fly outwards. With no resultant force it obeys the first law and carries on in a straight line along the tangent, in the direction it was moving at that instant.

The “force” that throws you outwards. In a car turning left you seem to be flung to the right. Nothing is pushing you that way. Your body is trying to carry on in a straight line while the car turns underneath you, until the door or the seat belt supplies the inward force that makes you turn too. There is no outward force to draw.

Why the speed stays the same. The force is at right angles to the velocity, so it has no component along the direction of motion and does no work. With no work done the kinetic energy cannot change.

Why static friction, when the car is moving. A rolling tyre does not slide over the road: the patch touching the ground is at rest on it for an instant. The sideways grip is therefore static friction, which can be anything up to \(\mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N}\). The car skids when the bend demands more than that.

Rain and tight bends. On a wet road \(\mu_\mathrm{s}\) may fall to 0.40. The greatest speed becomes \(\sqrt{0.40 \times 9.8 \times 50} = 14 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\): halving the grip cuts the safe speed by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\), not 2. Halving the radius does the same.

On a banked track the surface is tilted, so the normal force leans towards the centre of the bend and its horizontal component supplies some or all of the centripetal force. Less friction is needed. You may be asked to explain this, but not to calculate with it.

Why the string cannot be horizontal. The ball has no vertical acceleration, so something must balance its weight, and the only upward force available is the vertical part of the tension. A horizontal string has none. However fast you whirl the ball, the string always slopes down a little.

A worked case. A ball moves at 2.0 m s⁻¹ in a horizontal circle of radius 0.60 m. \(\tan \theta = \frac{2.0^{2}}{0.60 \times 9.8} = 0.68\), so the string is at 34° to the vertical. The mass has cancelled: a heavy ball and a light one at the same speed and radius hang at the same angle.

Resolve vertically and towards the centre, not along the string. The acceleration is horizontal, so those are the directions in which the second law is simplest.

Building each equation. In both places, take “towards the centre” as positive and add up the real forces. At the top the centre is below the ball, so tension and weight both point towards it. At the bottom the centre is above, so the tension points towards it and the weight away. The resultant towards the centre must equal \(\frac{mv^{2}}{r}\).

The slowest it can go over the top. As the speed at the top is reduced, less force is needed and the tension falls. A string cannot push, so the least possible tension is zero. Then the weight alone is the centripetal force: \(mg = \frac{mv^{2}}{r}\), so \(v = \sqrt{gr}\). Any slower and the string goes slack before the top.

Only the top and the bottom are examined. At other points the weight has a component along the path as well, which is what changes the speed.

The same two equations describe a car on a hump-backed bridge or a rider on a loop: replace the tension by the normal force, with its direction.

Why the string is most likely to break at the bottom. There the tension has two jobs: it must hold up the weight and also supply the whole centripetal force, at the point where the ball is moving fastest. At the top the weight supplies part of the centripetal force for free, and the ball is at its slowest.

Where the two speeds come from. They are not independent. Between the bottom and the top the ball rises by one diameter, 1.6 m, and kinetic energy is transferred to gravitational potential energy. For each kilogram: \(\frac{1}{2}v^{2} = \frac{1}{2} \times 7.0^{2} - 9.8 \times 1.6 = 8.8\), so \(v = 4.2 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). This is conservation of energy, from A.3.
