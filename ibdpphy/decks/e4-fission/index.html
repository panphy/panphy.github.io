E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

One reaction of many

The slide shows \({}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}\). Check it as in E.3: nucleon numbers, \(1 + 235 = 141 + 92 + 3\); proton numbers, \(0 + 92 = 56 + 36 + 0\). A uranium nucleus can split in dozens of different ways, into different pairs of fragments with two or three neutrons. The two fragments are usually unequal in size.

Why the nucleus breaks apart

When the neutron is absorbed, the new uranium-236 nucleus gains the neutron’s binding energy, about 6.5 MeV, and is left wobbling violently, like a drop of liquid that has been struck. It stretches, narrows in the middle and, pushed apart by the repulsion between its two positively charged halves, breaks in two.

Spontaneous fission needs no neutron. It happens in the heaviest nuclei, but for uranium it is extremely rare compared with alpha decay.

In any fission equation, find a missing number by balancing nucleon number and proton number, exactly as for a decay equation.

Where the 15 MeV comes from

To touch a uranium nucleus, a proton must come within about 8.6 fm of its center, the sum of the two radii. The electric potential energy there is \(\dfrac{k(92e)(e)}{r}\), and with \(ke^{2} = 1.44\) MeV fm that is \(\dfrac{92 \times 1.44}{8.6} \approx 15\) MeV. A neutron pays none of this.

Why slow is better

A slow neutron spends longer close to each nucleus it passes, so it is far more likely to be absorbed. The neutrons from fission are fast, about 1 MeV, so a reactor slows them to about 0.03 eV, the energy of a gas molecule at room temperature. That is the job of the moderator, later in this deck.

A neutron cannot be stored

A free neutron decays by beta-minus decay, into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, with a half-life of about 10 minutes. A reactor cannot keep a supply: the neutrons for each fission must come from the fissions before it.

Saying that the nucleus attracts a neutron. Nothing pulls it in: it simply meets no repulsion, so it reaches the nucleus, where the strong force takes over.

MOVING UP THE CURVE
E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

Why mass is not on the list

Nucleon number and charge balance exactly, as in any nuclear equation, and so do energy and momentum. Mass does not, because mass is itself a form of energy: \(E = mc^{2}\). What is conserved is the total energy, rest energy included. The products have less rest energy than the uranium and the neutron, and the difference appears as their kinetic energy.

Reading the curve

Binding energy per nucleon measures how tightly a nucleus is held together: the higher on the graph, the more energy each nucleon gave up when the nucleus was formed, and the more stable it is. The peak is near iron and nickel. Uranium sits on the downward slope to the right. Split it into two middle-sized nuclei and each of its 235 nucleons ends up about 0.8 MeV more tightly bound. That is roughly \(235 \times 0.8 \approx 190\) MeV released in all.

Where the slope comes from

Every proton in a nucleus repels every other, over the whole width of the nucleus, while the strong nuclear force only acts between neighbors. In a very large nucleus the repulsion adds up and the nucleons are less tightly bound on average. Two smaller nuclei have less of that repulsion to fight.

The other side of the peak

Very light nuclei can also move up the curve, by joining together. That is fusion, in E.5.

Saying that the fragments have less binding energy. They have more. Binding energy is energy already given out: more of it means a lower, more stable state.

Worked example: first method

Energy released, from binding energies.

\({}^{235}\mathrm{U}\) absorbs a neutron and splits into \({}^{141}\mathrm{Ba}\), \({}^{92}\mathrm{Kr}\) and three neutrons. Find the energy released. Energy released = binding energy after − before \(= 1958 - 1784\) \(= \class{res}{174 \,\mathrm{MeV}}\)
Free neutrons have no binding energy: leave them out.

Total, not per nucleon

The numbers in the table are per nucleon. Multiply each by its nucleon number to get the total binding energy of that nucleus before adding or subtracting anything. Subtracting the per-nucleon values directly, \(8.4 - 7.6\), gives the gain for one nucleon, not for the reaction.

After minus before

The products are more tightly bound, so their total binding energy is larger. The increase is the energy that has been given out.

How big is 174 MeV?

Burning one atom of carbon releases about 4 eV. One fission releases over forty million times as much. That ratio is why a nuclear power station uses a few tonnes of fuel a year where a coal station burns millions of tonnes.

The incoming neutron and the three released are free particles with zero binding energy. They appear in the mass calculation on the next slide, but not in this one.

E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

The same answer, a different route

Use final minus initial: \(\Delta m = m_\mathrm{products} - m_\mathrm{reactants} < 0\). The rest-energy change \(\Delta m c^{2}\) is negative and the released energy \(-\Delta m c^{2}\) is positive. Including the products’ kinetic energy and radiation, \(\Delta E_\mathrm{total} = 0\). The products have less mass than what went in. The missing mass, 0.186 u, about a fifth of the mass of one nucleon, has been converted to energy: \(E = mc^{2}\). The two methods agree, as they must: binding energy is mass defect expressed as energy.

Keep every figure

The answer comes from the difference of two numbers that agree to four significant figures. Round the masses to 236.05 and 235.87 and the difference becomes 0.18, an error of several per cent. Subtract first and round at the end.

In joules

\(173 \,\mathrm{MeV} = 173 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 2.8 \times 10^{-11}\) J for one fission.

Data booklet: \(1 \,\mathrm{u} = 931.5 \,\mathrm{MeV} \,c^{-2}\). Multiplying a mass defect in u by 931.5 gives the energy in MeV directly.

Forgetting that one neutron goes in and three come out. Count all the particles on both sides.

A kilogram of uranium-235.

  • So 1 kg of uranium-235 matches over two thousand tonnes of coal

Where the 173 MeV goes

The two fragments are both positively charged and start almost touching, so they repel each other fiercely and fly apart. They carry about 165 MeV as kinetic energy between them. The neutrons take a few MeV, and the rest leaves as gamma rays. The fragments travel only a few thousandths of a millimeter through the fuel before collisions bring them to rest, and their kinetic energy becomes thermal energy of the fuel rod. A reactor is a way of heating metal rods.

Specific energy

\(7.1 \times 10^{13}\) J kg⁻¹ is the figure in the fuel table of A.3: about a million times that of any chemical fuel.

\(1 \,\mathrm{MeV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-13}\) J. Converting once, at the end, avoids carrying powers of ten through the working.

E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

How fast it can grow

In pure uranium-235 the time from one generation of fissions to the next is about a hundred-millionth of a second. If every fission caused two more, eighty generations, less than a millionth of a second, would be enough to split every nucleus in a kilogram. That is a bomb. A reactor is built so that this cannot happen.

Not every neutron causes a fission

Some escape through the surface of the fuel. Some are absorbed by other nuclei without causing fission, especially by uranium-238, which makes up over 99% of natural uranium. What matters is how many, on average, go on to cause another fission.

A chain reaction: the neutrons released by one fission go on to induce further fissions.

The least mass that keeps the chain going is the critical mass: about 52 kg for a bare sphere of uranium-235, 17 cm across.
E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

Surface against volume

The neutrons are made throughout the volume of the fuel, which grows as \(r^{3}\), but they escape through its surface, which grows only as \(r^{2}\). Double the radius and eight times as many neutrons are made, while the surface they can leak through is only four times as large. Above some size, enough neutrons are caught inside for each fission to cause at least one more.

Not a fixed number

The critical mass depends on the shape and on what surrounds the fuel. A sphere has the least surface for its volume, so it needs the least mass. A layer of material around the fuel that reflects neutrons back lowers the critical mass further.

Starting a reactor

A reactor does not wait for a stray neutron. A small neutron source in the core makes sure the chain starts smoothly whenever the control rods are raised.

The fuel in a power reactor is only 3 to 5% uranium-235, so a reactor holds many tonnes of fuel, spread out and mixed with the moderator.

How much fuel actually split

A kiloton is the energy of a thousand tonnes of TNT, \(4.2 \times 10^{12}\) J, so 21 kilotons is about \(8.8 \times 10^{13}\) J. One kilogram of uranium-235 holds about \(2.6 \times 10^{24}\) nuclei, and at \(2.8 \times 10^{-11}\) J each, splitting all of them gives about \(7 \times 10^{13}\) J. So the whole explosion came from splitting only about a kilogram of nuclei.

Why a reactor cannot do this

A bomb packs nearly pure fissile metal together in a fraction of a millisecond, so that almost every neutron finds another nucleus. Reactor fuel is only 3 to 5% uranium-235, and its chain reaction runs on slow neutrons, which take far longer to cause the next fission. If a reactor overheats, it can melt or burn, but it cannot explode like a bomb.

The Baker bomb was a plutonium bomb of the Nagasaki type. Its photograph is one of the most reproduced pictures of the twentieth century.

E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

A fine balance

“Critical” sounds like an emergency, but in a reactor it is the normal working state: every fission leads, on average, to exactly one more, so the number of fissions each second, and the power, stay constant. To raise the power the operators let the number creep slightly above one for a while, then bring it back to one at the new level.

The two jobs

The neutrons released by fission are fast, and fast neutrons are poor at causing fission in uranium-235. They must be slowed: that is the moderator’s job. And since each fission releases two or three neutrons where only one is wanted, the extra ones must be removed: that is the job of the control rods. The next slides take these in turn.

A power reactor cannot explode like a bomb. Its fuel is only a few per cent uranium-235, far too dilute for a fast chain reaction.

  • Water or graphite

E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

Why light nuclei

This is a collision problem from A.2. A ball that hits a much heavier ball bounces off with almost all its speed. A ball that hits one of its own mass can hand over most of its kinetic energy in a single collision. A neutron slows fastest by colliding with nuclei close to its own mass: the hydrogen in water, or carbon in graphite. Bouncing off uranium nuclei would hardly slow it at all.

Why slow is better

A slow neutron spends longer near a nucleus, so it is more likely to be captured. For uranium-235 a thermal neutron is hundreds of times more likely to cause fission than a fast one. “Thermal” means in thermal equilibrium with the moderator: a kinetic energy of about \(\frac{3}{2} k_\mathrm{B} T\), a fraction of an electronvolt.

A good moderator slows neutrons without absorbing them, which rules out many materials.

Saying that the moderator slows the reaction. It slows the neutrons, which makes the reaction go. Without a moderator the chain reaction would stop.

Control rods.

  • Rods of a material that absorbs neutrons strongly, such as boron or cadmium
  • Lowered further in: more neutrons absorbed, fewer fissions, less power
  • Raised: more fissions, more power

How the power is held steady

Instruments count the neutrons in the core. If the count starts to rise, the rods are moved a little further in and absorb more; if it falls, they are eased out. The adjustment is continuous and automatic.

Shutting down

In an emergency the rods are dropped fully into the core, in many designs simply by releasing them to fall under gravity. Within seconds the chain reaction stops. The core does not go cold, though: the fission products already in the fuel go on decaying and giving out thermal energy, at first about 7% of full power, so cooling must continue for a long time afterwards. Loss of that cooling was what destroyed the reactors at Fukushima in 2011.

Mixing up the two. Control rods absorb; the moderator slows without absorbing.

A nuclear power plant.

  • Heat exchanger: hot coolant from the core boils water in a separate loop
  • Steam turns a turbine and generator
  • Shielding: steel and concrete absorb neutrons and gamma rays
A PRESSURIZED-WATER REACTORSCHEMATIC

Why two loops

The water that flows through the core becomes radioactive, and it could carry traces of fission products if a fuel rod leaked. The heat exchanger passes its thermal energy through the walls of metal tubes to a second, separate supply of water, which never touches the core. Only that clean water, as steam, goes out to the turbine hall.

Why loop 1 does not boil

Its water leaves the core at about 300 °C, but it is kept at about 150 times atmospheric pressure, which raises its boiling point above that. It stays liquid, carries energy well, and keeps moderating the neutrons. Loop 2 is at a much lower pressure, so the same hot coil boils it.

From here on it is an ordinary power station

Steam drives a turbine, which turns a generator (D.4); the steam is condensed and pumped back. The reactor replaces the furnace of a coal or gas station and nothing else. Like them it is a heat engine, with an efficiency of about a third (B.4).

What the shielding stops

Neutrons and gamma rays are both very penetrating. A steel pressure vessel and several meters of concrete surround the core, to protect the people working there and to contain the radioactive material if something goes wrong.

Know the job of each of the four named parts: moderator, control rods, heat exchanger and shielding.

In this design the same water is both coolant and moderator.

One pellet, one tonne of coal

A fuel pellet has a mass of about 7 g. Over its years in a reactor, each gram of uranium releases roughly \(4 \times 10^{9}\) J, so one pellet releases about \(3 \times 10^{10}\) J. Burning one tonne of coal releases about \(2.4 \times 10^{10}\) J. A pellet the size of a fingertip does the work of a tonne of coal.

Why the core glows blue

Light travels through water at only about \(0.75c\). Fast electrons from the decay of fission products can move faster than that, and as they do they give out a cone of blue light, called Cherenkov radiation, a little like the bow wave of a boat. The glow is a sign of intense radiation, which the water absorbs before it reaches the surface.

Cherenkov radiation is not in the course. Nothing in it moves faster than light in a vacuum.

A sense of scale

A large power station, enough for a city of a million people, consumes less than four kilograms of uranium-235 a day: a lump the size of a tennis ball. A coal station of the same output burns about ten thousand tonnes of coal a day, several trainloads, and sends some thirty thousand tonnes of carbon dioxide into the air.

Fuel rods hold more than that

Reactor fuel is only 3 to 5% uranium-235; the rest is uranium-238, which does not sustain the chain reaction. So the fuel actually loaded is some thirty times the mass calculated here, and it stays in the core for several years.

E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

Why the fragments are neutron-rich

Stable heavy nuclei need many more neutrons than protons: uranium-235 has 143 neutrons to 92 protons, a ratio of 1.55. Stable nuclei of middle size need fewer: about 1.3 neutrons per proton. When uranium splits, the fragments inherit its high ratio. Barium-141 has 85 neutrons where the stable isotopes of barium have at most 82. Such nuclei correct the imbalance by beta-minus decay, turning neutrons into protons, usually several times in succession.

Why spent fuel is so much more dangerous than new fuel

Fresh uranium fuel is only weakly radioactive and can be handled with gloves. After years in a reactor the same rods are filled with intensely radioactive fission products and would deliver a fatal dose in minutes. They also go on producing thermal energy from their decay.

Middle half-lives are the worst

A very short half-life means fierce activity that is soon over. A very long one means feeble activity. Strontium-90 and cesium-137, at about thirty years, are active enough to be dangerous and last long enough to be a problem for centuries.

Strontium behaves chemically like calcium and is taken up by bone; iodine collects in the thyroid gland. Where a nuclide goes in the body matters as much as its activity.

Managing nuclear waste.

  • First years: under water in cooling ponds, which absorb radiation and remove heat
  • Then: sealed in steel and concrete casks, or reprocessed and set in glass
  • Finally: burial deep in stable rock

Two timescales

The graph has a logarithmic time axis: each step is ten times longer than the last. For the first few hundred years the activity is dominated by fission products such as strontium-90 and cesium-137. After ten half-lives, about 300 years, only a thousandth of them is left. What remains is a much lower activity from plutonium and other heavy nuclei, which lasts for tens of thousands of years. A store has to be safe on both timescales.

The impact of long-term storage

The waste must be kept from groundwater and from people for longer than any human institution has lasted. That means choosing rock that has been stable for millions of years, containers that will not corrode, and some way of warning generations who may not share our languages. It also means cost, transport of dangerous material, and the consent of whoever lives nearby. Against that, the volume is small: all the high-level waste a person’s lifetime of electricity would produce fits in a drinks can.

The guide expects you to consider the impact of long-term storage. Be ready with points on both sides: the long timescale and its risks, and the small volume and the possibility of deep, stable burial.

E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

Why it is still hot

Shutting a reactor down stops the chain reaction, but not the fission products, which go on decaying. Just after shutdown their decay still gives out about 6% of the reactor’s full power, enough to melt the core if the cooling stops. The heat falls quickly over the first days and then slowly over years, which is why the fuel spends its first years under water.

Why water

Water is cheap, carries heat away well, and a few meters of it absorb the gamma rays and neutrons, so people can work safely at the edge of the pool. Once the heat output is low enough, air flowing around a thick steel and concrete cask can remove it instead.

Three stages, in order: cooling ponds, then dry casks or reprocessing, then deep burial.

Cesium-137: half-life 30 years What fraction remains after 300 years? Number of half-lives \(= \dfrac{300}{30} = 10\) Fraction remaining \(= \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{10}\) \(= \class{res}{\dfrac{1}{1024}}\)
  • About a thousandth after three centuries
  • Plutonium-239 over the same time: \(\dfrac{300}{24\,000}\) of a half-life. Hardly any has gone
Ten half-lives: a factor of a thousand.
E.4 FISSION, SL + HL

A useful guide

\(2^{10} = 1024\), so every ten half-lives reduce the activity to about one thousandth of its initial value, and twenty half-lives to about one millionth. For cesium-137 and strontium-90 that is 300 and 600 years: long, but within the span of buildings that still stand. For plutonium-239 ten half-lives is 240 000 years.

This is E.3 put to work

The method is the half-life calculation for whole numbers of half-lives. At HL, a time that is not a whole number of half-lives needs \(N = N_{0} e^{-\lambda t}\).

Activity and the number of undecayed nuclei fall by the same fraction, so the answer serves for either.
