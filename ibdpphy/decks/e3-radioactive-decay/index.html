E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

Why the chemistry is the same

Chemical behavior is decided by the electrons, and a neutral atom has as many electrons as it has protons. Extra neutrons change the mass of the nucleus but not its charge, so all three kinds of carbon form the same compounds. Carbon dioxide made with carbon-14 behaves in the atmosphere, and in a plant, exactly like ordinary carbon dioxide.

Why the nuclear behavior differs

Stability depends on the balance of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable. Carbon-14 has too many neutrons for its six protons and decays, with a half-life of 5730 years. This is what makes radiocarbon dating possible, later in the deck.

Nucleon number \(A\) = protons + neutrons. Proton number \(Z\) = protons. Neutrons = \(A - Z\). E.1 introduced this notation.

Holding the protons in

The strong nuclear force.

  • Protons repel one another, yet nuclei exist
  • An attractive force acts between nucleons: proton or neutron alike
  • Short range: only between near neighbors
  • Much stronger than the electric force at that range
The curves are not required: know that the strong force is attractive and short range.
TWO PROTONSSKETCH, NOT TO SCALE
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

How big the problem is

Two protons 2 fm apart repel with \(\frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^{2}}{(2 \times 10^{-15})^{2}} = 58 \,\mathrm{N}\). That is the weight of a 6 kg mass, acting on a particle of mass \(1.7 \times 10^{-27} \,\mathrm{kg}\). Gravity between them is weaker by a factor of about \(10^{36}\). Something far stronger must hold them, and it must act on neutrons too, since neutrons stay in the nucleus.

Why short range matters

A nucleon feels the strong force only from the nucleons touching it, but a proton feels the electric repulsion of every other proton in the nucleus. In a large nucleus the repulsion adds up while the attraction does not. That is why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons, and why the very heaviest are unstable.

At very small separations, well under 1 fm, the strong force becomes repulsive. This stops the nucleus collapsing and is why all nuclei have about the same density (E.1, HL).

Mass and energy

Mass defect.

\(E = mc^{2}\)
  • A nucleus has less mass than its separate nucleons
  • The difference is the mass defect
  • The missing mass was released as energy when the nucleus formed
MAKING HELIUM-4

What \(E = mc^{2}\) says

Mass and energy are two measures of the same thing, linked by the factor \(c^{2}\). When a system gives out energy \(E\), its mass falls by \(\frac{E}{c^{2}}\). This happens in a chemical reaction too, but there the change is about one part in \(10^{10}\) and cannot be measured. In nuclear changes it is nearly one percent, and it can.

Three units of mass

Nuclear masses are given in kilograms, in unified atomic mass units (u), or in \(\mathrm{MeV} \,c^{-2}\). The last one makes the arithmetic disappear: a mass of \(1 \,\mathrm{u}\) is equivalent to \(931.5 \,\mathrm{MeV}\) of energy, so \(1 \,\mathrm{u} = 931.5 \,\mathrm{MeV} \,c^{-2}\). A proton has a mass of \(938 \,\mathrm{MeV} \,c^{-2}\).

\(1 \,\mathrm{u} = 1.661 \times 10^{-27} \,\mathrm{kg}\) is one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. All of these values are in the data booklet.

How tightly a nucleus is held

Nuclear binding energy.

  • The energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its nucleons
  • Equal to the energy released when the nucleus forms
  • Binding energy = mass defect × \(c^{2}\)
Divide by \(A\) for the binding energy per nucleon: a measure of stability.
Helium-4 nucleus: \(4.001506 \,\mathrm{u}\) Proton \(1.007276 \,\mathrm{u}\) · neutron \(1.008665 \,\mathrm{u}\) \(\Delta m = m_\mathrm{He} - (2 m_\mathrm{p} + 2 m_\mathrm{n})\) \(\Delta m = 4.001506 - 4.031882 = -0.030376 \,\mathrm{u}\) \(E_\mathrm{b} = -\Delta m\,c^{2} = -(-0.030376) \times 931.5 = \class{res}{28.3 \,\mathrm{MeV}}\) Per nucleon: \(\dfrac{28.3}{4} = \class{res}{7.07 \,\mathrm{MeV}}\)
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

Binding energy is not energy the nucleus has

For formation from separate nucleons, \(\Delta m = m_\mathrm{final} - m_\mathrm{initial} < 0\). The mass defect is the positive magnitude \(-\Delta m\), and the positive binding energy is \(E_\mathrm{b} = -\Delta m c^{2}\). It is energy the nucleus has lost: the amount you would have to put back in to pull every nucleon free. A nucleus with a large binding energy sits deep in an energy well and is hard to break up. Think of it as a debt, not as savings.

Why divide by the number of nucleons

A big nucleus has a large total binding energy simply because it has many nucleons. To compare how firmly each nucleon is held in different nuclei, divide by \(A\). The higher the binding energy per nucleon, the more stable the nucleus.

Rounding the masses early. The mass defect is a small difference between large numbers: keep every digit you are given until the subtraction is done.

E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

Fusion and fission on one graph

Joining two light nuclei gives a product further to the right and higher up: each nucleon is more tightly bound than before, so energy is released. That is fusion, the source of the Sun’s energy. Splitting a very heavy nucleus gives two products further to the left, also higher up: fission. Both move towards iron, from opposite ends.

How much energy

The energy released is the total binding energy of the products minus that of what you started with. When uranium-235 (7.6 MeV per nucleon) splits into fragments at about 8.5 MeV per nucleon, each of the 235 nucleons gains about 0.9 MeV: roughly 200 MeV for one fission, millions of times more than any chemical reaction between two atoms.

The spike at helium-4

Helium-4 is far more tightly bound than its neighbors. This is why alpha particles, and not single protons or neutrons, are what heavy nuclei throw out.

Saying iron has the most binding energy. It has the most binding energy per nucleon. Uranium has a far larger total.

Worked example

The energy released when radium-226 decays.

\({}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra}\) → \({}^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn}\) + \(\class{alpha}{{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}}\)
Masses: \(226.025410 \,\mathrm{u}\) → \(222.017578 \,\mathrm{u}\) + \(4.002603 \,\mathrm{u}\) \(\Delta m = m_\mathrm{after} - m_\mathrm{before}\) \(\Delta m = 226.020181 - 226.025410 = -0.005229 \,\mathrm{u}\) \(E_\mathrm{released} = -\Delta m\,c^{2} = -(-0.005229) \times 931.5 = \class{res}{4.87 \,\mathrm{MeV}}\)
  • The products have less mass: energy is released
  • It appears as kinetic energy, mostly of the alpha particle
  • Released energy: \(7.8 \times 10^{-13} \,\mathrm{J}\)
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

Why the alpha particle takes most of the energy

The radium nucleus is at rest, so the total momentum afterwards must be zero: the alpha particle and the radon nucleus fly apart with equal and opposite momenta (A.2). Kinetic energy is \(\frac{p^{2}}{2m}\), so for the same momentum the lighter particle has more of it. The alpha particle, with 4 of the 226 mass units, carries \(\frac{222}{226}\) of the energy: 4.78 MeV.

A decay can only happen if mass is lost

If the products would have more mass than the original nucleus, the decay cannot happen by itself, because it would need energy from nowhere. This test tells you which decays are possible. The rest-energy change is \(\Delta E_\mathrm{rest} = \Delta m c^{2} < 0\); the products gain kinetic energy, so \(\Delta E_\mathrm{rest} + \Delta E_\mathrm{k} = 0\).

These are masses of whole atoms, electrons included. The 88 electrons on the left balance the 86 + 2 on the right, so they cancel.

Spontaneous

Impossible to influence

Random for one nucleus, yet very predictable for a large number.
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

How something random can be predictable

Nobody can say whether a single coin will land heads. Toss a million coins and you can be sure that very close to half a million will. A sample of radioactive material holds an enormous number of nuclei, so the fraction that decays in a given time is extremely steady, even though each decay is a matter of chance. That steady fraction is what half-life measures.

Why chemistry cannot touch it

Heating, squeezing or reacting a substance changes how its electrons and atoms behave. The energies involved are a few electronvolts. Changes inside a nucleus involve millions of electronvolts, so the nucleus does not notice what the electrons are doing.

Evidence for randomness: the count rate from a steady source is never perfectly steady. It fluctuates from one second to the next.

Why heavy nuclei do this

A nucleus with more than 82 protons is too large for the short-range strong force to hold against the long-range repulsion of all its protons. Throwing out two protons and two neutrons together reduces the charge and moves the nucleus up the binding energy curve. The helium-4 group is so tightly bound itself that emitting it as one piece releases far more energy than emitting four separate nucleons would.

One energy

All the alpha particles from a given decay come out with the same kinetic energy, typically 4 to 9 MeV. That sharp energy is evidence that nuclei have discrete energy levels, which the HL part returns to.

In any decay equation the top numbers balance and the bottom numbers balance: \(241 = 237 + 4\) and \(95 = 93 + 2\).

The electron was not there before

There are no electrons inside a nucleus. The beta particle is created at the instant of the decay, when one of the neutrons turns into a proton, and it leaves at once. It is an ordinary electron, but it comes from the nucleus, not from the electron shells of the atom.

Why the bottom number is −1

The electron is written \({}^{0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}\): nucleon number 0, because it is not a nucleon, and “proton number” −1, standing for its charge. Then both rows balance: \(14 = 14 + 0\) and \(6 = 7 - 1\). Charge is conserved: one positive proton and one negative electron appear together.

Saying the nucleus loses a neutron and therefore has a smaller nucleon number. It has one neutron fewer and one proton more: the total is unchanged.

The positron

A positron is the antiparticle of the electron: the same mass, the opposite charge. It does not last long. Within a fraction of a millimeter in a solid it meets an electron and the two annihilate, their mass turning into two gamma photons that travel in opposite directions.

Where this is used

Fluorine-18 is the isotope used in PET scans. It is attached to a sugar that collects in active tissue. Detectors on opposite sides of the patient pick up the pairs of gamma photons, and a computer works back to where each pair came from.

Only inside a nucleus

A free proton never decays like this, because a neutron has more mass than a proton. Inside a nucleus the energy can come from the rest of the nucleus becoming more tightly bound.

Minus goes with antineutrino, plus with neutrino. In each decay one particle and one antiparticle are created.

HL only How anyone knows it is there

When a nucleus at rest emits a single particle, the two must fly apart back to back and share the energy in a fixed way. In beta decay the electron and the recoiling nucleus are often seen not moving back to back, and the electron’s energy varies from one decay to the next. Something unseen must be taking the missing momentum and energy.

How many there are

The Sun’s fusion reactions make neutrinos in vast numbers. About \(6 \times 10^{10}\) of them pass through every square centimeter of you each second, day and night, since the Earth does not stop them. Almost none interact.

The force responsible for beta decay, the weak nuclear force, is not part of this course. You need the neutrino’s existence and its place in the equations.

The same idea as atomic spectra

In E.1 an electron dropping to a lower level in an atom emits a photon whose energy equals the difference between the levels. A nucleus also has energy levels. After an alpha or beta decay the new nucleus is often left in an excited level, and it drops to its ground state by emitting a photon. The difference is the size of the energy: a few eV for an atom, hundreds of keV or several MeV for a nucleus.

Photons from a nucleus have much shorter wavelengths

A 1.0 MeV gamma photon has \(\lambda = \frac{hc}{E} = \frac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{1.0 \times 10^{6}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-12} \,\mathrm{m}\), about half a million times shorter than visible light.

The “m” in technetium-99m means metastable: an excited state that lasts unusually long, hours in this case and not a tiny fraction of a second.

Two conservation laws do all the work

The top numbers must add up to the same total on both sides: nucleons are neither created nor destroyed. The bottom numbers must add up too: that is conservation of charge. Neutrinos and photons have zero for both, so they never affect the balance.

Working out an unknown

Polonium-210 (\(Z = 84\)) decays by alpha emission. The new nucleus has \(A = 210 - 4 = 206\) and \(Z = 84 - 2 = 82\). Look up \(Z = 82\): lead. So the product is lead-206. Always find the element from the proton number.

The star on nickel-60 marks an excited state. It was left that way by the beta decay of cobalt-60, which is why cobalt-60 is used as a gamma source.

Leaving out the neutrino or antineutrino. It is needed for full marks in a beta decay equation.

E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

Ionizing and penetrating are opposites

Radiation loses energy each time it knocks an electron out of an atom. An alpha particle is slow, heavy and has charge \(+2e\): it ionizes thousands of atoms in every millimeter of air, so its energy is used up within a few centimeters. A beta particle is fast and light with charge \(e\), ionizes far less often, and travels meters. A gamma photon has no charge and ionizes only when it happens to hit an electron, so it can pass through centimeters of lead.

Gamma radiation is never completely stopped

Each centimeter of lead removes the same fraction of the photons. The intensity keeps falling, but it never reaches exactly zero, which is why the diagram says “reduced”.

The danger follows from this. Outside the body, gamma is the most hazardous because it gets in. Inside the body, alpha is the worst, because all its energy is delivered to a tiny volume of tissue.

Telling them apart in the laboratory

Put absorbers between a source and a detector. If a sheet of paper cuts the count rate sharply, alpha is present. If paper makes little difference but a few millimeters of aluminum cuts it, beta. If the count rate is still well above background behind aluminum, gamma.

Using a field

In a magnetic field alpha and beta-minus particles curve in opposite directions, because their charges have opposite signs, and gamma goes straight on. The beta particles curve much more sharply than the alpha particles: they have a far smaller mass, so much less momentum for the same sort of energy (D.3).

E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · SL + HL

Why the count rate is smaller than the activity

A source sends radiation out in all directions and the detector covers only a small part of the sphere round it. Some radiation is absorbed by the air or by the source itself on the way, and some passes through the detector without being recorded. A source of activity 40 000 Bq might give a count rate of a few hundred counts per second.

Why that does not matter for half-life

If nothing in the arrangement is moved, the detector always catches the same fraction. When the activity halves, the count rate halves. So the half-life can be found from the count rate without ever knowing the activity itself.

Activity is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei because each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in each second: twice as many nuclei, twice as many decays.

The time to halve

Half-life.

  • The time for half the undecayed nuclei in a sample to decay
  • Also the time for the activity, or the count rate, to halve
  • Always the same for a given nuclide, whenever you start
After \(n\) half-lives: \(\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\) remains.

Why it halves and does not fall steadily

Each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in the next second, however long it has already survived. With half as many nuclei left, half as many decay each second. So the sample loses the same fraction in equal times, not the same number: a half in the first 8 days, a half of what is left in the next 8 days.

It never quite reaches zero

After 10 half-lives about one thousandth remains, \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{10} = \frac{1}{1024}\). After 20, about one millionth. Half-lives range from a tiny fraction of a second to billions of years: 4.5 billion years for uranium-238, about the age of the Earth.

Thinking everything has decayed after two half-lives. After two, a quarter remains.

What the grids show

With only 64 nuclei, chance matters: 33 survive the first half-life where 32 were expected, and 15 survive the second where 16 or 17 were expected. Run it again and the numbers would be slightly different, with different nuclei surviving.

Why real samples behave so regularly

The scatter is roughly the square root of the number expected. For 32 it is about 6, nearly 20%. For a real sample with \(10^{20}\) nuclei it is about \(10^{10}\), one part in ten billion. The randomness is still there, but it is far too small to see.

A good classroom model: throw 100 dice, remove every six, and repeat. The number left falls with a “half-life” of just under four throws.

Why measure the background for a long time

The background count rate is low and random, so a short measurement is unreliable: 20 counts in one minute could easily have been 15 or 25. Counting for several minutes and dividing gives a much steadier average.

When it matters most

For a strong source the background is a small correction. But as the source decays, its count rate falls towards the background, and in the end most of what the detector records has nothing to do with the source. Without the correction, the count rate would seem to level off above zero and the half-life would come out too long.

Measure the background with the source well away, in the same place and with the same detector, before or after the experiment.

The corrected count rate starts at 480 counts per minute. It reaches 240 after 2.0 hours and 120 after 4.0 hours: two readings of the half-life, both 2.0 hours. Starting from 400 and reading the time to reach 200 would give a third. Agreement between them is the check that the decay really is exponential.

What goes wrong without the correction

The measured curve starts at 510 and would need to fall to 255. That takes about 2.2 hours, and the next halving takes longer still. A “half-life” that keeps growing is the sign that the background has not been removed.

Draw the curve through the scatter of the points, not from point to point. Each reading is uncertain because decay is random.

Two questions decide every choice

First, penetration: what does the radiation have to get through, and what should it not get through? Alpha would never leave the body or the pipe. Gamma would pass through a sheet of paper as if it were not there, so it could not measure its thickness. Second, half-life: long enough to do the job, short enough not to remain a hazard afterwards.

How carbon dating works

Cosmic rays keep making carbon-14 in the atmosphere, and living things take it in, so while they are alive they contain a steady, known proportion. At death the intake stops and the carbon-14 decays. The fraction left tells you how long ago that was. After about ten half-lives too little is left to measure.

A sample has a quarter of the carbon-14 of living wood. A quarter is two half-lives: \(2 \times 5730 = 11\,460\) years old.

E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · ADDITIONAL HL

Why constant binding energy means short range

Suppose every nucleon attracted every other nucleon, as every mass attracts every other mass. The number of pairs among \(A\) nucleons grows as \(A^{2}\), so the binding energy per nucleon would grow in proportion to \(A\). It does not: above \(A = 60\) it stays close to 8 MeV. So each nucleon must be bound only to the few nucleons next to it. Adding more nucleons to the far side of the nucleus makes no difference to it. The force is said to saturate.

Why the curve still droops

The electric repulsion is long range: every proton does repel every other proton. That energy grows faster than the number of nucleons and slowly lowers the binding energy per nucleon for heavy nuclei, from 8.8 MeV at iron to 7.6 MeV at uranium.

A liquid behaves the same way: each molecule is held only by its neighbors, so the energy needed to boil off a gram is the same whatever the size of the drop.

The ratio of neutrons to protons

Neutrons and stability.

  • Light stable nuclei: \(N \approx Z\)
  • Heavy stable nuclei need more neutrons: about 1.5 for each proton
  • Off the band: beta decay moves a nucleus back towards it
  • Beyond lead, \(Z = 82\): no stable nuclides
THE BAND OF STABILITY
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · ADDITIONAL HL

What the neutrons do

A neutron adds strong attraction without adding any electric repulsion. It also spaces the protons further apart. In a small nucleus equal numbers are enough. As \(Z\) grows, the total repulsion grows quickly, and more and more neutrons are needed to hold the protons in: lead-208 has 82 protons and 126 neutrons.

How decay follows the chart

A nucleus above the band has too many neutrons. Beta-minus decay turns one neutron into a proton: one step down and one step right, towards the band. A nucleus below the band has too few. Beta-plus decay turns a proton into a neutron: one step up and one left. Alpha decay removes two of each, moving a heavy nucleus diagonally down the chart.

Carbon-14 has \(N = 8\), \(Z = 6\): above the band, so beta-minus. Fluorine-18 has \(N = 9\), \(Z = 9\): below the band for its size, so beta-plus.

Evidence from alpha and gamma spectra

Discrete nuclear energy levels.

  • Alpha particles from one nuclide have a few sharp energies
  • Gamma photons have sharp energies too
  • The differences between the alpha energies match the gamma energies
A nucleus, like an atom, has discrete energy levels.
ONE PARENT, TWO ROUTES
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · ADDITIONAL HL

The argument

The parent nucleus starts with a fixed energy. If the daughter could be left with any energy at all, the alpha particles would come out with a continuous spread. They do not: their energies are a short list of exact values. So the daughter can be left only in certain states: its ground state, or one of a few excited states, each with a definite energy.

The check

A daughter left in an excited state then drops to the ground state and emits a gamma photon. Its energy should equal the difference between the two alpha routes, apart from the small recoil of the nucleus. Measurements agree: most alpha particles from americium-241 have about 60 keV less than the most energetic ones, and the decay is followed by a 59.5 keV gamma photon.

Compare E.1: line spectra are the evidence for discrete energy levels in atoms. Nuclear levels are about a million times further apart.

A spectrum that should not be continuous

The beta spectrum and the neutrino.

  • Beta particles have a continuous range of energies, up to a maximum
  • Yet the nucleus changes between two fixed levels
  • The rest of the energy goes to a third particle: the neutrino
\(E_\mathrm{max}\) is the full energy of the decay.
BETA PARTICLES FROM ONE NUCLIDE
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · ADDITIONAL HL

Why two particles would give one energy

If the decay produced only the daughter nucleus and an electron, conservation of momentum and energy together would fix how the energy is shared, as in alpha decay. Every electron would come out with the same energy, close to the maximum. The measured spread means that energy and momentum are being shared among three bodies, and three bodies can share them in endless ways.

Reading the graph

An electron with nearly \(E_\mathrm{max}\) left almost nothing for the neutrino. An electron with very little energy came from a decay in which the neutrino took nearly all of it. In every decay the electron and the neutrino together carry \(E_\mathrm{max}\).

Contrast the three spectra: alpha and gamma are discrete, evidence for nuclear energy levels. Beta is continuous, evidence for the neutrino.

The chance of decay

The decay constant and the decay law.

\(N = N_{0} e^{-\lambda t}\)
  • \(\lambda\): the decay constant, in s⁻¹
  • The probability that a nucleus decays in unit time
  • \(N\): undecayed nuclei left after time \(t\)

Where it comes from

Decays per second \(\propto\) nuclei left

A quantity that falls at a rate proportional to itself falls exponentially

A limit on “probability”

Only when \(\lambda t\) is small

\(\lambda \times t\) is the chance of decay in time \(t\) only if it is much less than 1

E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · ADDITIONAL HL

What \(\lambda\) means

If \(\lambda = 0.010 \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\), each nucleus has a 1% chance of decaying in the next second, and in a large sample 1% of the nuclei do decay in that second. A large \(\lambda\) means a short-lived nuclide.

Why only for small \(\lambda t\)

Take iodine-131, with \(\lambda = 0.0864\) per day. Over one day, \(\lambda t = 0.086\) and the true fraction that decays is \(1 - e^{-0.0864} = 0.083\): close. Over 20 days, \(\lambda t = 1.73\), which cannot be a probability at all, since it is more than 1. The true fraction decayed is \(1 - e^{-1.73} = 0.82\). The simple product works only while the number of nuclei has hardly changed during the interval.

\(\lambda\) and \(t\) must use the same unit of time: per second with seconds, per day with days. Their product has no unit.

Three linked equations

Activity and half-life.

\(A = \lambda N = \lambda N_{0} e^{-\lambda t}\)
  • Activity is the rate of decay
  • It falls with the same exponential as \(N\): \(A = A_{0} e^{-\lambda t}\)
At \(t = T_{\frac{1}{2}}\), half the nuclei are left \(\dfrac{1}{2} N_{0} = N_{0} e^{-\lambda T_{\frac{1}{2}}}\) \(\ln 2 = \lambda T_{\frac{1}{2}}\) \(T_{\frac{1}{2}} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\)
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · ADDITIONAL HL

Why \(A = \lambda N\)

Each of the \(N\) nuclei has a probability \(\lambda\) of decaying in each second, so the number of decays per second is \(\lambda N\). This one line connects something you can measure with a detector, the activity, to something you cannot count directly, the number of nuclei.

The half-life step

Cancel \(N_{0}\) to get \(\frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T}\). Take natural logarithms: \(\ln \frac{1}{2} = -\lambda T\). Since \(\ln \frac{1}{2} = -\ln 2\), the minus signs cancel. \(\ln 2 = 0.693\), so a quick form is \(T_{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}\).

A long half-life means a small decay constant and, for the same number of nuclei, a low activity. Uranium-238 is only weakly radioactive because its half-life is so long.

Worked example

Iodine-131 after 20 days.

Half-life 8.02 days · initial activity 400 kBq \(\lambda = \dfrac{\ln 2}{T_{\frac{1}{2}}} = \dfrac{0.693}{8.02} = 0.0864 \text{ day}^{-1}\) \(A = A_{0} e^{-\lambda t}\) \(A = 400 \times e^{-0.0864 \times 20} = 400 \times 0.178\) \(A = \class{res}{71 \,\mathrm{kBq}}\)
  • 20 days is 2.5 half-lives: not a whole number
  • Check: between \(\frac{1}{4}\) (100 kBq) and \(\frac{1}{8}\) (50 kBq)
  • Time in days, so \(\lambda\) in day⁻¹
E.3 RADIOACTIVE DECAY · ADDITIONAL HL

Another route to the same answer

\(A = A_{0} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\) works for any \(n\), not only whole numbers. Here \(n = \frac{20}{8.02} = 2.49\), and \(400 \times 0.5^{2.49} = 71 \,\mathrm{kBq}\). The two forms are the same equation written in two ways.

Finding the time instead

How long until the activity is 25 kBq? Rearrange: \(e^{-\lambda t} = \frac{25}{400}\), so \(\lambda t = \ln \frac{400}{25} = 2.77\) and \(t = \frac{2.77}{0.0864} = 32\) days. Here that is exactly four half-lives, a good check.

Forgetting the minus sign in the exponent. \(e^{+1.73}\) is 5.6, which would make the activity grow.

Worked example

The activity of 1.0 μg of cobalt-60.

Half-life 5.27 years \(= 1.66 \times 10^{8} \,\mathrm{s}\) \(\lambda = \dfrac{\ln 2}{T_{\frac{1}{2}}} = \dfrac{0.693}{1.66 \times 10^{8}} = 4.17 \times 10^{-9} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) Mass of one atom ≈ \(60 \,\mathrm{u}\) \(N = \dfrac{m}{m_\mathrm{atom}} = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-9}}{60 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27}} = 1.00 \times 10^{16}\) \(A = \lambda N = 4.17 \times 10^{-9} \times 1.00 \times 10^{16}\) \(A = \class{res}{4.2 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{Bq}}\)
  • For becquerels, \(\lambda\) must be in s⁻¹
  • \(N\) from the mass and the mass of one atom
  • A millionth of a gram: forty million decays every second

Measuring a very long half-life

This calculation can be run backwards. Nobody can watch uranium-238 for 4.5 billion years. But you can weigh a sample, work out \(N\), measure its activity \(A\) with a detector, and find \(\lambda = \frac{A}{N}\). The half-life follows from \(\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\). Long half-lives are measured this way, without waiting for any noticeable change.

Using moles

The number of atoms can also be found as \(\frac{\text{mass}}{\text{molar mass}} \times N_\mathrm{A}\): \(\frac{1.0 \times 10^{-6} \,\mathrm{g}}{60 \,\mathrm{g} \,\mathrm{mol}^{-1}} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.0 \times 10^{16}\). Both routes give the same number.

One year is \(3.16 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{s}\). It is worth remembering for this topic and for D.1.

Finding \(\lambda\) from data

A straight-line graph for decay.

\(A = A_{0} e^{-\lambda t}\) \(\ln A = \ln A_{0} - \lambda t\)
  • Plot \(\ln A\) against \(t\): a straight line
  • Gradient \(= -\lambda\)
  • Then \(T_{\frac{1}{2}} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\)

Why bother

Judging a half-life from a curve depends on how well the curve has been drawn through scattered points. A straight line is much easier to judge by eye, and its gradient uses every reading at once. If the points do not lie on a straight line, the decay is not a single exponential: perhaps the background was not subtracted, or there are two nuclides in the sample.

A case with numbers

If \(\ln A\) falls from 6.0 to 3.0 while \(t\) goes from 0 to 25 minutes, the gradient is \(\frac{3.0 - 6.0}{25} = -0.12 \,\mathrm{min}^{-1}\). So \(\lambda = 0.12 \,\mathrm{min}^{-1}\) and \(T_{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{0.693}{0.12} = 5.8\) minutes.

Radioactive dating at higher level

A piece of wood has 35% of the carbon-14 activity of living wood. \(\lambda = \frac{0.693}{5730} = 1.21 \times 10^{-4} \,\mathrm{year}^{-1}\), and \(t = \frac{\ln \left(\frac{1}{0.35}\right)}{\lambda} = \frac{1.05}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}} = 8700\) years.

The corrected count rate can be used in place of the activity. It only shifts the line up or down, and the gradient is the same.
