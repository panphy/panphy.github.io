Part 01 · SL + HL

Describing motion.

Where is it, how fast, and how is that changing?

A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Where is it?

Distance and displacement.

  • Position: where a body is, measured from a chosen origin
  • Distance: the length of the path traveled. A scalar
  • Displacement: the change in position. A vector, with a direction
ONE JOURNEY, TWO MEASURES
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

A lap of a running track

One lap is a distance of 400 m. The runner ends where they began, so the displacement is zero. Half a lap is 200 m of distance, but the displacement is only the straight line across the track, which is much shorter.

Direction in a straight line

Along one line there are only two directions, so a plus or minus sign carries the direction. Choose which way is positive at the start of a problem and keep to it: a displacement of \(-3 \,\mathrm{m}\) then means 3 m the other way.

Distance can only grow as a journey goes on. Displacement can grow, shrink, pass through zero and become negative.

How fast?

Speed and velocity.

  • Speed: rate of change of distance. A scalar
  • Velocity: rate of change of displacement. A vector
\(v = \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\)
One 400 m lap in 50 s Average speed \(= \dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \dfrac{400}{50} = \class{res}{8.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\) Displacement \(= 0\) Average velocity \(= \dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{0}{50} = \class{res}{0}\)
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Velocity can change while speed does not

A car rounding a bend at a steady 20 m s⁻¹ keeps the same speed, but its direction is changing, so its velocity is changing. That is why circular motion in A.2 counts as accelerated motion.

A negative velocity is not a low velocity

With east as positive, \(-15 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) is 15 m s⁻¹ towards the west. The sign is the direction; the size is the speed.

\(\Delta\) means “change in”: the final value minus the initial value. \(\Delta s\) is a displacement, not a position.

Over a journey, or at a moment?

Average and instantaneous.

  • Average velocity: the whole displacement divided by the whole time
  • Instantaneous velocity: the velocity at one moment
  • On a displacement–time graph: a chord gives the average, a tangent the instantaneous
DISPLACEMENT AGAINST TIME
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

How the two are connected

Take the average over a shorter and shorter time around one moment. The two ends of the chord close in on each other and the chord swings round until it lies along the tangent. Instantaneous velocity is the average velocity over a time interval too short to matter.

In a car

The speedometer shows instantaneous speed. Distance for the trip divided by the time it took is the average speed. A journey at an average of 60 km h⁻¹ can include stops and stretches at 100 km h⁻¹.

To draw a tangent, lay the ruler so that it touches the curve at the point with equal small gaps either side. Make the triangle for its gradient as large as the graph allows.

A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

How is the velocity changing?

Acceleration.

\(a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{v - u}{t}\)
  • The rate of change of velocity. A vector
  • \(u\): initial velocity, \(v\): final velocity
  • Uniform acceleration: the same at every moment
A car goes from 12 to 30 m s⁻¹ in 6.0 s \(a = \dfrac{v - u}{t} = \dfrac{30 - 12}{6.0} = \class{res}{3.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\) Then brakes from 30 to 10 m s⁻¹ in 4.0 s \(a = \dfrac{v - u}{t} = \dfrac{10 - 30}{4.0} = \class{res}{-5.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\)
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

What the unit says

\(3.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\) means the velocity rises by 3.0 m s⁻¹ in every second: 12, 15, 18, 21 and so on.

What the sign says

The sign of an acceleration is its direction, not whether the body is speeding up. Compare it with the sign of the velocity. Same sign: the speed increases. Opposite signs: the speed decreases. A ball thrown upwards has a downward acceleration the whole time: it slows on the way up and speeds up on the way down.

Reading every negative acceleration as “slowing down”. A body moving in the negative direction with a negative acceleration is getting faster.

Part 02 · SL + HL

Motion graphs.

The gradient and the area tell the whole story

A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Graph 1

Displacement–time graphs.

  • Gradient = velocity
  • Straight line: constant velocity
  • Flat: at rest
  • Curve: the velocity is changing
DISPLACEMENT AGAINST TIME
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Why the gradient is the velocity

A gradient is rise over run. Here the rise is a change in displacement and the run is the time it took, and \(\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\) is the definition of velocity. Its unit confirms it: meters divided by seconds.

A falling line

The gradient is negative, so the velocity is negative: the body is moving in the negative direction. While its displacement is still positive, that means heading back towards the origin. Where the line crosses the time axis the body passes its starting point.

Treating the graph as a picture of the path. A line that rises and falls can describe a body that only ever moves along one straight road.

Graph 2

Velocity–time graphs.

  • Gradient = acceleration
  • Area under the graph = displacement
  • Flat: constant velocity, not at rest
  • Below the time axis: moving the other way
VELOCITY AGAINST TIME
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Why the area is the displacement

At a constant velocity, displacement is velocity × time, which is the height × width of a rectangle under the line. When the velocity changes, cut the time into thin strips. In each strip the velocity hardly changes, so each strip’s area is the displacement in that short time. Add the strips and you have the whole area.

Area below the axis

It counts as negative displacement: the body is coming back. Add areas with their signs for the displacement. Add them all as positive for the distance traveled.

Reading a flat line as “stopped”. On a velocity–time graph only the time axis itself means at rest.

Linking the graphs

Three graphs, one motion.

UNIFORM ACCELERATION FROM REST
Area under an acceleration–time graph = the change in velocity.
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Reading left to right

The displacement curve gets steeper at a steady rate, so its gradient, the velocity, is a straight rising line. That line has one fixed gradient, so the acceleration is a horizontal line.

Reading right to left

An area gives only a change. The area under the acceleration graph tells you how much the velocity changed, not what it is: you also need the velocity at the start. In the same way, the area under the velocity graph gives the displacement from wherever the body started.

Asked to sketch one graph from another, mark the special moments first: where the gradient is zero, the next graph crosses its axis.

When the acceleration changes

Non-uniform acceleration.

  • The velocity–time graph is a curve
  • Acceleration at one moment: gradient of the tangent
  • Displacement: still the area. Count squares
The equations of motion in part 3 do not apply.
VELOCITY AGAINST TIME
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Counting squares properly

First find what one square is worth: its height in m s⁻¹ times its width in s. A square 2 m s⁻¹ high and 0.5 s wide is worth 1 m. Count the whole squares, then pair up part squares, counting those more than half full and ignoring the rest. Multiply the count by the value of one square.

Where this happens

A falling object with air resistance, a car pulling away, a sprinter leaving the blocks: in each the acceleration is large at first and fades. Part 4 returns to the falling object.

A quick check on a square count: the area must lie between the largest triangle that fits under the curve and the rectangle that encloses it.

Worked example

Reading a journey.

First 4 s: gradient \(a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{12 - 0}{4.0} = \class{res}{3.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\) Last 3 s: gradient \(a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{0 - 12}{3.0} = \class{res}{-4.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\) Whole journey: area \(s = \text{area} = 24 + 72 + 18 = \class{res}{114 \,\mathrm{m}}\) Average speed \(= \dfrac{s}{t} = \dfrac{114}{13} = \class{res}{8.8 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
A SHORT CAR JOURNEY
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

The three areas

Triangle: \(\frac{1}{2} \times 4.0 \times 12 = 24 \,\mathrm{m}\). Rectangle: \(6.0 \times 12 = 72 \,\mathrm{m}\). Triangle: \(\frac{1}{2} \times 3.0 \times 12 = 18 \,\mathrm{m}\).

A faster route

The whole shape is a trapezium with parallel sides 13 s and 6 s, and height 12 m s⁻¹: \(\frac{1}{2}(13 + 6) \times 12 = 114 \,\mathrm{m}\).

Taking the average speed as half of 12 m s⁻¹. That works only when the velocity changes steadily for the whole time. Here the car spends 6 s at full speed, so the average is higher.

Four equations for uniform acceleration

A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

From the velocity–time graph

Where the equations come from.

Gradient = acceleration \(a = \dfrac{v - u}{t}\)  so  \(\class{res}{v = u + at}\) Area = displacement: rectangle + triangle \(\class{res}{s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}}\) Area = displacement: a trapezium \(\class{res}{s = \dfrac{u + v}{2}t}\)
UNIFORM ACCELERATION FROM u TO v
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

The triangle

Its base is \(t\) and its height is the gain in velocity, \(v - u\), which is \(at\). Half base times height gives \(\frac{1}{2} \times t \times at = \frac{1}{2}at^{2}\).

The fourth equation

It has no \(t\) in it. From the first, \(t = \frac{v - u}{a}\). Put that into \(s = \frac{u + v}{2}t\): \(s = \frac{(v + u)(v - u)}{2a} = \frac{v^{2} - u^{2}}{2a}\), which rearranges to \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\).

What the trapezium says

\(\frac{u + v}{2}\) is the average velocity when the velocity changes steadily. Displacement is average velocity × time.

You do not have to derive these in an examination: all four are in the data booklet. Knowing where they come from tells you when they can be used.

Uniform acceleration in a straight line

The four equations.

EquationLeaves outUse it when…
\(s = \dfrac{u + v}{2}t\)\(a\)the acceleration is not given or wanted
\(v = u + at\)\(s\)the displacement is not given or wanted
\(s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}\)\(v\)the final velocity is not given or wanted
\(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\)\(t\)the time is not given or wanted
Five quantities: \(s\), \(u\), \(v\), \(a\), \(t\). Know three, and you can find the other two.
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Why three are enough

Each equation links four of the five quantities. With three known, the equation that contains those three and the one you want has a single unknown.

The condition

Every one of these was built on a straight velocity–time line. If the acceleration changes during the motion, split the motion into stages that each have a constant acceleration and treat them one at a time. The final velocity of one stage is the initial velocity of the next.

\(s\) is displacement, not distance. If a body turns round during the motion, \(s\) is still measured from the start in a straight line.

The data booklet prints the last equation as \(v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as\). It is the same equation, with \(u^{2}\) moved to the other side.

A method that always works

Solving a problem.

A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Values hidden in the wording

“From rest” and “dropped” mean \(u = 0\). “Comes to rest” and “stops” mean \(v = 0\). “At its highest point” means the vertical velocity is zero. “Returns to where it started” means \(s = 0\). A body moving freely under gravity has \(a = 9.8 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\) downward.

Why choose a direction first

Displacement, velocity and acceleration are all vectors. If up is positive, a ball on its way down has a negative velocity and an acceleration of \(-9.8 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\). Most wrong answers come from a sign that was never decided.

Worked example

A car brakes to a stop.

\(u = 30 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) · \(v = 0\) · \(a = -5.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\) Distance: no \(t\), so \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\) \(s = \dfrac{v^{2} - u^{2}}{2a} = \dfrac{0 - 30^{2}}{2(-5.0)} = \class{res}{90 \,\mathrm{m}}\) Time: \(v = u + at\) \(t = \dfrac{v - u}{a} = \dfrac{0 - 30}{-5.0} = \class{res}{6.0 \,\mathrm{s}}\)
  • Check: \(s = \dfrac{u + v}{2}t = 15 \times 6.0 = 90 \,\mathrm{m}\)
  • At twice the speed: four times the distance
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Why four times

With \(v = 0\), the equation gives \(s = \frac{u^{2}}{2 \times 5.0}\): the braking distance depends on the square of the starting speed. At 60 m s⁻¹ it is \(\frac{3600}{10} = 360 \,\mathrm{m}\). The time to stop only doubles, but the average speed during the stop doubles too.

The sign of the acceleration

The car moves in the positive direction and slows, so the acceleration is negative. Leaving the minus sign out gives a negative distance: a sign that something was set up wrongly.

This is braking distance only. The distance a car travels while the driver is still reacting is covered at constant speed and has to be added.

Motion under gravity alone

Free fall.

  • With no air resistance, every body falls with the same acceleration
  • Near the Earth’s surface: \(\class{res}{g = 9.8 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}}\), downward
  • It is the same on the way up, at the top and on the way down
FALLING FROM RESTEQUAL TIME STEPS
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Why 1, 4, 9, 16

From rest, \(s = \frac{1}{2}gt^{2}\): the distance fallen depends on the square of the time. After 1 s a body has fallen 4.9 m, after 2 s 19.6 m, after 3 s 44.1 m. Its velocity rises by 9.8 m s⁻¹ every second.

Mass does not matter

A heavier body is pulled harder but is also harder to accelerate, and the two effects cancel exactly. In air a feather falls slowly only because air resistance is large compared with its weight. The next slide shows the same test done where there is no air at all.

Use the data booklet value, 9.8 m s⁻². In this topic \(g\) is treated as constant: the heights involved are tiny compared with the radius of the Earth.

Worked example

A ball thrown straight up.

Up is positive \(u = +14.7 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) · \(a = -9.8 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\) Top: \(v = 0\), and \(v = u + at\) \(t = \dfrac{v - u}{a} = \dfrac{0 - 14.7}{-9.8} = \class{res}{1.5 \,\mathrm{s}}\) Height: \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\) \(s = \dfrac{v^{2} - u^{2}}{2a} = \dfrac{0 - 14.7^{2}}{2(-9.8)} = \class{res}{11 \,\mathrm{m}}\) Back at the hand: \(t = \class{res}{3.0 \,\mathrm{s}}\), \(v = \class{res}{-14.7 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
VELOCITY AGAINST TIME
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

The symmetry

The graph is one straight line. The area above the axis is the height gained and the area below is the height lost. Back at the hand the two are equal, so the triangles are the same size: the time down equals the time up, and the speed on return equals the launch speed.

Checking the return

Use \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^{2}\) with \(s = 0\): \(0 = 14.7t - 4.9t^{2}\), so \(t = 0\) (the launch) or \(t = \frac{14.7}{4.9} = 3.0 \,\mathrm{s}\).

Saying the acceleration is zero at the top. The velocity is zero for an instant, but it is still changing at 9.8 m s⁻² downward. If the acceleration were zero the ball would stay there.

Practical

Measuring \(g\).

  • Release a steel ball from rest
  • Time its fall through a measured height \(h\)
  • Repeat for a range of heights
  • \(h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^{2}\): plot \(h\) against \(t^{2}\)
A straight line through the origin, gradient \(\tfrac{1}{2}g\).
FREE-FALL TIMERNOT TO SCALE
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Why a graph and not one drop

A single height gives one value of \(g\) with no way of telling how good it is. A line through several points averages out the random scatter in the times, and the way the points sit about the line shows the quality of the data.

What a systematic error does

An electromagnet does not let go at once, so every time is too long by about the same amount. The points then no longer fall on a line through the origin. If instead \(h\) is measured to the wrong part of the ball, every height is wrong by the same amount: the line is shifted and has an intercept, but its gradient, and so \(g\), is unchanged.

The times are short, about 0.3 s for a 0.5 m drop, so they must be measured electronically. A hand-held stopwatch has a reaction-time error as large as the reading.

Part 04 · SL + HL

Projectile motion.

Two simple motions happening at once

A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

The key idea

Two independent motions.

  • Horizontal: no force, so constant velocity
  • Vertical: gravity, so acceleration \(g\)
  • Neither affects the other
The only thing they share is the time.
RELEASED AT THE SAME MOMENT
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Why they are independent

Gravity pulls straight down. It has no sideways part, so it cannot change the sideways velocity. And moving sideways does not change how hard gravity pulls. So the vertical motion of the launched ball is exactly that of the dropped ball.

A demonstration

Put one coin at the edge of a table and flick a second coin sideways off the table at the same moment as knocking the first straight off. Listen: they hit the floor together, however hard the flick.

This holds only while air resistance can be ignored, which is the case in every projectile calculation in this course.

Method

Keep two columns.

HorizontalVertical
AccelerationZero\(g = 9.8 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\), downward
VelocityStays at its starting valueChanges: \(v = u + at\)
Displacementvelocity × time\(s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2}\)
Find the time from one column, then use it in the other.
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Which column gives the time?

Usually the vertical one, because the flight ends when the body reaches the ground. The height it has to fall, or its vertical launch velocity, fixes how long it stays in the air. The horizontal column then turns that time into a distance.

Never mix the columns

A horizontal velocity does not belong in a vertical equation. The four equations of motion are used in the vertical column only, with vertical values of \(s\), \(u\), \(v\) and \(a\).

You are not expected to know or use an equation for the shape of the path.

Worked example

Launched horizontally from a cliff.

44.1 m high Launched horizontally at 12 m s⁻¹ Vertical: \(u = 0\), so \(s = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^{2}\) \(t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2s}{g}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 44.1}{9.8}} = \class{res}{3.0 \,\mathrm{s}}\) Horizontal: \(x = u_{x}t = 12 \times 3.0 = \class{res}{36 \,\mathrm{m}}\)
Velocity on landing Vertical: \(v_{y} = gt = 9.8 \times 3.0 = 29.4 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) \(v = \sqrt{v_{x}^{2} + v_{y}^{2}} = \sqrt{12^{2} + 29.4^{2}} = \class{res}{32 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\) \(\tan \theta = \dfrac{v_{y}}{v_{x}} = \dfrac{29.4}{12}\)  so  \(\class{res}{68^{\circ}}\) below horizontal
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Launched horizontally means the vertical velocity starts at zero

All of the 12 m s⁻¹ is sideways. So the time in the air is the same as for a ball simply dropped from 44.1 m. Launching faster does not keep it up any longer: it only lands further out.

Putting the velocity back together

The two components are at right angles, so the speed comes from Pythagoras and the direction from the tangent of the angle. The answer needs both: a velocity is a vector.

Adding the components: 12 + 29.4. Velocities at right angles combine by Pythagoras, never by simple addition.

Resolving the launch velocity

Launched at an angle.

  • Horizontal: \(u \cos \theta\). It never changes
  • Vertical: \(u \sin \theta\). It changes at \(g\)
  • At the top the vertical velocity is zero
  • Landing at launch height: the path is symmetrical
COMPONENTS OF THE LAUNCH VELOCITY
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Which is cos and which is sin?

The component next to the angle takes cos. With \(\theta\) measured from the horizontal, as here, the horizontal component is \(u \cos \theta\). If a question gives the angle to the vertical instead, they swap. Draw the triangle every time.

The body is not at rest at the top

It still has all of its horizontal velocity, \(u \cos \theta\). That is its smallest speed in the whole flight.

On level ground with no air resistance, 45° gives the greatest range, and two angles that add to 90°, such as 30° and 60°, give the same range.

Following the velocity

What changes, and what does not.

VELOCITY COMPONENTS AT EQUAL TIME STEPSNO AIR RESISTANCE
The acceleration is the same at every point: \(g\), straight down.
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

Reading the arrows

The vertical arrow loses the same length in each time step, because the vertical velocity changes by 9.8 m s⁻¹ every second all the way round: positive, through zero, to negative. The horizontal arrow is untouched.

The velocity is along the path

At each point the two components add to give a velocity that is a tangent to the curve. The acceleration is not along the path: it points straight down everywhere, which is why the path bends.

At the same height on the way up and on the way down, the speed is the same. Only the direction of the vertical component has reversed.

Worked example

20 m s⁻¹ at 30° above the horizontal.

Components Horizontal: \(u \cos \theta = 20 \cos 30^{\circ} = \class{res}{17.3 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\) Vertical: \(u \sin \theta = 20 \sin 30^{\circ} = \class{res}{10.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\) Time to the top: vertical \(v = 0\) \(t = \dfrac{u_{y}}{g} = \dfrac{10.0}{9.8} = \class{res}{1.02 \,\mathrm{s}}\)
Maximum height: \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\) \(s = \dfrac{u_{y}^{2}}{2g} = \dfrac{10.0^{2}}{2 \times 9.8} = \class{res}{5.1 \,\mathrm{m}}\) Lands at launch height Time of flight \(= 2t = 2 \times 1.02 = \class{res}{2.04 \,\mathrm{s}}\) Range \(= u_{x} \times 2t = 17.3 \times 2.04 = \class{res}{35.3 \,\mathrm{m}}\)
A.1 KINEMATICS · SL + HL

The order of the steps

Resolve first. Then the vertical column gives the time to the top and the height. The symmetry of the path doubles the time. Only at the end does the horizontal column turn the time of flight into a range.

Doubling the time is allowed only here

It relies on the body landing at the height it was launched from. If it lands higher or lower, the two halves of the flight are not equal and the vertical column must be solved for the whole flight, as in the next example.

Check that the calculator is in degrees before finding the components.

Worked example

Why find the landing velocity first

Going straight for the time with \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^{2}\) gives \(25 = 4.0t + 4.9t^{2}\), a quadratic. Using \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\) and then \(v = u + at\) reaches the same time in two easy steps.

Why down is positive here

The launch velocity, the acceleration and the displacement all point downward. Calling down positive makes every vertical value positive, so there are no signs to lose.

Compare with dropping it from rest: \(t = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 25}{9.8}} = 2.26 \,\mathrm{s}\). Starting with a downward velocity gets it to the ground sooner.

Horizontal motion

There is now a force against the sideways motion, so the horizontal velocity is no longer constant: it falls throughout the flight. The body covers less ground in each second than the one before, which shortens the range and makes the last part of the path steep.

Vertical motion

On the way up, resistance and gravity both act downward, so the body slows more quickly than at \(g\) and does not climb as high. On the way down, resistance acts upward against gravity, so it speeds up more slowly than at \(g\). It therefore takes longer to come down than it took to go up, and lands more slowly than it was launched.

These effects are examined in words and sketches only. Describe the change and give the reason: which way the resistance acts and what it does to each component.

Terminal velocity.

  • From rest: the acceleration starts at \(g\)
  • As the speed rises, the resistance rises
  • So the acceleration falls
  • Resistance equals weight: no acceleration, constant terminal velocity

The graph tells the same story

Its gradient is the acceleration. It begins at \(g\), when the body is hardly moving and there is no resistance. The curve then bends over as the resistance grows, and becomes flat when the two forces balance.

A skydiver

In free fall the terminal velocity is about 55 m s⁻¹. Opening the parachute suddenly makes the resistance far greater than the weight, so the acceleration is upward and the skydiver slows. As the speed drops, so does the resistance, until the forces balance again at a new, much lower terminal velocity of about 5 m s⁻¹.

Saying the resistance becomes greater than the weight as the body falls. It rises until it equals the weight and then stops rising, because the speed stops rising.

This ignores the upthrust of the fluid, which is tiny in air. In a liquid the terminal velocity is reached when drag and upthrust together equal the weight. The forces themselves, including the viscous drag on a small sphere, are in A.2.

Why simplify at all?

With air resistance included there are no simple equations: the acceleration changes from moment to moment and the path has to be worked out step by step by computer. Leaving it out gives an answer in three lines, and for a dense, slow body such as a shot or a dropped stone that answer is very close to what is measured.

Galileo did the same

His rolling ball was slowed a little by friction and by the air. He treated those as small disturbances to be reduced, with a hard ball and a smooth groove, and looked for the simple rule underneath. Deciding what can be ignored is part of doing physics.

When a question asks for an assumption, name the one that matters: “air resistance is negligible” for a projectile, “the acceleration is constant” for the equations of motion.
