Part 01

The photoelectric effect.

How can light be used to create an electric current?

E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

From E.1

Photons: a reminder.

  • Light arrives as photons
  • Energy of each depends on frequency only
  • Intensity: how many arrive each second
\(E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}\)
RadiationWavelengthPhoton energy
Red light700 nm1.77 eV
Violet light400 nm3.10 eV
Ultraviolet250 nm4.96 eV
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

The shortcut used all through this topic. The data booklet gives \(hc = 1.24 \times 10^{-6} \,\mathrm{eV} \,\mathrm{m}\). Divide it by the wavelength in metres and the photon energy comes out in eV: \(\frac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{400 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.10 \,\mathrm{eV}\).

Two separate things. Frequency sets how much energy each photon has. Intensity sets how many photons arrive. A dim ultraviolet lamp sends few photons, but each one carries more energy than any photon from the brightest red lamp.

Work in eV in this topic. Work functions and electron energies are quoted in eV, so converting to joules is usually wasted effort.

The effect

Light knocks electrons out of a metal.

  • Shine light on a clean metal
  • Electrons may be emitted
  • They are called photoelectrons
But only if the frequency is high enough.
PHOTOELECTRIC EMISSIONNOT TO SCALE
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

A demonstration. Put a clean zinc plate on a negatively charged electroscope. Under a bright filament lamp the leaf stays up. Under a weak ultraviolet lamp it falls at once: electrons are leaving the zinc. Put a sheet of glass in the way, which absorbs ultraviolet, and it stops.

What needs explaining. The weak lamp works and the bright one does not. Whatever decides whether electrons escape, it is not how much energy arrives each second.

A photoelectron is an ordinary electron. The name only says how it was released.

“Clean” matters: an oxide layer on the surface changes the energy needed to escape.

The evidence

Four things the experiments show.

01 · Threshold

No emission below a certain frequency

The threshold frequency \(f_{0}\), different for each metal

02 · No delay

Emission starts at once

Even when the light is very dim

03 · Intensity

Brighter light: more electrons

But their maximum kinetic energy does not change

04 · Frequency

Higher frequency: faster electrons

Maximum kinetic energy rises with \(f\)

E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Why “maximum” kinetic energy? The electrons come out with a range of energies. One at the very surface loses the least on the way out and leaves with the most. One from deeper in loses more in collisions. Only the maximum is a clean, repeatable number.

How these were measured. Light of one frequency falls on a metal plate in a vacuum, and the current of photoelectrons is measured. Changing the frequency, the intensity and the metal one at a time gives the four results.

Learn these four as a set. Almost every extended question on the photoelectric effect asks you to explain two or three of them.

E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

The wave picture. A wave spreads its energy evenly over the whole surface, and a brighter wave delivers energy faster. So any electron should be able to soak up energy until it has enough to leave. A dim wave would just take longer, and a bright one of any colour would do it.

Putting a number on the delay. For very dim light, spreading the energy over all the atoms of the surface gives each electron so little that it would need minutes or hours to collect enough. The measured delay is less than a billionth of a second.

For each feature, write what the wave theory predicts, what is seen, and that they disagree. Naming the feature alone does not earn the mark.

Saying the wave theory predicts no emission. It predicts emission; it gets the conditions wrong.

Einstein (1905)

One photon, one electron.

  • Light is a stream of photons, each of energy \(hf\)
  • One photon gives all its energy to one electron
  • Work function \(\Phi\): the least energy needed to leave the metal
  • What is left over is kinetic energy
WHERE THE PHOTON’S ENERGY GOES
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

How this explains all four results.

Threshold: if \(hf\) is less than \(\Phi\), no single photon can free an electron, and photons do not team up. More photons do not help.

No delay: the energy arrives in one lump, so the electron has enough the moment a photon reaches it.

Intensity: brighter light is more photons each second, so more electrons each second. Each photon is no bigger, so the maximum energy is the same.

Frequency: a higher frequency means more energy in each photon and more left over after paying \(\Phi\).

Einstein’s Nobel Prize (1921) was for this explanation, not for relativity.

The work function belongs to the metal. It is the minimum: most electrons need more than \(\Phi\) to get out.

Einstein’s equation

The photoelectric equation.

\(E_{\mathrm{max}} = hf - \Phi\)
  • \(E_{\mathrm{max}}\): maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons
  • \(\Phi\): work function of the metal
  • At the threshold \(E_{\mathrm{max}} = 0\), so \(hf_{0} = \Phi\)
Threshold for zinc · \(\Phi = 4.30 \,\mathrm{eV}\) \(f_{0} = \dfrac{\Phi}{h} = \dfrac{4.30 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}\)   \(= \class{res}{1.04 \times 10^{15} \,\mathrm{Hz}}\) \(\lambda _{0} = \dfrac{c}{f_{0}} = \class{res}{2.88 \times 10^{-7} \,\mathrm{m}}\) 288 nm: ultraviolet
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

It is conservation of energy. Energy in (one photon, \(hf\)) = energy to escape \((\Phi )\) + kinetic energy left \((E_{\mathrm{max}})\). Nothing else is going on.

Why zinc ignores visible light. Its threshold wavelength is 288 nm. Visible light runs from 400 to 700 nm, so every visible photon has too little energy, which is what the electroscope showed. Metals such as caesium and sodium have work functions near 2 eV and do respond to visible light.

Mixing units: \(hf\) worked out in joules with \(\Phi\) left in eV. Put both in the same unit before subtracting.

A negative answer for \(E_{\mathrm{max}}\) means the frequency is below the threshold: no electrons are emitted.

Einstein’s equation as a graph

One straight line, three readings.

  • \(E_{\mathrm{max}} = hf - \Phi\) has the form \(y = mx + c\)
  • Gradient = \(h\), the same for every metal
  • Intercept on the \(f\) axis = \(f_{0}\)
  • Intercept on the energy axis = \(-\Phi\)
Another metal: a parallel line.
MAXIMUM KINETIC ENERGY AGAINST FREQUENCY
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Reading the graph. Below \(f_{0}\) there are no electrons, so there are no points; the dashed part is the line extended back to find \(-\Phi\). Above \(f_{0}\), each extra hertz adds the same extra energy, \(h\).

Why every metal gives the same gradient. The gradient is the Planck constant, which belongs to light and not to the metal. Changing the metal changes only \(\Phi\), sliding the line sideways.

Robert Millikan spent years measuring this line, expecting to prove Einstein wrong. His results, published in 1916, fitted the equation and gave an accurate value of \(h\).

If the energy axis is in eV, the gradient is \(h\) in eV s: multiply by \(1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) for J s.

Measuring the maximum kinetic energy

Stopping the fastest electrons.

  • Make the collector negative
  • Raise the p.d. until the current is zero
  • That p.d. is the stopping potential \(V_{\mathrm{s}}\)
\(eV_{\mathrm{s}} = E_{\mathrm{max}}\)
PHOTOCELLNOT TO SCALE
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Why it works. An electron crossing to a collector that is negative by \(V\) loses kinetic energy \(eV\). Slow electrons are turned back first. When even the fastest just fail to arrive, the work done against the field equals their kinetic energy: \(eV_{\mathrm{s}} = E_{\mathrm{max}}\).

The convenient part. An electron stopped by 0.66 V had 0.66 eV. The stopping potential in volts is the maximum kinetic energy in electronvolts.

Brighter light of the same frequency gives a bigger current but the same stopping potential. Higher frequency gives a bigger stopping potential.

Worked example

250 nm ultraviolet on zinc.

\(\Phi = 4.30 \,\mathrm{eV}\) · \(hc = 1.24 \times 10^{-6} \,\mathrm{eV} \,\mathrm{m}\) Photon: \(E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{250 \times 10^{-9}} = \class{res}{4.96 \,\mathrm{eV}}\) \(E_{\mathrm{max}} = hf - \Phi = 4.96 - 4.30 = \class{res}{0.66 \,\mathrm{eV}}\)   \(= 0.66 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = \class{res}{1.1 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{J}}\) Stopping potential \(V_{\mathrm{s}} = \dfrac{E_{\mathrm{max}}}{e} = \class{res}{0.66 \,\mathrm{V}}\)
  • Double the intensity: still 0.66 eV, twice the current
  • Use 300 nm instead: 4.13 eV per photon, no emission
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

The order to work in. Photon energy first, then compare it with the work function. If the photon has less, stop: there is no emission and nothing more to calculate. If it has more, the difference is \(E_{\mathrm{max}}\).

Finding the speed. \(E_{\mathrm{max}} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\) with the energy in joules: \(v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.06 \times 10^{-19}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 4.8 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\).

Doubling the intensity does not double \(E_{\mathrm{max}}\). It doubles the number of photons, so the number of electrons.

E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

de Broglie (1924)

If waves can be particles…?

  • Light, a wave, also behaves as particles
  • de Broglie: particles should also behave as waves
  • Every moving particle has a wavelength
\(\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}\)
\(p = mv\), the momentum. More momentum → shorter wavelength.
THE DE BROGLIE WAVELENGTH
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Where the idea came from. A photon of wavelength \(\lambda\) has momentum \(p = h/\lambda\). de Broglie proposed, with no experimental evidence at the time, that the same relation holds for electrons and every other particle, read the other way round: \(\lambda = h/p\).

From energy to wavelength. For a particle that is not moving close to the speed of light, \(E_{\mathrm{k}} = p^{2}/2m\), so \(p = \sqrt{2mE_{\mathrm{k}}}\). An electron accelerated from rest through a p.d. \(V\) has \(E_{\mathrm{k}} = eV\).

This also answers a question left open in E.1. A whole number of electron wavelengths fitting round a Bohr orbit, \(2\pi r = n\lambda\), gives exactly Bohr’s rule \(mvr = nh/2\pi\).

Worked examples

An electron and a tennis ball.

Electron accelerated through 150 V \(E_{\mathrm{k}} = eV = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 150\)   \(= 2.40 \times 10^{-17} \,\mathrm{J}\) \(p = \sqrt{2mE_{\mathrm{k}}}\)   \(= \sqrt{2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.40 \times 10^{-17}}\)   \(= 6.61 \times 10^{-24} \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) \(\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \class{res}{1.0 \times 10^{-10} \,\mathrm{m}}\) About the spacing of atoms in a crystal
Tennis ball · 0.058 kg at \(50 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) \(p = mv = 0.058 \times 50 = 2.9 \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) \(\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p} = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{2.9}\)   \(= \class{res}{2.3 \times 10^{-34} \,\mathrm{m}}\) Far smaller than a nucleus
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Why we never see a ball diffract. Diffraction is noticeable only when the gap is about as wide as the wavelength. An electron’s wavelength matches the gaps between atoms in a crystal, so a crystal diffracts it. The ball’s wavelength is about \(\frac{1}{10^{19}}\) of the diameter of a nucleus, and nothing has gaps that small.

Changing the wavelength. For the electron, \(\lambda = h/\sqrt{2meV}\), so \(\lambda \propto 1/\sqrt{V}\). Four times the accelerating p.d. halves the wavelength.

Using \(\lambda = hc/E\) for an electron. That is for photons only. For a particle with mass use \(\lambda = h/p\).

E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

The test · Davisson & Germer, G. P. Thomson (1927)

Electron diffraction.

  • Fire electrons through thin graphite
  • The screen shows rings, not one spot
  • Rings are a diffraction pattern
  • Only waves diffract
ELECTRON DIFFRACTION TUBENOT TO SCALE
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Why graphite? Its layers of carbon atoms are spaced about \(10^{-10} \,\mathrm{m}\) apart, close to the wavelength of electrons accelerated through a few thousand volts. The regular layers act as a diffraction grating. The tiny crystals in the film point in every direction, so each diffracted beam is spread round into a ring.

Why the rings are evidence. Particles passing through a thin film would scatter into a blur that is brightest in the middle and fades steadily outwards. Bright rings with dark gaps between them mean that at some angles the electrons reinforce and at others they cancel: interference, which only waves do.

The same pattern builds up even when electrons are sent through one at a time. Each lands at one point, like a particle; the points pile up into rings, like a wave.

Evidence → conclusion

Testing \(\lambda = h/p\).

Diffraction of particles is the evidence for the wave nature of matter.
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Why do the rings shrink? Raising the p.d. gives each electron more kinetic energy and so more momentum. By \(\lambda = h/p\) the wavelength falls. A shorter wavelength is diffracted through a smaller angle, as it is for light at a grating, so the rings close in.

A prediction that could have failed. de Broglie’s equation says exactly what the wavelength should be for a given p.d. The wavelength measured from the rings, using the known spacing of the atoms, agreed.

Asked for evidence that matter has wave properties: name the experiment, say what is seen, and say that diffraction is something only waves do.

Diffraction of particles

Where the first minimum falls.

  • Particles scattered by an obstacle of width \(D\)
  • Counted at each angle \(\theta\)
  • Fewest arrive at the first minimum
\(\sin \theta \approx \dfrac{\lambda}{D}\)with \(\lambda = h/p\)
Measure \(\theta\) → find \(D\). This is how nuclei are measured.
SCATTERED PARTICLES AGAINST ANGLESKETCH
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

The same rule as for light. In C.3, light of wavelength \(\lambda\) passing a slit of width \(b\) has its first minimum at \(\theta = \frac{\lambda}{b}\). Particles do the same with their de Broglie wavelength, and an obstacle diffracts like a gap of the same width.

Worked example. Electrons with a de Broglie wavelength of 2.9 fm are scattered by carbon-12 nuclei, about 5.5 fm across. \(\sin \theta \approx \frac{2.9}{5.5} = 0.53\), so the first minimum is near 32°. The rule gives an estimate: the exact angle depends on how the charge is spread through the nucleus, which is what such experiments measure.

Why very fast electrons? To give a clear minimum the wavelength must be about the size of the nucleus, a few femtometres. That needs a very large momentum, so the electrons come from an accelerator.

Shorter wavelength (more momentum) or a bigger obstacle moves the minimum to a smaller angle.

Electrons are used because they do not feel the strong nuclear force, so the pattern shows the size of the nucleus cleanly, unlike the alpha particles in E.1.

Putting it together

Wave–particle duality.

Behaves as a waveBehaves as a particle
LightDiffraction, interferencePhotoelectric effect, Compton scattering
ElectronsDiffraction by crystalsEach arrives at one point, with charge and mass
Neither picture alone is complete. Which one shows up depends on the experiment.
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

What duality does and does not say. It does not say an electron is sometimes a little ball and sometimes a ripple. It says that “wave” and “particle” are both pictures taken from everyday objects, and that something as small as an electron or a photon fits neither one completely.

A useful guide. How it travels (spreading through gaps, forming patterns) is described by a wave. How it arrives or exchanges energy (in one place, in one lump) is described by a particle.

The two equations that tie the pictures together: \(E = hf\) and \(p = h/\lambda\). In each, a particle quantity on the left equals \(h\) times a wave quantity.

Compton (1923)

X-rays that change wavelength.

  • X-rays are scattered by electrons
  • The scattered X-rays have a longer wavelength
  • The bigger the angle, the bigger the change
  • The electron recoils
A collision between two particles.
ONE PHOTON, ONE ELECTRONNOT TO SCALE
~ Photon− Electron
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Why a longer wavelength? The photon gives some of its energy to the electron. A photon cannot slow down: it always travels at \(c\). The only way it can have less energy is a lower frequency, \(E = hf\), and so a longer wavelength.

What the wave theory expected. A wave shaking an electron makes it oscillate at the wave’s own frequency, and the electron then radiates at that same frequency. The scattered X-rays should have had exactly the wavelength of those going in.

Compton used X-rays on graphite. The outer electrons of carbon are held so weakly compared with an X-ray photon’s energy that they behave as free electrons at rest.

The Compton formula

The shift in wavelength.

\(\lambda _{\mathrm{f}} - \lambda _{\mathrm{i}} = \dfrac{h}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c}(1 - \cos \theta )\)
  • \(\theta\): angle the photon is turned through
  • \(h/m_{\mathrm{e}}c = \class{res}{2.43 \times 10^{-12} \,\mathrm{m}}\)
  • The shift depends only on the angle
Angle \(\theta\)\(1 - \cos \theta\)Shift \(\Delta \lambda\)
0°00
90°12.43 pm
180°24.85 pm
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Reading the formula. A photon that carries straight on \((\theta = 0)\) has not interacted, so nothing changes. One that bounces straight back \((\theta = 180^{\circ})\) has hit the electron hardest and gives it the most energy, so it has the largest shift.

What is not in the formula. Neither the starting wavelength nor the material appears. Every photon scattered through 90° by a free electron gains 2.43 pm, whatever it started with.

Why X-rays are needed. The shift is at most 4.85 pm. For X-rays of about 70 pm that is a few percent and can be measured. For visible light of 500 000 pm it is far too small to notice.

The formula is in the data booklet and its derivation is not required. \(1 \,\mathrm{pm} = 10^{-12} \,\mathrm{m}\).

Leaving the calculator in radians when the angles are in degrees. Check with \(\cos 90^{\circ} = 0\).

Worked example

An X-ray scattered through 60°.

Incoming wavelength 71.0 pm \(\Delta \lambda = \dfrac{h}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c}(1 - \cos \theta )\)   \(= 2.43 \times (1 - \cos 60^{\circ}) = \class{res}{1.2 \,\mathrm{pm}}\) \(\lambda _{\mathrm{f}} = \lambda _{\mathrm{i}} + \Delta \lambda = 71.0 + 1.2 = \class{res}{72.2 \,\mathrm{pm}}\) Before: \(\dfrac{hc}{\lambda _{\mathrm{i}}} = \dfrac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{71.0 \times 10^{-12}} = 17.46 \,\mathrm{keV}\) After: \(\dfrac{hc}{\lambda _{\mathrm{f}}} = \dfrac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{72.2 \times 10^{-12}} = 17.17 \,\mathrm{keV}\)
  • The photon has lost about 0.29 keV
  • The electron has gained it as kinetic energy
E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Two steps, never one. The formula gives the change in wavelength. Add it to the starting wavelength to get the final one. The energy the electron receives is then the difference between the two photon energies.

Keep the extra figures. The two photon energies differ by under 2%, so rounding them early would wipe out the answer. Carry four significant figures until the subtraction is done.

Giving 1.2 pm as the final wavelength. It is the shift.

E.2 QUANTUM PHYSICS · HL ONLY

Why is this stronger evidence than the photoelectric effect? The photoelectric effect shows that light delivers energy in lumps. Compton scattering shows more: the photon also has momentum, and energy and momentum are both conserved in a collision with a single electron, exactly as for two particles. The measured shift at every angle matches that calculation.

And it is seen directly. Here the photon is still there afterwards, travelling in a new direction with a new wavelength that can be measured. In the photoelectric effect the photon disappears.

For a photon, \(p = h/\lambda = E/c\). It is the same relation as de Broglie’s, now applied to light.
