Fusion.

Joining light nuclei

What fusion is.

DEUTERIUM AND TRITIUMPRESS → TO PLAY
  • Two light nuclei join to make a heavier one
  • The product is more tightly bound: mass is lost and energy released
  • Per kilogram of fuel, several times more than fission
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

The reaction on the slide. \({}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}\). Nucleon numbers: \(2 + 3 = 4 + 1\). Proton numbers: \(1 + 1 = 2 + 0\). It releases 17.6 MeV, most of it carried by the neutron, and it is the reaction that experimental fusion reactors on the Earth use, because it is the easiest to start.

Fission and fusion compared. Both release energy by ending with nuclei that are more tightly bound than the ones they began with. Fission gets there by splitting the heaviest nuclei; fusion by joining the lightest. One fusion releases less energy than one fission, 17.6 MeV against about 170 MeV, but the fuel is fifty times lighter, so the energy per kilogram is greater.

In the Sun the fuel is ordinary hydrogen, and it takes a chain of three steps to build one helium-4 nucleus from four protons.

²H is deuterium and ³H is tritium: the isotopes of hydrogen with one and two neutrons.

Binding energy per nucleon

Why fusion releases energy.

  • The curve is steep for light nuclei
  • ²H: 1.1 MeV per nucleon. ⁴He: 7.1 MeV per nucleon
  • Fusion releases energy up to iron, at the peak
Beyond iron, fusing nuclei would take in energy.

The same curve as E.4, read from the other end. Fission starts at the far right and moves up towards the peak by splitting. Fusion starts at the far left and moves up towards the peak by joining. Both end closer to iron-56, the most tightly bound region.

Why the left side is so steep. The strong nuclear force acts only between neighbouring nucleons. In deuterium each nucleon has one neighbour. In helium-4 each has three, all close together, so each is bound far more tightly. Helium-4 is an especially stable arrangement, which is why it stands above the line of its neighbours on the graph and why it is the particle emitted in alpha decay.

Why iron is the end. A star can only gain energy by fusing nuclei that end up higher on the curve. Once the core of a massive star has turned to iron, no further fusion can support it, and Part 2 shows what follows.

The elements heavier than iron in the Earth, and in you, were made where energy was available to be absorbed: in exploding stars and colliding neutron stars.

Worked example

Energy released in fusion.

²H + ³H → ⁴He + n · masses / u ²H 2.014102 · ³H 3.016049 ⁴He 4.002602 · n 1.008665 Before: \(2.014102 + 3.016049 = 5.030151\) After: \(4.002602 + 1.008665 = 5.011267\) \(\Delta m = 0.018884 \,\mathrm{u}\) \(E = 0.018884 \times 931.5 = \class{res}{17.6 \,\mathrm{MeV}}\)
In the Sun, overall: 4 ¹H → ⁴He ¹H 1.007825 u \(\Delta m = 4 \times 1.007825 - 4.002602\) \(\Delta m = 0.028698 \,\mathrm{u}\) \(E = 0.028698 \times 931.5 = \class{res}{26.7 \,\mathrm{MeV}}\)
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

The fraction of mass that disappears. For hydrogen turning into helium, 0.0287 u vanishes out of 4.0313 u: 0.71%. Seven parts in a thousand of the mass of the hydrogen is converted to energy. In fission the figure is less than one part in a thousand. That fraction, 0.007, is the number that decides how long a star can shine.

Method. It is exactly the mass-defect calculation of E.3 and E.4: add up the masses on each side, subtract, and multiply the difference in u by 931.5 to get MeV. Keep all the decimal places until the subtraction is done.

The masses used are those of neutral atoms, which include electrons. In these reactions the electrons balance on the two sides, so they make no difference.

Temperature and density

The conditions for fusion.

  • Nuclei are positive: they repel
  • The strong force acts only within about \(10^{-15}\) m
  • High temperature: enough kinetic energy to get that close
  • High density: enough collisions each second
TWO PROTONS APPROACHING
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

How hot. From B.1, the average kinetic energy of a particle is \(\frac{3}{2} k_\mathrm{B} T\), so a hotter gas has faster nuclei. Two protons approaching head-on convert their kinetic energy into electric potential energy as they close in (D.2). To get within reach of the strong force they must not be stopped and turned back first. In the core of the Sun the temperature is about fifteen million kelvin.

How dense. Even at that temperature a head-on collision at the right speed is rare, and most collisions that do happen fail to produce fusion. A high density of nuclei means an enormous number of collisions each second, so that enough of them succeed. The centre of the Sun is about 150 times as dense as water, and still a gas of bare nuclei and electrons: a plasma.

Why fusion is hard on the Earth. A star holds its hot, dense fuel together by its own gravity. A laboratory has to do it with magnetic fields or lasers, at temperatures of a hundred million kelvin, far hotter than the Sun, to make up for a much lower density.

Two conditions, two reasons: high temperature to overcome the repulsion; high density for a high rate of collisions.

Worked example

How long can the Sun shine.

\(L_{\odot} = 3.85 \times 10^{26} \,\mathrm{W}\) Mass converted to energy each second \(E = mc^{2}\)  so  \(\dfrac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{L}{c^{2}}\) \(= \dfrac{3.85 \times 10^{26}}{(3.00 \times 10^{8})^{2}}\) \(= \class{res}{4.3 \times 10^{9} \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
Mass \(2.0 \times 10^{30}\) kg · 10% of it fuses 0.71% of that mass becomes energy \(E = 0.10 \times 2.0 \times 10^{30} \times 0.0071 \times c^{2}\) \(E = 1.3 \times 10^{44} \,\mathrm{J}\) \(t = \dfrac{E}{L} = \dfrac{1.3 \times 10^{44}}{3.85 \times 10^{26}} = 3.3 \times 10^{17} \,\mathrm{s}\) \(t = \class{res}{1.0 \times 10^{10} \,\text{years}}\)
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Four million tonnes a second. The Sun’s mass falls by \(4.3 \times 10^{9}\) kg every second, and it has done so for 4.6 thousand million years. It is so massive that in all that time it has lost less than a thousandth of its mass in this way.

Why only 10%. Fusion happens only in the core, where it is hot and dense enough. The hydrogen in the outer layers never gets there. When the core’s hydrogen is used up, after about ten thousand million years, the Sun leaves the main sequence. It is roughly half way through.

What the estimate rests on. A luminosity, a mass, and the 0.71% from the last worked example. Nothing else. A calculation this short settled a question that had puzzled the nineteenth century, when the best available answer, gravitational shrinking, gave the Sun only a few tens of millions of years.

One year is \(3.16 \times 10^{7}\) s.

Part 02

The life of a star.

A long balance between gravity and pressure, and what happens when it ends

E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Why a star keeps its size

A star in equilibrium.

  • Gravity pulls every part of the star inwards
  • Radiation pressure and gas pressure push outwards
  • Fusion in the core supplies the energy that keeps the pressure up
Balanced: a stable star.
INWARDS AND OUTWARDS
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

A balance that corrects itself. Suppose the fusion in the core speeds up a little. The core gets hotter, the pressure rises and the star expands. Expanding cools the core and thins it out, so the fusion slows again. If instead the fusion slackens, the pressure drops, gravity squeezes the core, the core heats up and the fusion recovers. The star is its own thermostat, which is how the Sun has kept almost the same output for thousands of millions of years.

What radiation pressure is. Light carries momentum, so it pushes on whatever absorbs it. Photons from the core are absorbed and re-emitted countless times on their way out, each time giving the gas an outward push. In stars like the Sun the pressure of the hot gas does most of the work; in the most massive stars the pressure of the radiation takes over.

The phrase the guide uses: an equilibrium between outward radiation pressure and inward gravitational forces.

One number sets the story

Mass decides.

  • More mass: stronger gravity, so a hotter, denser core
  • Fusion runs far faster: much greater luminosity
  • So the fuel is used up sooner
  • And the star ends differently

Why bigger stars die younger. It seems the wrong way round: a star with ten times the fuel should last longer. But the rate of fusion is extremely sensitive to temperature, and the extra weight of a massive star makes its core much hotter. A star of ten solar masses has ten times the fuel and burns it about three thousand times as fast. Its life is \(\frac{10}{3000}\), about a three-hundredth, of the Sun’s.

Check the table by ratio. Lifetime is proportional to fuel divided by the rate of using it: mass ÷ luminosity. For the half-mass star, \(\frac{0.5}{0.1} = 5\) times the Sun’s life. Such stars have not had time to die since the universe began.

Birth. Every star starts as a cloud of gas and dust that collapses under its own gravity. Gravitational potential energy becomes thermal energy, the centre heats up, and when it is hot and dense enough for hydrogen to fuse, the collapse stops and a main sequence star is born.

The figures in the table are rounded orders of magnitude. What you need is the trend, and the reason for it.

Up to about eight solar masses

The life of a star like the Sun.

FOUR STAGESSIZES NOT TO SCALE
When the core runs out of hydrogen, fusion there stops, the core contracts and heats, and the outer layers swell and cool.
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Red giant. With no fusion to hold it up, the helium core is squeezed by gravity and gets hotter. Hydrogen goes on fusing in a shell round it, faster than before. The extra output pushes the outer layers outwards to a hundred times their old size. Spread over that huge surface, the star is very luminous but cool, and so red. In the core, helium eventually starts fusing to carbon and oxygen.

Planetary nebula. A star of this mass never gets hot enough to fuse carbon. Its outer layers drift away into space as a glowing shell. The name is historical: in a small telescope such a shell looks like the disc of a planet.

White dwarf. What remains is the bare core: about the mass of the Sun in a body the size of the Earth. No fusion happens in it. It is hot, so it shines, and it simply cools for the rest of time.

A white dwarf is held up not by thermal pressure but by electrons resisting being squeezed into the same state. That works only up to 1.4 solar masses.

More than about eight solar masses

The life of a massive star.

FOUR STAGESSIZES NOT TO SCALE
The core is hot enough to fuse heavier and heavier nuclei, until it is iron. Then fusion can supply no more energy.
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Red supergiant. Each time a fuel runs out, the core contracts, heats up and lights the next: helium to carbon, carbon to neon, and on through oxygen and silicon to iron. Each stage is shorter than the last: the final one takes about a day. The star ends with layers like an onion, the heaviest elements at the centre.

Supernova. Iron is at the peak of the binding energy curve. Fusing it would absorb energy, not release it. With nothing to hold it up, the iron core collapses in less than a second. The outer layers fall in, rebound and are blown into space in an explosion that can briefly outshine a whole galaxy.

What is left. The collapsed core is a neutron star: more than the mass of the Sun packed into a ball about 20 km across, at the density of an atomic nucleus (E.1). If the core is more massive still, nothing can stop the collapse, and it becomes a black hole.

The carbon, oxygen and iron scattered by such explosions are the raw material of later stars, of planets and of living things.

E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Luminosity against temperature

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram.

  • Luminosity upwards, in units of the Sun’s
  • Temperature across, decreasing to the right
  • Both scales are logarithmic
  • Stars fall into a few regions, not everywhere
EACH DOT IS A STARILLUSTRATIVE
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

The backwards axis. Temperature runs from hot on the left to cool on the right. This is a historical accident: the diagram was first drawn with the classes of stellar spectra along the bottom, and the classes had been lettered before anyone knew they were a temperature sequence. The convention has stuck. Blue-white stars are on the left, red stars on the right.

Why logarithmic. Stars range over a factor of ten thousand million in luminosity, from a ten-thousandth of the Sun’s to a million times it. On an ordinary scale all but the brightest would be squashed against the axis. On a logarithmic scale each step up is a factor of a hundred.

What the empty spaces mean. If any combination of temperature and luminosity were possible, the dots would be scattered evenly. They are not. The pattern is a record of how stars work: only certain combinations are stable, and stars spend most of their lives in one of them.

Drawing the temperature axis increasing to the right. In an exam sketch, label the hot end and the cool end.

E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

The main sequence is a sequence of mass. Part 2 showed that a more massive star is hotter and far more luminous. So the heavy stars sit at the upper left of the band and the light ones at the lower right. A star stays at very nearly the same point on the band for as long as it is fusing hydrogen in its core. It does not slide along the main sequence as it ages.

Reasoning about size. A red giant has a low surface temperature, so each square metre of its surface radiates little. Yet its luminosity is high. The only way to radiate a great deal from a cool surface is to have an enormous surface. A white dwarf is the opposite case: hot, so each square metre radiates strongly, yet faint overall, so it must be tiny.

Why most stars are on the main sequence. Fusing hydrogen is by far the longest stage of a star’s life. Catch a star at a random moment and that is most likely what it is doing.

You need to know that the instability strip exists and where it is. The pulsating stars in it, including Cepheid variables, are not part of the course.

Size on the HR diagram

Lines of constant radius.

\(L = \sigma A T^{4} = 4 \pi R^{2} \sigma T^{4}\)
  • For one radius, \(L \propto T^{4}\): a straight line on these axes
  • Sloping down to the right
  • Larger stars: lines further up and right
THREE RADII
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Where the lines come from. A star is nearly a black body, so by the Stefan–Boltzmann law of B.1 its luminosity is \(4 \pi R^{2} \sigma T^{4}\). Fix the radius and the luminosity depends only on \(T^{4}\). Halving the temperature cuts the luminosity by sixteen. On logarithmic axes a power law is a straight line, and since temperature decreases to the right, the line slopes down to the right.

Reading sizes. The white dwarfs lie along the line for a hundredth of the Sun’s radius: about the size of the Earth. The main sequence cuts across the lines at a shallow angle, from a tenth of a solar radius at the cool end to about ten at the hot end. Red giants are near a hundred solar radii, and the largest supergiants reach a thousand: larger than the orbit of Jupiter.

Moving at right angles to the lines, up and to the right, means getting bigger.

Sketching and interpreting lines of constant radius is required. Two stars on the same line have the same size, however different they look.

A life as a path

The Sun’s path on the HR diagram.

  • Now: on the main sequence, hardly moving
  • Red giant: up and to the right. Cooler, larger, more luminous
  • Outer layers lost: across to the left as the hot core is uncovered
  • White dwarf: down to the lower left, then cooling
A SKETCH OF THE PATH
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

The speed along the path is very uneven. The dot on the slide moves steadily, but a real star does not. The Sun will spend about ten thousand million years at its starting point, about a thousand million as a red giant, a few tens of thousands crossing to the left, and then the rest of time fading as a white dwarf. The number of stars seen in each region reflects how long stars spend there.

The path is not a journey through space. The diagram is a graph of properties. A star “moving” on it is staying where it is in the sky while its temperature and luminosity change.

A massive star starts higher up the main sequence and moves to the right across the top of the diagram, into the supergiants, before it explodes.

Each move can be explained with \(L = 4 \pi R^{2} \sigma T^{4}\). Up and right: \(R\) is growing faster than \(T^{4}\) is falling.

Measuring stars.

How far away, how bright, how hot and how big, from a point of light

Distance by geometry

Stellar parallax.

\(d \,\text{(parsec)} = \dfrac{1}{p \,\text{(arc-second)}}\)
  • A nearby star shifts against distant ones as the Earth orbits
  • \(p\): half the total shift in six months
  • Further away: a smaller angle
NOT TO SCALE
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Try it. Hold up a finger at arm’s length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the background. Bring it closer and it jumps further. Your eyes are a few centimetres apart; for a star, the two viewpoints are opposite sides of the Earth’s orbit, 300 million kilometres apart.

Why the equation is so simple. In the long thin triangle of the diagram, \(\tan p = \frac{1 \,\mathrm{AU}}{d}\), and for such tiny angles \(\tan p = p\) in radians. The parsec is defined to make the constants disappear: it is the distance at which the parallax is exactly one arc-second. So a parallax of 0.5 arc-second means 2 pc, and 0.1 arc-second means 10 pc.

How small the angles are. An arc-second is \(\frac{1}{3600}\) of a degree: the width of a small coin seen from four kilometres. No star has a parallax as large as one arc-second. The first to be measured was 61 Cygni, by Friedrich Bessel in 1838. From the ground the method reaches a few hundred parsecs; the Gaia spacecraft, launched in 2013, measures angles of tens of millionths of an arc-second.

Using the whole shift. The parallax angle is half the shift between two sightings six months apart.

Which unit for what. The astronomical unit suits a planetary system: Neptune is 30 AU from the Sun. The light year is the unit of popular writing, and says how old the light is that you are seeing. The parsec is the working unit of astronomers, because it comes straight out of the parallax measurement.

The emptiness of space. The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is 270 000 times further from us than the Sun is. If the Sun were a grapefruit in London, the Earth would be a grain of sand eleven metres away, and the nearest star another grapefruit in Moscow.

One parsec in metres. \(3.26 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15} = 3.08 \times 10^{16}\) m. All three conversions are in the data booklet, and converting between AU, light years and parsecs is required.

Treating a light year as a time. It is a distance.

Worked example

The radius of a star.

Sirius: \(L = 9.8 \times 10^{27} \,\mathrm{W}\) · \(T = 9940 \,\mathrm{K}\) \(L = 4 \pi R^{2} \sigma T^{4}\) \(R = \sqrt{\dfrac{L}{4 \pi \sigma T^{4}}}\) \(R = \sqrt{\dfrac{9.8 \times 10^{27}}{4 \pi \times 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 9940^{4}}}\) \(R = \class{res}{1.2 \times 10^{9} \,\mathrm{m}}\)
The same, as a ratio to the Sun (5800 K) \(\dfrac{R}{R_{\odot}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{L}{L_{\odot}}} \left(\dfrac{T_{\odot}}{T}\right)^{2}\) \(= \sqrt{25.4} \times \left(\dfrac{5800}{9940}\right)^{2}\) \(= \class{res}{1.7}\)
E.5 FUSION AND STARS · SL + HL

Why a ratio is easier. Divide \(L = 4 \pi R^{2} \sigma T^{4}\) for the star by the same equation for the Sun and the constants cancel: \(\frac{L}{L_{\odot}} = \left(\frac{R}{R_{\odot}}\right)^{2} \left(\frac{T}{T_{\odot}}\right)^{4}\). Luminosities are usually given in units of the Sun’s, so this form avoids large powers of ten altogether.

Sirius has a companion. Sirius B has almost the same surface temperature as Sirius but only 0.026 of the Sun’s luminosity. The same working gives a radius under a hundredth of the Sun’s: a white dwarf, the first ever identified. Two stars side by side in the sky, equally hot, one ten thousand times fainter than the other, differ only in size.

Raising the temperature to the fourth power but forgetting that the radius is squared, so a square root is needed at the end.

What is actually measured. A star is too far away to show a disc in most telescopes. All that arrives is light. From it astronomers measure three things: the direction it comes from, which over a year gives the parallax; how much power arrives per square metre, the apparent brightness; and how that power is spread across the wavelengths, the spectrum. Everything on the slide follows from those three.

Temperature and composition from the spectrum. The wavelength at which the spectrum peaks gives the surface temperature by Wien’s law, \(\lambda_\mathrm{max} T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\) m K. The dark lines crossing it are the fingerprints of the elements in the star’s outer layers (E.1): this is what Cecilia Payne read.

A chain is as strong as its first link. An error in the distance is squared in the luminosity and carries through to the radius. This is why precise parallaxes matter so much.

A typical long question walks down this chain. Recognise which step each part is asking for.
