The same thing happens at every step

A model explains the evidence of its day. An experiment gives a result it cannot explain. A new model is built that explains both the old evidence and the new.

Each model keeps what still works

The nuclear model kept the electrons and moved the positive charge to the center. Bohr kept the nucleus and organized the electrons. The earlier scientists were not foolish; each model was the best explanation of what was then known.

Old models stay useful

A chemist counting electrons in shells is using Bohr’s picture. An engineer treating a gas as tiny hard spheres is using the oldest one. A model is kept wherever it still gives the right answer.

Why did Thomson suggest this?

He had shown that atoms contain negative electrons. Atoms are neutral, so there had to be positive charge to balance them. Nobody knew where it was, so he took the simplest idea: spread through the whole atom.

What it predicted

With charge and mass spread thinly, nothing is concentrated enough to push a fast particle off course. The next experiment tested exactly that.

Why alpha particles?

They are positive, fast and heavy. Positive charge inside the atom would push on them, and how far they were pushed would show how that charge is arranged.

Why gold?

It can be beaten into an extremely thin foil, so an alpha particle is unlikely to be pushed off course more than once.

Why count at every angle, even back towards the source?

Nobody expected anything there. Checking anyway led to the discovery.

Why in a vacuum?

Alpha particles are stopped by a few centimeters of air, so the chamber was pumped out.

Why do the few that bounced back tell us the most?

To turn a fast, heavy alpha particle right round takes a large force from something much more massive than it. And because it happened so rarely, that something must be a very small target, seldom hit head-on.

Rutherford said it was as surprising as firing a shell at tissue paper and having it come back and hit you.

The units

\(e\) is the elementary charge, \(1.60 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{C}\). The unified atomic mass unit u is \(1.661 \times 10^{-27} \,\mathrm{kg}\), defined as one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Both are in the data booklet.

Why does only the nucleus count for mass?

An electron has about \(\frac{1}{1840}\) of the mass of a proton. Even gold’s 79 electrons add up to less than 0.03% of the mass of the atom.

The neutron is very slightly heavier than the proton (1.008665 u against 1.007276 u). That small difference matters in E.3, where it decides which decays can happen.

  • Neutrons: \(N = A - Z\)
  • X is the chemical symbol, fixed by \(Z\)

Work through another one

Uranium with 235 on top and 92 below. Protons: 92. Neutrons: \(235 - 92 = 143\). Electrons in the neutral atom: 92.

Why do the protons decide the element?

\(Z\) sets the charge of the nucleus, so it sets how many electrons the neutral atom holds and how it behaves chemically. Change \(Z\) and it is a different element. Change \(N\) alone and it is another isotope of the same element: carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have \(Z = 6\).

You are not expected to recall chemical symbols. Questions give the symbol or the proton number.

The same notation is used for particles: an alpha particle is helium-4 with \(A = 4, Z = 2\), and an electron is written with \(A = 0, Z = -1\).

How empty is “mostly empty”?

The radii differ by a factor of about \(10^{5}\). Scale an atom up to a stadium 200 m across and the nucleus is a bead 2 mm across on the center spot.

Using the numbers

Volume goes as radius cubed, so the nucleus fills about \((10^{-5})^{3} = 10^{-15}\) of the atom while holding more than 99.9% of its mass. Nuclear matter must be enormously dense: the HL part puts a number on it.

Why can you not push your hand through a table? The electrons on the outside of your atoms repel the electrons on the outside of the table’s.

Nature of science

Why models change.

  • A model must explain the evidence
  • It makes predictions we can test
  • New evidence can change or replace it
  • Results are published and peer reviewed

The atom is the standard example

The plum pudding model predicted that alpha particles would pass almost straight through gold foil. Some bounced back. The model could not explain that, so it was replaced by one that could.

Is the current model “proved”?

No model is. It is accepted because it explains all the evidence so far, and stays accepted until evidence turns up that it cannot explain.

Energy levels

Only certain energies allowed.

  • An electron in an atom can have only certain energies
  • These are discrete energy levels
  • Lowest level: the ground state
  • Energies are negative: the electron is bound
Zero energy = electron just free of the atom.
HYDROGEN: ENERGY LEVELSNOT TO SCALE
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, SL + HL

Think of a ladder

You can stand on a rung, never between rungs. An electron in an atom is the same: it has one of the allowed energies and nothing in between. “Discrete” means separate, countable values.

Why negative?

Zero is chosen as the energy of an electron that has just escaped and is at rest. An electron held by the nucleus has less energy than that, so its energy is below zero. To free a ground-state hydrogen electron you must supply 13.6 eV: the ionization energy.

The electronvolt is the energy gained by an electron moving through 1 V: \(1 \,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{J}\). Convert to joules before using \(E = hf\).

Reading the levels as distances. The diagram shows energy, not where the electron is.

Photons

Light comes in packets.

  • Light is emitted and absorbed as photons
  • Each photon carries one fixed amount of energy
  • Higher frequency → more energy per photon
\(E = hf\)
\(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{s}\), the Planck constant. With \(c = f\lambda\): \(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\).
Worked example: green laser, \(\lambda = 532\,\mathrm{nm}\) \(f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{532 \times 10^{-9}}\)   \(= \class{res}{5.64 \times 10^{14} \,\mathrm{Hz}}\) \(E = hf = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 5.64 \times 10^{14}\)   \(= \class{res}{3.74 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{J}}\)   \(= \dfrac{3.74 \times 10^{-19}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = \class{res}{2.34 \,\mathrm{eV}}\)
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, SL + HL

What is a photon?

A packet (quantum) of electromagnetic energy. Light of one frequency is a stream of identical photons. A brighter beam of the same color has more photons each second, not bigger ones.

How small is one photon?

A 1 mW laser pointer giving out \(3.74 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{J}\) photons emits \(\frac{0.001}{3.74 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{15}\) of them every second. That is why light normally looks continuous.

Visible photons carry roughly 1.8 eV (red) to 3.1 eV (violet). An answer far outside that for visible light means a slip with powers of ten or with nm.

Leaving the wavelength in nm. \(532 \,\mathrm{nm} = 532 \times 10^{-9} \,\mathrm{m}\).

Atomic transitions

One transition, one photon.

  • Absorb a photon → jump up a level
  • Drop down a level → emit a photon

− Electron+ Proton~ Photon, \(hf = E_{2} - E_{1}\)
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, SL + HL

Energy is conserved in every jump

The photon’s energy is exactly the difference between the two levels: \(hf = E_{\mathrm{upper}} - E_{\mathrm{lower}}\). A big gap gives a high-frequency photon; a small gap, a low-frequency one.

Absorption is all or nothing

A photon is absorbed only if its energy matches a gap exactly. One with slightly too little or slightly too much passes straight through. That is why a gas removes only certain frequencies from white light.

An atom that has absorbed a photon is excited. It usually drops back within about \(10^{-8} \,\mathrm{s}\), sometimes in several smaller steps, emitting a photon for each.

Worked example

From levels to a spectral line.

HYDROGEN: n = 3 → n = 2NOT TO SCALE
Find the wavelength of the photon emitted \(\Delta E_\mathrm{atom} = E_{2} - E_{3}\)   \(= (-3.40) - (-1.51) = \class{res}{-1.89 \,\mathrm{eV}}\) \(E_\gamma = -\Delta E_\mathrm{atom} = \class{res}{3.02 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{J}}\) \(f = \dfrac{E_\gamma}{h} = \dfrac{3.02 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}\)   \(= \class{res}{4.56 \times 10^{14} \,\mathrm{Hz}}\) \(\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \class{res}{6.58 \times 10^{-7} \,\mathrm{m}}\) (red)
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, SL + HL

The method never changes

Use \(\Delta E_\mathrm{atom} = E_\mathrm{f} - E_\mathrm{i}\). For emission this is negative. Conservation gives \(\Delta E_\mathrm{total} = \Delta E_\mathrm{atom} + E_\gamma = 0\), so the photon has positive energy \(E_\gamma = -\Delta E_\mathrm{atom} = hf\). Convert eV to J before using \(h\), then use \(c = f\lambda\).

Does it match?

Hydrogen’s red line is measured at 656 nm. We got 658 nm because the level energies were rounded to three significant figures. A subtraction of two rounded numbers loses precision.

A quicker route: the data booklet gives \(hc = 1.24 \times 10^{-6} \,\mathrm{eV} \,\mathrm{m}\), so \(\lambda = \frac{hc}{E_\gamma} = \frac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{1.89} = 6.56 \times 10^{-7} \,\mathrm{m}\) with the energy left in eV.

Using one level energy instead of the difference between two.

Where do the lines come from?

Heating the gas, or passing a current through it, lifts electrons to higher levels. Each time one drops back it emits a photon whose energy is the gap between two levels. Only certain gaps exist, so only certain frequencies appear.

Hydrogen’s four visible lines all end on \(n = 2\): from 3 (656 nm), 4 (486 nm), 5 (434 nm) and 6 (410 nm). Drops to \(n = 1\) release more energy and lie in the ultraviolet.

A hot solid or a dense gas gives a continuous spectrum instead, because its atoms are so close together that their levels smear into bands.

Spectra

Absorption spectra.

Why the same wavelengths?

The gaps between levels are the same whether the electron goes up or down. A photon that fits a gap is absorbed and lifts the electron; the same gap gives the same photon when the electron falls.

If the atoms re-emit the light, why is the line dark?

The excited atoms do emit again, but in all directions, and often in several smaller steps. Very little of it carries on towards the observer, so that wavelength is much dimmer than its neighbors.

Saying the dark lines are “missing colors the source did not make”. The source made them; the gas removed them.

Writing the argument

Start from what is seen (discrete lines), go through the photon (\(E = hf\), so discrete frequencies mean discrete photon energies), and finish at the atom (photon energy equals the difference between two levels, so the levels themselves must be discrete).

Counting lines

Each pair of levels gives one possible line. Four levels give 6 pairs, so up to 6 lines: 4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.

“Explain how spectra provide evidence for energy levels” needs all three links. Stopping at “the lines are discrete” earns one mark at most.

WHAT IS THE STAR MADE OF?
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, SL + HL

Why is the pattern unique?

The levels depend on the charge of the nucleus and on how the electrons arrange themselves around it. No two elements share both, so no two share a set of energy gaps.

How a star is analyzed

Its hot, dense interior gives a continuous spectrum. The cooler gas of its outer layers absorbs at its own wavelengths. Comparing the dark lines with spectra measured in the laboratory shows which elements are there.

One shared line is not enough. Helium’s yellow line (588 nm) sits almost on top of sodium’s (589 nm), but helium’s other lines are missing from this star.

Helium was found this way in the Sun’s spectrum in 1868, a yellow line that fitted no known element, before it was found on Earth. If all of a star’s lines are shifted by the same fraction, the shift gives its speed towards or away from us (Doppler effect, C.5).

\(E_{\mathrm{k}} = \dfrac{k(2e)(Ze)}{r}\)
An upper limit on the radius of the nucleus.
HEAD-ON COLLISIONNOT TO SCALE
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, ADDITIONAL HL

Why energy conservation is enough

The alpha particle starts far away, where the electric potential energy is zero, with kinetic energy \(E_{\mathrm{k}}\). The repulsion does negative work on it all the way in. It stops when the potential energy \(\frac{kq_{1}q_{2}}{r}\) has taken all of \(E_{\mathrm{k}}\). Rearranged: \(r = \frac{2kZe^{2}}{E_{\mathrm{k}}}\).

What is assumed?

A head-on path; a nucleus so heavy that it does not recoil; and only the electric force acting, which is true while the alpha stays outside the nucleus.

More energy → smaller \(r\). Larger \(Z\) → larger \(r\). Both follow directly from the formula.

Forgetting that the alpha particle’s charge is \(2e\), or leaving \(E_{\mathrm{k}}\) in MeV.

Worked example

A 5.0 MeV alpha meets gold.

Gold: \(Z = 79\), \(k = 8.99 \times 10^{9} \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{m}^{2} \,\mathrm{C}^{-2}\) \(E_{\mathrm{k}} = 5.0 \,\mathrm{MeV} = 5.0 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \,\mathrm{J}\)   \(= \class{res}{8.0 \times 10^{-13} \,\mathrm{J}}\) \(r = \dfrac{2kZe^{2}}{E_{\mathrm{k}}}\)   \(= \dfrac{2 \times 8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 79 \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^{2}}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}}\)   \(= \class{res}{4.5 \times 10^{-14} \,\mathrm{m}}\)
  • The alpha turned back 45 fm from the center
  • So the gold nucleus is smaller than that
  • The atom is ≈ \(10^{-10} \,\mathrm{m}\): over 2000 times bigger
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, ADDITIONAL HL

Why only an upper limit?

The alpha particle never touched the nucleus; it was turned back by the electric force while still outside. All the experiment shows is that the nucleus fits inside \(4.5 \times 10^{-14} \,\mathrm{m}\). The next slides show gold’s radius is in fact about \(7 \times 10^{-15} \,\mathrm{m}\).

To get closer, use more energy

Doubling \(E_{\mathrm{k}}\) halves \(r\). Rutherford was limited to the few MeV that radioactive sources provide.

1 fm (femtometer) = \(10^{-15} \,\mathrm{m}\), the natural unit for nuclei.

Scattering at high energies

Where Rutherford’s formula fails.

  • Rutherford assumed only electric repulsion
  • Low energy: prediction and count agree
  • High energy: the alpha gets close enough to touch
  • The strong nuclear force now acts too
  • Fewer are scattered than predicted
The energy where it fails gives the size of the nucleus.
ALPHAS SCATTERED AT ONE ANGLESKETCH
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, ADDITIONAL HL

What changes at high energy?

The strong nuclear force attracts nucleons but reaches only a few femtometers. A low-energy alpha is turned back long before that, so it feels only electric repulsion and Rutherford’s formula works. A fast one reaches the surface of the nucleus, where the strong force pulls on it and may absorb it. The simple electric calculation no longer describes what happens.

Turning the failure into a measurement

At the energy where the counts first depart from the prediction, the distance of closest approach roughly equals the radius of the nucleus plus that of the alpha. For gold that is about \(7.0 + 1.9 = 8.9 \,\mathrm{fm}\), which needs an alpha of roughly 26 MeV.

In scattering questions the energies are kept low enough that you only consider the electric force, unless the question is about the deviation itself.

Where does the cube root come from?

Nucleons pack like marbles in a bag: each takes up about the same space. So the volume of a nucleus is proportional to the number of nucleons, \(V \propto A\). Volume also goes as \(R^{3}\), so \(R^{3} \propto A\) and \(R \propto A^{1/3}\).

Worked example, gold-197

\(197^{1/3} = 5.82\), so \(R = 1.20 \times 10^{-15} \times 5.82 = 6.98 \times 10^{-15} \,\mathrm{m}\).

The radius grows slowly

Gold has 49 times the nucleons of helium-4 but less than 4 times the radius. To double the radius you need \(2^{3} = 8\) times as many nucleons.

On a calculator the power \(\frac{1}{3}\) needs brackets: \(A\) ^ (1/3).

Using the proton number \(Z\) in place of \(A\).

Size of the nucleus

Every nucleus has the same density.

Mass \(\approx A\,\mathrm{u}\), volume \(= \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}\) \(\rho = \dfrac{A\,\mathrm{u}}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi (R_{0}A^{1/3})^{3}}\) \(= \dfrac{3\,\mathrm{u}}{4\pi R_{0}^{3}}\)   \(= \dfrac{3 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27}}{4\pi \times (1.20 \times 10^{-15})^{3}}\)   \(\approx \class{res}{2.3 \times 10^{17} \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m}^{-3}}\)
  • \(A\) cancels: the same for all nuclei
  • About \(10^{14}\) times the density of water
  • Ordinary matter is mostly empty space
E.1 STRUCTURE OF THE ATOM, ADDITIONAL HL

Why does \(A\) cancel?

Mass is proportional to \(A\) (each nucleon is about 1 u). Volume is proportional to \(R^{3}\), and \((A^{1/3})^{3} = A\), so volume is proportional to \(A\) as well. The ratio does not depend on \(A\). A nucleus behaves like a drop of liquid: add more and it gets bigger, not denser.

Making sense of \(10^{17}\)

A teaspoon (\(5\,\mathrm{cm}^{3}\)) of nuclear matter would have a mass of about \(10^{12} \,\mathrm{kg}\), a billion tonnes.

A neutron star is a star crushed until it is nuclear matter throughout: more than the mass of the Sun in a ball about 20 km across.

Using the formula

\(n = 2\): \(\frac{-13.6}{4} = -3.40 \,\mathrm{eV}\). \(n = 3\): \(\frac{-13.6}{9} = -1.51 \,\mathrm{eV}\). \(n = 4\): \(\frac{-13.6}{16} = -0.85 \,\mathrm{eV}\). As \(n\) → ∞ the energy → 0 and the electron is free.

A transition, the quick way

For \(4 \to 2\): \(\Delta E_\mathrm{atom} = -3.40 - (-0.85) = -2.55 \,\mathrm{eV}\), so \(E_\gamma = -\Delta E_\mathrm{atom} = 2.55 \,\mathrm{eV}\). With \(hc = 1.24 \times 10^{-6} \,\mathrm{eV} \,\mathrm{m}, \lambda = \frac{1.24 \times 10^{-6}}{2.55} = 4.86 \times 10^{-7} \,\mathrm{m}\): hydrogen’s blue-green line at 486 nm.

Why it mattered

One formula with one constant reproduced every line of hydrogen then measured, and predicted further infrared lines that were found afterwards.

The Bohr model for hydrogen

Why only those levels?

  • Bohr’s rule: angular momentum is quantized
\(mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\)
  • Only certain orbits fit: \(r \propto n^{2}\)
  • Each orbit has one energy: \(E \propto -\frac{1}{n^{2}}\)

How the rule fixes the orbits

The electric force keeps the electron in a circle: \(\frac{ke^{2}}{r^{2}} = \frac{mv^{2}}{r}\). Bohr’s rule gives \(v = \frac{nh}{2\pi mr}\). Put one into the other and \(r = \frac{n^{2}h^{2}}{4\pi^{2}kme^{2}}\). Everything except \(n\) is a constant, so \(r \propto n^{2}\). For \(n = 1\) it gives \(5.3 \times 10^{-11} \,\mathrm{m}\), the right size for an atom.

And the energies

The total energy of the orbiting electron is \(-\frac{ke^{2}}{2r}\). With \(r \propto n^{2}\) this is proportional to \(-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\), and the constants multiply out to 13.6 eV.

Why should angular momentum be quantized? Bohr could not say. E.2 gives a reason: an electron has a wavelength \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}\), and a whole number of wavelengths must fit round the orbit, \(2\pi r = n\lambda\). That is the same equation.

\(mvr\) is the angular momentum of a particle in a circular orbit. The derivation is not required, but you should be able to use the rule.

Why does a second electron break it?

Bohr’s calculation has one electron attracted by one nucleus. Add another electron and the two repel each other, which the model has no way to include. It gives the wrong levels even for helium.

What else it gets wrong

It mixes classical orbits with a quantum rule that has no justification. It cannot say how likely each transition is, so it cannot give line intensities. And an electron with a definite orbit has an exact position and momentum together, which later physics showed is impossible.

Why still teach it?

It was the first model to give discrete levels from a calculation, and its formula for hydrogen is exactly what quantum mechanics gives too.
