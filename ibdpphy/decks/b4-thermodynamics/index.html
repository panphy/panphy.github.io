Part 01 · HL only

The first law.

Conservation of energy for a gas: heat in, work out, internal energy changed

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

What are we talking about?

System and surroundings.

  • The system is the thing studied, usually a fixed amount of gas. Everything else is the surroundings

Closed system

No mass in or out

Energy can cross the boundary, as heat or as work

Example: gas sealed in a cylinder with a piston

Isolated system

Neither mass nor energy in or out

Its total energy is fixed

Example: a sealed, perfectly insulated flask. The whole universe

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Why the distinction matters. The first law is written for a closed system: it keeps account of the energy that crosses the boundary. The second law, in its simplest form, is about an isolated system: it says what the entropy inside can do when nothing crosses. Using a law with the wrong kind of system is the most common source of confusion in this topic.

Heat and work are transfers, not contents. A gas does not “contain” heat or work. It contains internal energy. Heat is energy crossing the boundary because of a temperature difference; work is energy crossing because the boundary moves against a force.

Draw a dotted line round the system before you start. Anything crossing that line is \(Q\) or \(W\). Anything that stays inside is \(U\).

Energy inside the gas

Internal energy of a monatomic ideal gas.

\(\Delta U = \dfrac{3}{2} n R \Delta T = \dfrac{3}{2} N k_\mathrm{B} \Delta T\)
  • The total random kinetic energy of the atoms
  • Depends only on temperature, for a fixed amount of gas
  • \(n\) moles, or \(N\) atoms
2.0 mol of helium heated from 300 K to 450 K \(\Delta U = \dfrac{3}{2} n R \Delta T\) \(\Delta U = \dfrac{3}{2} \times 2.0 \times 8.31 \times 150\) \(\Delta U = \class{res}{3.7 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{J}}\)
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Where the equation comes from. In B.3 the average kinetic energy of one atom of an ideal gas is \(\frac{3}{2} k_\mathrm{B} T\). An ideal gas has no forces between its atoms, so no potential energy. Its internal energy is just the kinetic energy of all \(N\) atoms: \(U = \frac{3}{2} N k_\mathrm{B} T\). Since \(N k_\mathrm{B} = nR\), the two forms are the same.

The key consequence. If the temperature of the gas does not change, its internal energy does not change, whatever happens to its pressure and volume. If the temperature returns to its starting value, so does the internal energy. This one fact settles half the questions in the topic.

Because \(PV = nRT\), you can also write \(\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} \Delta (PV)\). It saves working out the temperatures when a question gives pressures and volumes.

Using this for a gas of molecules such as oxygen or nitrogen. The factor \(\frac{3}{2}\) is for single atoms only.

Moving the boundary

Work done by a gas.

GAS IN A CYLINDERPRESS → TO LET IT EXPAND
\(W = F \Delta x = P A \Delta x\) \(W = P \Delta V\)
  • At constant pressure
  • Expansion: work done by the gas, positive
  • Compression: work done on the gas, negative
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

The step in the middle. The piston has area \(A\) and moves a distance \(\Delta x\), so the volume of the gas grows by \(A \Delta x\). That is \(\Delta V\). Force times distance has become pressure times change in volume, and the shape of the container no longer matters: the same result holds for a balloon or a bubble.

Where the energy comes from. Atoms that hit a piston moving away from them bounce off more slowly, as a ball does off a bat that is being pulled back. So a gas that does work loses internal energy, and cools, unless heat is supplied at the same time to make up for it.

A numerical case: a gas at \(1.0 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{Pa}\) expands by \(2.0 \times 10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\), two litres. \(W = 1.0 \times 10^{5} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} = 200 \,\mathrm{J}\).

When the pressure changes

Work on a pressure–volume diagram.

  • Work done = the area under the path
  • Path to the right: expansion, work done by the gas
  • Path to the left: compression, work done on the gas
  • Vertical path: no work
AN EXPANSION
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Why the area. Split the change into thin vertical strips. Across each strip the pressure is nearly constant, so the work is \(P \Delta V\): the height of the strip times its width. Adding the strips gives the area under the curve. It is the same argument as for a force–displacement graph in A.3.

Work depends on the route. There are many paths between the same two states on the diagram, and each has a different area under it. So the work done, and the heat needed, depend on how the change is carried out. The change in internal energy does not: it depends only on the two end states. That is why \(U\) is called a property of the state, and \(Q\) and \(W\) are not.

Estimate an awkward area by counting squares, after working out how many joules one square is worth: pascals times cubic metres.

Conservation of energy

The first law of thermodynamics.

\(\Delta U = Q - W\)
  • Internal energy rises by the thermal energy supplied, less the work the gas does
  • For a closed system
PositiveNegative
\(Q\)Thermal energy supplied to the systemThermal energy lost by it
\(W\)Work done by the systemWork done on it
\(\Delta U\)Temperature risesTemperature falls
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Reading it as a budget. \(Q\) is income. \(W\) is what is spent. \(\Delta U\) is the change in savings: income minus spending. If the gas spends more in work than it receives as heat, its savings, the internal energy, must fall.

The sign convention. This is the Clausius convention, and it is the one used in every exam question: \(Q\) is counted positive when it goes into the gas, \(W\) is positive when it is done by the gas. Some books write \(\Delta U = Q + W\) with \(W\) as the work done on the gas. The physics is identical, but mixing the two gives wrong signs.

The data booklet. It prints the same law rearranged, as \(Q = \Delta U + W\). Nothing is different: add \(W\) to both sides of the form on the slide.

Before substituting, write beside each quantity whether it is into or out of the gas, and give it its sign. Most mistakes are sign mistakes.

Worked example

Using the first law.

A gas is supplied with 500 J of thermal energy and does 200 J of work as it expands \(\Delta U = Q - W\) \(\Delta U = 500 - 200 = \class{res}{+300 \,\mathrm{J}}\) Its temperature rises
150 J of work is done on a gas while it loses 400 J of thermal energy \(Q = -400 \,\mathrm{J}\)  ·  \(W = -150 \,\mathrm{J}\) \(\Delta U = Q - W = -400 - (-150) = \class{res}{-250 \,\mathrm{J}}\) Its temperature falls
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Checking the second one by common sense. Compressing the gas puts 150 J in. Cooling it takes 400 J out. The gas ends 250 J down. The signs in the equation only keep track of that.

From internal energy to temperature. For 0.50 mol of a monatomic gas, a change of −250 J means \(\Delta T = \frac{\Delta U}{\frac{3}{2} n R} = \frac{-250}{1.5 \times 0.50 \times 8.31} = -40 \,\mathrm{K}\).

Writing \(W = +150 \,\mathrm{J}\) because “work is done”. Work done on the system is negative in this convention.

Part 02 · HL only

Gas processes.

Four changes, each with one thing held fixed, and how they join into a cycle

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Keeping one variable fixed

Four processes.

  • Isobaric: constant pressure
  • Isovolumetric: constant volume
  • Isothermal: constant temperature
  • Adiabatic: no thermal energy in or out
FOUR LINES THROUGH ONE STATEEITHER DIRECTION
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

The names. “Iso” means the same. Bar is pressure, as in barometer; therm is temperature, as in thermometer. Adiabatic comes from Greek words meaning “not passing through”: no heat passes through the walls.

Telling the two curves apart. An isothermal and an adiabatic expansion both curve downwards, but the adiabatic curve is steeper. In an isothermal expansion heat flows in and keeps the temperature up. In an adiabatic expansion nothing replaces the energy the gas spends on work, so it cools, and its pressure falls for two reasons at once: larger volume and lower temperature.

Each point on a P–V diagram is one state of the gas, with one temperature: \(T = \frac{PV}{nR}\). Further from the origin means hotter.

The lines have no arrows because each change can go either way: to the right is an expansion, to the left a compression. An arrow belongs on a diagram only when a question says which way the gas went.

Constant volume, constant pressure

Isovolumetric and isobaric changes.

Isovolumetric

\(W = 0\), so \(\Delta U = Q\)

All the heat supplied raises the temperature

Isobaric

\(W = P \Delta V\), so \(\Delta U = Q - P \Delta V\)

Some of the heat is spent on work

Isobaric: \(P = 1.0 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{Pa}\) \(V\) from \(2.0 \times 10^{-3}\) to \(5.0 \times 10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) \(W = P \Delta V = 1.0 \times 10^{5} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = \class{res}{300 \,\mathrm{J}}\) \(\Delta U = \dfrac{3}{2} P \Delta V = \dfrac{3}{2} \times 300 = \class{res}{450 \,\mathrm{J}}\) \(\Delta U = Q - W\)  so  \(Q = \Delta U + W = \class{res}{750 \,\mathrm{J}}\)
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Why heating at constant pressure costs more. To raise the temperature of the same gas by the same amount, more heat is needed at constant pressure than at constant volume. At constant volume every joule goes into the internal energy. At constant pressure the gas must expand as it warms, and for a monatomic ideal gas only \(\frac{3}{5}\) of the heat raises the internal energy: here 450 J out of 750 J. The other \(\frac{2}{5}\) leaves as work.

Where \(\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} P \Delta V\) comes from. \(\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} n R \Delta T\), and at constant pressure \(n R \Delta T = P \Delta V\) from the ideal gas equation.

In an isovolumetric change, \(\frac{P}{T}\) is constant. In an isobaric change, \(\frac{V}{T}\) is constant. Both follow from \(PV = nRT\).

Constant temperature

Isothermal changes.

  • \(T\) constant, so \(\Delta U = 0\)
  • First law: \(0 = Q - W\), so \(Q = W\)
  • \(PV\) = constant: Boyle’s law
All the heat supplied comes straight out as work.
AN ISOTHERMAL CHANGE
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

How to do it in practice. The gas must be able to exchange heat freely with surroundings at a fixed temperature, and the change must be slow, so that heat has time to flow. Push a piston in slowly on a gas in a thin metal cylinder standing in a water bath and the compression is very nearly isothermal.

Compression. Everything reverses. Work is done on the gas, \(W\) is negative, so \(Q\) is negative by the same amount: the gas must give out heat equal to the work done on it, or its temperature would rise.

Assuming “no temperature change” means “no heat”. In an isothermal change heat does flow. It is the internal energy that does not change.

No thermal energy transferred

Adiabatic changes.

\(P V^{\frac{5}{3}} = \text{constant}\)
  • \(Q = 0\), so \(\Delta U = -W\)
  • Expansion: the gas does work and cools
  • Compression: work is done on it and it warms
ADIABATIC AND ISOTHERMAL COMPARED
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

How to do it in practice. Either insulate the gas well, or make the change so fast that heat has no time to flow. A bicycle pump gets hot because the air in it is compressed quickly. Gas escaping from a pressurised can expands quickly and comes out cold. Sound waves compress and expand air so rapidly that the changes are adiabatic.

Where the temperature goes. With no heat exchanged, the work done by the gas comes entirely out of its internal energy: \(\Delta U = -W\). Internal energy is temperature, for an ideal gas, so the temperature must fall.

The power \(\frac{5}{3}\) is for a monatomic ideal gas only. It is given in the data booklet.

Clouds form this way: moist air rises, expands adiabatically in the lower pressure, cools, and its water vapour condenses.

Worked example

An adiabatic compression.

\(P_{1} = 1.0 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{Pa}\) · \(V_{1} = 4.0 \times 10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) · \(T_{1} = 300 \,\mathrm{K}\) Compressed to \(V_{2} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) \(P_{1} V_{1}^{\frac{5}{3}} = P_{2} V_{2}^{\frac{5}{3}}\)  so  \(P_{2} = P_{1} \left(\dfrac{V_{1}}{V_{2}}\right)^{\frac{5}{3}}\) \(P_{2} = 1.0 \times 10^{5} \times 4.0^{\frac{5}{3}} = \class{res}{1.0 \times 10^{6} \,\mathrm{Pa}}\)
The new temperature \(\dfrac{P_{1} V_{1}}{T_{1}} = \dfrac{P_{2} V_{2}}{T_{2}}\) \(T_{2} = T_{1} \dfrac{P_{2} V_{2}}{P_{1} V_{1}} = 300 \times \dfrac{10.1 \times 1.0}{1.0 \times 4.0}\) \(T_{2} = \class{res}{760 \,\mathrm{K}}\)
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Compare with an isothermal compression. Squeezed slowly to a quarter of its volume at 300 K, the gas would reach only four times the pressure, \(4.0 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{Pa}\). Done adiabatically the pressure rises tenfold, because the gas has also become much hotter. A diesel engine compresses air so sharply that the fuel ignites without a spark.

The work done. With \(Q = 0\), the work done on the gas equals the rise in its internal energy: \(\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} (P_{2} V_{2} - P_{1} V_{1}) = \frac{3}{2} (1010 - 400) = 910 \,\mathrm{J}\).

On a calculator, \(4.0^{\frac{5}{3}}\) is 4 ^ (5 / 3) = 10.1. The brackets matter.

Using \(P_{1} V_{1} = P_{2} V_{2}\). That is the isothermal law. The ideal gas equation with temperatures still holds for any change.

Side by side

The four processes compared.

Isovolumetric\(V\)\(\Delta U = Q\)Vertical line
Isobaric\(P\)\(\Delta U = Q - P \Delta V\)Horizontal line

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

One idea, four times. In each process one term of \(\Delta U = Q - W\) is zero, or one quantity is easy to find, and the law collapses to something simpler. Constant volume: no work. Constant temperature: no change in internal energy. Adiabatic: no heat. Constant pressure: all three terms survive, but the work is simply \(P \Delta V\).

A plan for any question. Name the process. Write down which term vanishes. Find the others from \(W = P \Delta V\) or the area, from \(\Delta U = \frac{3}{2} n R \Delta T\), and from the first law.

The ideal gas equation, \(PV = nRT\), holds at every point of every process. Use it to find any temperature you need.

Back to the start

Cyclic processes.

  • The gas returns to its starting state: over a cycle \(\Delta U = 0\)
  • Net work done = the area enclosed
  • Clockwise: net work done by the gas
Pressure in \(10^{5} \,\mathrm{Pa}\) · volume in \(10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) \(W = \Delta P \, \Delta V\) \(W = 2.0 \times 10^{5} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = \class{res}{600 \,\mathrm{J}}\)
A RECTANGULAR CYCLE
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Why the enclosed area. From A to B the gas expands at high pressure and does work equal to the whole area under the top edge: \(3.0 \times 10^{5} \times 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = 900 \,\mathrm{J}\). From C to D it is compressed at low pressure, and work is done on it equal to the area under the bottom edge, 300 J. The two vertical sides involve no work. The net work is the difference, 600 J: the area inside the loop.

Where it comes from. Over the cycle \(\Delta U = 0\), so the first law gives \(Q_\mathrm{net} = W_\mathrm{net}\). The gas takes in more heat than it gives out, and the difference appears as work. A gas taken round and round such a loop is an engine.

Go round the loop anticlockwise and the net work is done on the gas: that is a refrigerator or heat pump.

Part 03 · HL only

Entropy and the second law.

Why some things happen by themselves and their reverse never does

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

A direction for time

Processes that only go one way.

GAS IN HALF A BOXPRESS → TO REMOVE THE PARTITION
  • The gas spreads out. It never un-spreads
  • Heat flows from hot to cold, never back
  • Energy would be conserved either way: the first law allows both
  • Real processes are irreversible
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

What needs explaining. Film a ball bouncing to rest, an ice cube melting in a warm drink or smoke spreading through a room, and run the film backwards. Nothing in the reversed film breaks conservation of energy. The floor could cool slightly and give its thermal energy back to the ball. Yet it never happens. Some other law must pick out the direction in which things really go.

The answer in one sentence. There are overwhelmingly more ways for the gas to be spread through the whole box than for it to be in one half, so that is where chance takes it. The rest of this part makes that idea exact.

A reversible process is an ideal limit: one carried out so slowly and gently, with no friction and no temperature differences, that it could be run backwards leaving no trace.

Counting the ways

Microstates and macrostates.

  • Macrostate: what you can see, such as “2 heads”
  • Microstate: the exact arrangement, such as HTHT
  • Every microstate is equally likely
  • \(\Omega\): the number of microstates of a macrostate
FOUR COINS16 MICROSTATES
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

The six ways to get two heads. HHTT, HTHT, HTTH, THHT, THTH, TTHH. Each is exactly as likely as HHHH, which can happen in only one way. So “two heads” turns up six times as often as “four heads”, not because any arrangement is favoured but because more arrangements fit the description.

What happens with more coins. With 100 coins there are \(2^{100} \approx 10^{30}\) microstates. Only one of them is all heads. The macrostates close to 50 heads account for nearly all the rest. Shake the coins and you will always find a number close to 50. For the \(10^{23}\) atoms of a gas the same effect is so extreme that the most likely macrostate is the only one ever seen.

For the gas: the macrostate is its pressure, volume and temperature. A microstate is the position and velocity of every atom.

A measure of disorder

Entropy.

\(S = k_\mathrm{B} \ln \Omega\)
  • More microstates: more disorder, more entropy
  • Unit: J K⁻¹
  • \(k_\mathrm{B} = 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\)
Four coins, all heads: \(\Omega = 1\) \(S = k_\mathrm{B} \ln 1 = \class{res}{0}\) Two heads and two tails: \(\Omega = 6\) \(S = 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times \ln 6\) \(S = \class{res}{2.5 \times 10^{-23} \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}}\)
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Why a logarithm. Put two systems side by side. Every microstate of one can go with every microstate of the other, so the numbers of microstates multiply: \(\Omega = \Omega_{1} \Omega_{2}\). But we want entropy to add, like energy or volume: two identical blocks should have twice the entropy of one. The logarithm turns multiplying into adding: \(\ln (\Omega_{1} \Omega_{2}) = \ln \Omega_{1} + \ln \Omega_{2}\).

What “disorder” means here. Not untidiness, but the number of ways the atoms could be arranged without your noticing any difference. A crystal has few. A gas has vastly more. A gas in a large container has more than the same gas in a small one.

Entropy rises when a solid melts, a liquid boils, a gas expands, two substances mix, or anything is heated.

The large-scale view

Entropy change from thermal energy.

\(\Delta S = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{T}\)
  • \(\Delta Q\): thermal energy transferred at absolute temperature \(T\)
  • Energy in: entropy rises. Energy out: it falls
  • The same heat counts for more at a low temperature
0.10 kg of ice melts at 273 K Specific latent heat \(3.34 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\) \(\Delta Q = mL = 0.10 \times 3.34 \times 10^{5} = 3.34 \times 10^{4} \,\mathrm{J}\) \(\Delta S = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{T} = \dfrac{3.34 \times 10^{4}}{273}\) \(\Delta S = \class{res}{+122 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}}\)
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Two definitions, one quantity. Clausius defined entropy this way in 1865, from heat and temperature, with no mention of atoms. Boltzmann’s counting of microstates came later and gives the same numbers. Adding thermal energy to a body gives its atoms more energy to share out, and so more ways to share it: more microstates.

Why temperature is underneath. A given amount of heat makes a big difference to a cold body, whose atoms have little energy, and a small difference to a hot one, which already has a huge number of microstates. A whisper is noticed in a library and lost in a crowded street.

Use this form when the temperature stays constant during the transfer: a change of state, an isothermal process, or a reservoir so large that its temperature hardly changes.

Using °C. The temperature must be in kelvin.

Three ways of saying it

The second law of thermodynamics.

Entropy form

The entropy of an isolated system never decreases

Reversible process: it stays the same
Irreversible process: it increases

Clausius form

Heat does not flow by itself from cold to hot

Moving thermal energy from a colder body to a hotter one needs work

Kelvin form

No engine can turn heat entirely into work

Working in a cycle, it must reject some thermal energy to a colder body

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

They are the same law. Suppose heat could flow by itself from cold to hot, against Clausius. Then the waste heat of any engine could be sent back to its hot source for nothing, and the engine would in effect turn heat entirely into work, against Kelvin. Each statement implies the other, and both follow from the entropy form, as the next slide shows for the first.

Real processes. Friction, heat flow across a temperature difference, mixing and sudden expansion all increase entropy, and one or more of them is present in every real process. So in practice the entropy of an isolated system always goes up. It can never be brought back down, which is exactly what “irreversible” means.

The universe as a whole is an isolated system. Its entropy has been rising since the beginning, and this is what gives time its direction.

Worked example

Why heat flows from hot to cold.

600 J flows from a body at 400 K to one at 300 K \(\Delta S = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{T}\) for each body Hot body: \(\Delta S = \dfrac{-600}{400} = -1.5 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\) Cold body: \(\Delta S = \dfrac{+600}{300} = +2.0 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\) Total: \(\class{res}{+0.5 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}}\)
  • The total entropy rises: this happens by itself
  • Reversed, the total would be \(-0.5 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\): forbidden
  • The hot body’s entropy fell, but the cold body’s rose by more
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

The Clausius form, derived. The same 600 J is divided by a smaller temperature for the cold body than for the hot one, so the gain is always larger than the loss. Heat flowing from hot to cold always raises the total entropy. Heat flowing the other way would always lower it. The entropy form of the second law therefore forbids it.

Equal temperatures. If both bodies were at 350 K, the two changes would cancel and the total entropy would not change. That is the reversible limit, and also the reason heat stops flowing once two bodies reach the same temperature.

Always add up the entropy changes of everything involved. One part can go down as long as the total does not.

Systems that are not isolated

Entropy can fall locally.

  • The entropy of a system that is not isolated can decrease
  • The entropy of its surroundings then rises by at least as much

Water freezing

Liquid to ordered crystal

The latent heat it gives out raises the entropy of the colder air

A refrigerator

Cools its contents

It rejects more thermal energy to the room than it removes

A living thing

Builds order from food

It gives out heat and waste of higher entropy

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

A freezing pond, with numbers. 1.0 kg of water freezes at 273 K, giving out \(3.34 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{J}\) to air at 263 K. The water’s entropy changes by \(\frac{-3.34 \times 10^{5}}{273} = -1223 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\). The air’s changes by \(\frac{+3.34 \times 10^{5}}{263} = +1270 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\). The total is \(+47 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\): the water has become more ordered, and the universe less.

Life does not break the second law. A growing plant or animal is a highly ordered arrangement of atoms. It is not isolated. It takes in low-entropy energy, sunlight or food, and gives back heat at a low temperature. The entropy it exports is far greater than the entropy it loses.

When a question says entropy has decreased, look for what is outside the system. The answer is always there.

Heat engines.

Turning thermal energy into work, and the limit that no design can beat

B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

The story · Paris, 1824

Background story · not assessed

Carnot’s question.

  • Steam engines had been pumping mines since 1712, improved by trial and error
  • Carnot, a young army engineer, asked: is there a limit to how good an engine can be?
  • His answer, in 1824: yes, and it depends only on the two temperatures
  • He paid to print 600 copies. He died of cholera at 36
The limit came before the first and second laws were known.

Work from thermal energy

Heat engines.

\(\eta = \dfrac{\text{useful work}}{\text{input energy}} = \dfrac{W}{Q_\mathrm{h}}\)
  • Takes in \(Q_\mathrm{h}\) from a hot reservoir
  • Does work \(W\), using a gas taken round a cycle
  • Rejects \(Q_\mathrm{c}\) to a cold reservoir
  • \(W = Q_\mathrm{h} - Q_\mathrm{c}\)
ENERGY FLOW IN ONE CYCLE
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Why some heat must be thrown away. Over a cycle the gas returns to its starting state, so its entropy returns to its starting value. But it gained entropy when it took in \(Q_\mathrm{h}\). It must get rid of that entropy before the next cycle, and the only way to remove entropy is to give out thermal energy. That is \(Q_\mathrm{c}\). An engine with no cold reservoir would break the Kelvin form of the second law.

A worked case. An engine takes in 2000 J and rejects 1400 J each cycle. \(W = 2000 - 1400 = 600 \,\mathrm{J}\), and \(\eta = \frac{600}{2000} = 0.30\). Equivalently, \(\eta = 1 - \frac{Q_\mathrm{c}}{Q_\mathrm{h}}\).

A reservoir is a body so large that its temperature does not change when it gives out or takes in the heat: the furnace gases, the atmosphere, a river.

The best possible engine

The Carnot cycle.

  • A → B: isothermal expansion at \(T_\mathrm{h}\), taking in \(Q_\mathrm{h}\)
  • B → C: adiabatic expansion, cooling to \(T_\mathrm{c}\)
  • C → D: isothermal compression at \(T_\mathrm{c}\), rejecting \(Q_\mathrm{c}\)
  • D → A: adiabatic compression, warming to \(T_\mathrm{h}\)
TWO ISOTHERMALS, TWO ADIABATICS
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Why these four steps. Entropy is created whenever heat flows across a temperature difference. To waste nothing, heat must enter the gas only while the gas is at the temperature of the hot reservoir, and leave only while it is at the temperature of the cold one: two isothermal steps. To get the gas from one temperature to the other without any heat flow at all, the other two steps must be adiabatic.

Why nobody builds one. A reversible isothermal step would have to be infinitely slow, so a true Carnot engine would deliver its work at zero power. It is a yardstick, not a design: every real engine is compared with it.

Heat is exchanged only on the two apricot curves. On the two terracotta curves \(Q = 0\).

The limit

The Carnot efficiency.

\(\eta_\mathrm{Carnot} = 1 - \dfrac{T_\mathrm{c}}{T_\mathrm{h}}\)
  • The greatest efficiency possible between two reservoirs
  • Depends only on their temperatures, in kelvin
  • Real engines always do worse
Steam at 800 K · cooling water at 300 K \(\eta_\mathrm{Carnot} = 1 - \dfrac{T_\mathrm{c}}{T_\mathrm{h}} = 1 - \dfrac{300}{800} = \class{res}{0.63}\) A real engine: 2000 J in, 600 J of work \(\eta = \dfrac{W}{Q_\mathrm{h}} = \dfrac{600}{2000} = \class{res}{0.30}\)
B.4 THERMODYNAMICS · HL ONLY

Where the limit comes from. In the reversible Carnot cycle the total entropy does not change. The hot reservoir loses \(\frac{Q_\mathrm{h}}{T_\mathrm{h}}\) and the cold one gains \(\frac{Q_\mathrm{c}}{T_\mathrm{c}}\), so these are equal: \(\frac{Q_\mathrm{c}}{Q_\mathrm{h}} = \frac{T_\mathrm{c}}{T_\mathrm{h}}\). Put that into \(\eta = 1 - \frac{Q_\mathrm{c}}{Q_\mathrm{h}}\). Any real engine creates extra entropy, has to reject more heat to carry it away, and so does less work.

How to make an engine better. Raise \(T_\mathrm{h}\) or lower \(T_\mathrm{c}\). The cold reservoir is the air or a river, near 300 K, and cannot be changed much. So engineers push the hot temperature as high as materials allow. This is why power stations use steam at very high temperature and pressure, and why even the best turn well under two thirds of their heat into electricity.

Using °C. With 527 °C and 27 °C the formula gives 0.95: quite wrong. Convert to kelvin first.

An efficiency of 1 would need \(T_\mathrm{c} = 0 \,\mathrm{K}\), which cannot be reached.

The story · Zürich, 1865

Background story · not assessed

Clausius names entropy.

  • In 1850 Clausius put Carnot’s result together with Joule’s: energy is conserved, and heat will not flow uphill
  • In 1865 he found the quantity that measures this, \(\frac{\Delta Q}{T}\), and named it entropy
  • He built the word from the Greek for “transformation”, to sound like “energy”
His summary: the energy of the universe is constant; its entropy tends to a maximum.
