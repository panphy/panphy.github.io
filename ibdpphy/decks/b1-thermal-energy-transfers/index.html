The molecular model

Solids, liquids and gases.

  • The same particles in all three phases
  • What changes: their spacing, their motion and how strongly the forces between them act
Forces hold a solid in shape, hold a liquid together and are negligible in a gas.
THE SAME PARTICLES, THREE ARRANGEMENTS
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

What the model explains. A solid keeps its shape because each particle is held in place by its neighbours and can only vibrate. A liquid flows, but keeps its volume, because the particles are still touching and still attract each other, yet are free to change places. A gas fills any container because its particles are so far apart that the forces between them hardly act: each one flies in a straight line until it hits something.

Why liquids and solids are hard to squash. Their particles are already in contact. A gas is mostly empty space, so it can be compressed easily: in air, the molecules are about ten diameters apart.

Drawing the particles of a liquid far apart. A liquid is only slightly less dense than the solid: nearly all the particles still touch.

Mass per unit volume

Density.

\(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}\)
  • Unit: \(\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m}^{-3}\)
  • Solids and liquids: particles touching, similar densities
  • Gases: about a thousand times less dense
An aluminium block: 5.0 cm × 4.0 cm × 2.0 cm · mass 108 g \(V = 0.050 \times 0.040 \times 0.020 = 4.0 \times 10^{-5} \,\mathrm{m}^{3}\) \(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{0.108}{4.0 \times 10^{-5}}\) \(\rho = \class{res}{2.7 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m}^{-3}}\)
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

The factor of a thousand. Water has a density of 1000 kg m⁻³ and steam at 100 °C about 0.6 kg m⁻³. The molecules are the same, so in steam each one has about 1700 times as much space. The cube root of 1700 is about 12: on average the molecules of the gas are twelve times further apart than in the liquid.

Water is unusual. Ice (917 kg m⁻³) is less dense than liquid water, because its molecules lock into an open pattern. For nearly every other substance the solid is the denser phase and sinks in its own liquid.

Converting cm³ to m³ by dividing by 100. There are \(100^{3} = 10^{6}\) cubic centimetres in a cubic metre, so \(1 \,\mathrm{g} \,\mathrm{cm}^{-3} = 1000 \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{m}^{-3}\).

Two scales with the same step

Kelvin and Celsius.

\(T / \mathrm{K} = \theta / {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 273\)
  • The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero
  • One kelvin is the same size as one degree Celsius
  • So a temperature change is the same number on both scales
THE TWO SCALES
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

When you must use kelvin. Any equation with \(T\) itself in it needs the absolute temperature: \(\bar{E}_\mathrm{k} = \frac{3}{2} k_\mathrm{B} T\), the gas laws, \(L = \sigma A T^{4}\) and Wien’s law. Doubling the temperature of a gas from 20 °C does not mean heating it to 40 °C: it means going from 293 K to 586 K, which is 313 °C.

When either will do. \(Q = mc \Delta T\) and the conduction equation contain only a difference of temperatures, and a difference is the same on both scales. Heating water from 20 °C to 50 °C is a rise of 30 °C and of 30 K.

There is no degree sign with the kelvin: write 300 K, not 300 °K. And no temperature in kelvin is ever negative.

What temperature measures

Temperature and kinetic energy.

\(\bar{E}_\mathrm{k} = \dfrac{3}{2} k_\mathrm{B} T\)
  • \(\bar{E}_\mathrm{k}\): the average random kinetic energy of one particle
  • \(T\) must be in kelvin
  • \(k_\mathrm{B} = 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\), the Boltzmann constant
A molecule of air in a room at 27 °C \(T = 27 + 273 = 300 \,\mathrm{K}\) \(\bar{E}_\mathrm{k} = \dfrac{3}{2} k_\mathrm{B} T = \dfrac{3}{2} \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 300\) \(\bar{E}_\mathrm{k} = \class{res}{6.2 \times 10^{-21} \,\mathrm{J}}\)
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Same temperature, same kinetic energy. The equation has no mass in it. In air at 300 K a light hydrogen molecule and a heavy carbon dioxide molecule have the same average kinetic energy. The lighter one must therefore be moving faster: hydrogen at about 1900 m s⁻¹, carbon dioxide at about 400 m s⁻¹.

Why “average”. At any instant some molecules are almost at rest and a few are moving several times faster than the typical speed. They exchange energy at every collision. Temperature describes the whole crowd, not one member of it: a single molecule does not have a temperature.

Absolute zero. Put \(T = 0\) in the equation and the random kinetic energy is zero. That is why nothing can be colder: there is no motion left to remove.

The energies are tiny, so they are often given in electronvolts: \(6.2 \times 10^{-21} \,\mathrm{J}\) is about 0.04 eV.

B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

A cup and a bath. A cup of water at 80 °C is hotter than a bath at 40 °C: each of its molecules has more kinetic energy on average. But the bath has far more internal energy, because it has thousands of times as many molecules. Temperature tells you which way energy will flow. Internal energy tells you how much there is.

Why “random”. Throw a ball and all its molecules gain the same extra velocity. That ordered motion is the ball’s ordinary kinetic energy and does not make the ball hotter. Internal energy counts only the disordered motion of the molecules relative to each other.

Energy without a temperature rise. When ice melts, the energy supplied pulls the molecules out of their fixed places against the forces between them. The potential energy rises while the kinetic energy, and so the temperature, stays the same.

An ideal gas has no forces between its molecules, so its internal energy is kinetic only: \(U = \frac{3}{2} N k_\mathrm{B} T\) in B.3.

Which way the energy goes

Thermal energy flows from hot to cold.

  • It stops when the temperatures are equal: thermal equilibrium

B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Why hot to cold. Where the two blocks touch, fast molecules of the hot block collide with slow molecules of the cold one. In a collision between a fast and a slow particle the fast one usually loses energy and the slow one gains it. Energy also passes the other way in some collisions, which is why the slide says “resultant”: more goes from hot to cold than from cold to hot.

At equilibrium nothing has stopped. The molecules are still colliding and still exchanging energy across the boundary, but now as much goes one way as the other.

Why not half way. On the graph the cold block warms much more than the hot block cools. It has the smaller mass or the smaller specific heat capacity, so the same energy changes its temperature more. Part 2 puts numbers to this.

“Heat flows until both have the same energy.” It is the temperatures that become equal. The two bodies usually end with very different internal energies.

How much energy it takes to warm a substance, and to melt or boil it

Energy to change the temperature

Specific heat capacity.

\(Q = mc \Delta T\)
  • \(c\): the energy to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 K
  • Unit: \(\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\)
  • A property of the substance, and of its phase
Substance (approximate values)c / J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
Water4200
Ice2100
Aluminium900
Copper390
Lead130
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Water is the odd one out. It takes about ten times as much energy to warm a kilogram of water by one kelvin as a kilogram of copper. That is why the sea warms and cools so much more slowly than the land, why water is the usual coolant in engines and power stations, and why a hot-water bottle stays warm for hours.

Why metals are so low. A kilogram of lead contains far fewer atoms than a kilogram of aluminium, because each lead atom is nearly eight times heavier. Each atom takes about the same energy to speed up, so the substance with fewer atoms per kilogram needs less energy per kilogram.

\(\Delta T\) is a difference, so °C and K give the same answer here. Do not add 273.

“Specific” means “per unit mass”. The heat capacity of a whole object, \(mc\), is in J K⁻¹.

Worked example

Boiling a kettle.

1.2 kg of water at 18 °C is heated to 100 °C by a 2.0 kW element \(c = 4200 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\) \(Q = mc \Delta T\) \(Q = 1.2 \times 4200 \times (100 - 18)\) \(Q = \class{res}{4.1 \times 10^{5} \,\mathrm{J}}\)
The time it takes \(P = \dfrac{Q}{t}\)  so  \(t = \dfrac{Q}{P}\) \(t = \dfrac{4.1 \times 10^{5}}{2.0 \times 10^{3}}\) \(t = \class{res}{2.1 \times 10^{2} \,\mathrm{s}}\)
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

What was assumed. That every joule from the element went into the water. In a real kettle some warms the kettle itself and some escapes to the room, so the measured time is a little longer than three and a half minutes. If a question gives an efficiency, say 90%, the energy supplied must be \(\frac{4.1 \times 10^{5}}{0.90}\).

A sense of scale. 410 kJ would lift the same 1.2 kg of water to a height of 35 km. Warming things takes a great deal of energy compared with moving them, which is why heating dominates household energy bills.

Using the final temperature, 100, in place of the rise, 82.

An electrical method

Measuring specific heat capacity.

1.0 kg block · 48 W heater for 300 s · rise of 15 K \(Pt = mc \Delta T\) \(c = \dfrac{Pt}{m \Delta T} = \dfrac{48 \times 300}{1.0 \times 15}\) \(c = \class{res}{960 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}}\)
Accepted value for aluminium: 900. Energy lost to the room makes the result too high.
A HEATED METAL BLOCK
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Why too high, not too low. The calculation credits the block with all 14 400 J. In fact some of that energy leaked away, so the block received less and its temperature rose by less than it should have. A smaller \(\Delta T\) on the bottom of the fraction gives a larger \(c\). Whenever energy escapes, this method overestimates.

Reducing the error. Insulate the block. Put a drop of oil in the thermometer hole so that the thermometer reads the temperature of the metal. Keep watching after switching off and record the highest temperature reached, because the energy takes time to spread through the block. Better still, start a few degrees below room temperature and finish the same amount above it, so that the gains and losses roughly cancel.

A graph of temperature against time for a steady heater has gradient \(\frac{P}{mc}\). Using the gradient of the straight part is better than using one pair of readings.

Worked example

Hot copper into cold water.

0.20 kg of copper at 100 °C goes into 0.50 kg of water at 20 °C \(c_\mathrm{Cu} = 390\) · \(c_\mathrm{w} = 4200 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\) · final temperature \(T\) Energy lost by copper = energy gained by water \(m_\mathrm{Cu} c_\mathrm{Cu} (100 - T) = m_\mathrm{w} c_\mathrm{w} (T - 20)\) \(78 (100 - T) = 2100 (T - 20)\)
\(7800 + 42\,000 = 2178\,T\) \(T = \dfrac{49\,800}{2178}\) \(T = \class{res}{23 \,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}\)
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Why so close to 20 °C. Compare the two heat capacities, \(mc\). For the copper it is 78 J K⁻¹; for the water it is 2100 J K⁻¹, twenty-seven times as much. Every joule that leaves the copper cools it twenty-seven times as much as it warms the water. The copper falls 77 K while the water rises 3 K.

Setting it up. Write each temperature change as a positive number: the hot body falls from its starting temperature to \(T\), the cold one rises from its starting temperature to \(T\). Then “energy lost = energy gained” has no minus signs to go wrong.

Check that the answer lies between the two starting temperatures, and nearer to that of the body with the larger \(mc\).

Heating ice until it is steam

Changes of phase.

  • Sloping parts: the temperature rises, \(Q = mc \Delta T\)
  • Flat parts: the phase changes at constant temperature
  • The energy goes into potential energy, not kinetic
  • Cooling runs the graph backwards: condensing and freezing release energy
WATER HEATED AT A STEADY RATE
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

What the energy does on a flat part. During melting, the energy supplied frees the molecules from their fixed places. During boiling, it pulls them right away from each other, against the forces that hold the liquid together. In both cases the separation of the molecules changes, so their potential energy rises. Their average kinetic energy does not, and that is why the thermometer does not move.

Reading the slopes. The heater supplies energy at a steady rate, so a steeper line means a smaller specific heat capacity. Ice and steam both warm about twice as fast as liquid water: their specific heat capacities are about half as large.

The words. Solid to liquid is melting and the reverse is freezing. Liquid to gas is boiling (or evaporation) and the reverse is condensing.

Asked to explain a flat part, say three things: the energy increases the potential energy of the molecules, the kinetic energy is unchanged, so the temperature is constant.

Energy to change the phase

Specific latent heat.

\(Q = mL\)
  • \(L\): the energy to change the phase of 1 kg at constant temperature
  • Unit: \(\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\)
  • Fusion: solid ⇌ liquid. Vaporization: liquid ⇌ gas
WaterL / J kg⁻¹
Fusion, at 0 °C\(3.34 \times 10^{5}\)
Vaporization, at 100 °C\(2.26 \times 10^{6}\)
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Why vaporization takes so much more. Melting only loosens the molecules: they stay in contact, and nearly all the bonds between neighbours survive. Boiling separates them completely, so every bond must be broken. For water that takes nearly seven times as much energy per kilogram.

Why steam scalds. A gram of steam at 100 °C that condenses on your skin releases 2260 J before it has cooled at all. A gram of water at 100 °C cooling to skin temperature releases about 270 J. The same property is put to use in a power station: turning water into steam stores a great deal of energy in it, which the turbine then takes out.

Putting a temperature change into \(Q = mL\). There is none: the whole point is that the temperature stays constant.

“Latent” means hidden: the energy goes in without showing on a thermometer.

Worked example

Ice from the freezer.

0.050 kg of ice at −18 °C becomes water at 20 °C Warm the ice to 0 °C \(Q_{1} = mc_\mathrm{ice} \Delta T = 0.050 \times 2100 \times 18 = 1.9 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{J}\) Melt it \(Q_{2} = mL = 0.050 \times 3.34 \times 10^{5} = 1.67 \times 10^{4} \,\mathrm{J}\) Warm the water to 20 °C \(Q_{3} = mc_\mathrm{w} \Delta T = 0.050 \times 4200 \times 20 = 4.2 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{J}\)
\(Q = Q_{1} + Q_{2} + Q_{3}\) \(Q = \class{res}{2.3 \times 10^{4} \,\mathrm{J}}\)
Nearly three quarters of it is the melting.

One stage for every part of the graph. Sketch the heating curve and mark where the substance starts and ends. Each sloping part it crosses needs an \(mc \Delta T\), with the specific heat capacity of that phase. Each flat part needs an \(mL\). Here the path crosses two slopes and one flat part, so there are three terms.

Why ice cools a drink so well. The ice takes 23 kJ from the drink in all, and 17 kJ of that is absorbed at 0 °C, without the ice getting any warmer. The same mass of cold water at 0 °C would take only 4.2 kJ.

Two ways from liquid to gas

Boiling and evaporation.

Boiling

At one temperature, throughout the liquid

Bubbles of vapour form inside the liquid. The temperature stays at the boiling point while energy is supplied

Evaporation

At any temperature, from the surface

The fastest molecules near the surface escape. Those left behind have less energy on average, so the liquid cools

Both need the specific latent heat of vaporization for every kilogram that changes phase.
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Cooling by evaporation. The molecules in a liquid have a range of speeds. Only the fastest, and only those at the surface and heading outwards, have enough energy to break free of their neighbours. Each one that leaves takes more than its share of energy with it, so the average kinetic energy of the rest falls: the liquid gets colder. This is how sweating cools you, and why you feel cold when you step out of a swimming pool into a breeze.

What speeds it up. A higher temperature (more molecules fast enough to escape), a larger surface (more molecules in a position to escape), and a wind (which carries the escaped molecules away before they fall back in).

Sweating 1 g of water takes about 2400 J from your body: the latent heat is a little larger at skin temperature than at 100 °C.

Particle to particle

Conduction.

  • Particles at the hot end have more kinetic energy
  • They pass it to their neighbours through the forces between them
  • In a metal, free electrons carry energy much further and faster
  • Gases conduct poorly: the molecules are far apart
A BAR BETWEEN HOT AND COLD
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Why metals feel cold. A metal bench and a wooden bench in the same room are at the same temperature, but the metal feels colder. Your hand is warmer than both. The metal conducts energy away from your skin quickly, so your skin temperature drops; the wood conducts so slowly that the layer you touch soon warms up. Your sense of touch reports the rate at which you lose energy, not the temperature of the object.

Why trapped air insulates. Wool, feathers, foam and double glazing all work by holding air still. Air is a very poor conductor, and if it cannot move it cannot carry energy away by convection either.

Good conductors of electricity are good conductors of thermal energy, for the same reason: free electrons. Diamond is a rare exception, an electrical insulator that conducts thermal energy better than copper.

How fast energy is conducted

The rate of conduction.

\(\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = kA \dfrac{\Delta T}{\Delta x}\)
  • \(k\): the thermal conductivity of the material, in \(\mathrm{W} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\)
  • \(A\): the cross-sectional area the energy passes through
  • \(\dfrac{\Delta T}{\Delta x}\): the temperature gradient
Material (approximate values)k / W m⁻¹ K⁻¹
Copper400
Aluminium240
Glass0.80
Brick0.60

B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Each factor makes sense. Twice the area gives twice as many paths for the energy, so twice the rate. Twice the temperature difference pushes energy through twice as fast. Twice the thickness halves the rate: the same temperature difference is spread over twice the distance, so the gradient is half as steep.

The sign. The data booklet writes the equation with the sizes of the quantities. Energy always goes down the temperature gradient, from the hotter face to the colder one.

The same shape as Ohm’s law. A temperature difference drives a flow of energy through a material, just as a potential difference drives a flow of charge through a resistor. A thick layer with a low \(k\) is a large “thermal resistance”. B.5 uses the same idea for electric current.

\(\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}\) is a power, in watts. To find the energy conducted in a given time, multiply by the time in seconds.

Worked example

Energy lost through a window.

Glass: area 1.5 m² · thickness 4.0 mm · \(k = 0.80 \,\mathrm{W} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1} \,\mathrm{K}^{-1}\) Its two faces are at 12 °C and 7.0 °C \(\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = kA \dfrac{\Delta T}{\Delta x}\) \(\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = 0.80 \times 1.5 \times \dfrac{12 - 7.0}{4.0 \times 10^{-3}}\) \(\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \class{res}{1.5 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{W}}\)
  • In one hour: \(1500 \times 3600 = 5.4 \times 10^{6} \,\mathrm{J}\)
  • Glass twice as thick: half the rate
  • Two panes with still air between: far less
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

The temperatures are those of the glass. The room might be at 20 °C and the air outside at 0 °C, but the glass faces are at 12 °C and 7 °C. A thin layer of nearly still air clings to each side of the pane and, being a poor conductor, takes most of the 20 K difference. The equation needs the temperature difference across the material itself.

Why double glazing works. A 16 mm gap of still air has a conductivity 30 times smaller than glass and is four times thicker than the pane, so for the same temperature difference it conducts over a hundred times less. The gap must be narrow enough that the air inside cannot set up a convection current.

Leaving the thickness in millimetres. 4.0 mm is \(4.0 \times 10^{-3}\) m.

Carried by a moving fluid · described, not calculated

Convection.

  • The heated fluid expands: it becomes less dense
  • Less dense fluid rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace it
  • The fluid itself moves, carrying its energy with it
  • Not possible in a solid
A LIQUID HEATED FROM BELOW
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

It is a buoyancy effect. From A.2, a body in a fluid feels an upward buoyancy force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. A parcel of warm fluid displaces cooler fluid of the same volume but greater density, so the buoyancy force on it is larger than its weight and it accelerates upwards. Convection therefore needs gravity: in an orbiting spacecraft a candle flame is a dim sphere, because the hot gas does not rise away from it.

Where you meet it. Sea breezes: by day the land warms faster than the sea, air rises over the land and cooler air moves in from the sea. A room heater is placed low, and the element of a kettle at the bottom, so that the whole fluid circulates. Inside the Sun, the outer third of the radius carries energy to the surface by convection.

“Heat rises.” Thermal energy has no direction of its own. It is the warm, less dense fluid that rises.

B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

“Black” is about absorbing, not about looking dark. A body that absorbs every wavelength reflects nothing, so at room temperature it looks black. Heat it and the same surface glows: it is now emitting visible light of its own. The Sun is a very good black body and is anything but dark.

Why a good absorber must be a good emitter. Put a black object and a shiny one in an oven and wait. Both end at the temperature of the oven and stay there. The black one absorbs more of the radiation falling on it, so to stay at a steady temperature it must also emit more. If it did not, it would keep getting hotter than its surroundings by itself.

Net transfer. You emit about 500 W of infrared, but the walls of the room radiate almost as much back to you. What matters is the difference. In a cold room, or under a clear night sky, less comes back and you feel cold even in still air.

B.2 measures how far a real surface falls short of a black body with its emissivity, a number between 0 and 1.

The power a black body radiates

The Stefan–Boltzmann law.

\(L = \sigma A T^{4}\)
  • \(L\): the luminosity, the total power radiated, in watts
  • \(A\): the surface area of the body
  • \(\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \,\mathrm{W} \,\mathrm{m}^{-2} \,\mathrm{K}^{-4}\)
The Sun: radius \(6.96 \times 10^{8} \,\mathrm{m}\) · surface at 5800 K \(A = 4 \pi R^{2} = 4 \pi \times (6.96 \times 10^{8})^{2} = 6.09 \times 10^{18} \,\mathrm{m}^{2}\) \(L = \sigma A T^{4}\) \(L = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 6.09 \times 10^{18} \times 5800^{4}\) \(L = \class{res}{3.9 \times 10^{26} \,\mathrm{W}}\)

The fourth power is steep. Double the kelvin temperature and the power goes up by \(2^{4} = 16\). A star only 20% hotter than the Sun, with the same radius, would be twice as luminous, since \(1.2^{4} = 2.1\). This is why a small change in the temperature of a planet makes a noticeable change in the energy it radiates to space, which matters in B.2.

Two ways to be luminous. \(L\) depends on area as well as temperature. A red giant is cool, about 3500 K, but so enormous that it outshines the Sun thousands of times over. A white dwarf is very hot but the size of the Earth, and faint. E.5 uses this equation to find the radius of a star from its luminosity and temperature.

Using °C. With a fourth power the error is huge: \(293^{4}\) is about 46 000 times \(20^{4}\).

A star’s luminosity may be given in units of the Sun’s, \(L_{\odot}\).

The story · Vienna, 1879

Background story · not assessed

Stefan takes the temperature of the Sun.

  • In 1879 Stefan studied other people’s measurements of hot bodies cooling, and spotted the pattern: power ∝ \(T^{4}\)
  • He used it on sunlight and found the Sun’s surface to be about 5430 °C: the first sensible value
  • Today’s value is about 5500 °C
  • In 1884 his former student Ludwig Boltzmann derived the law from theory

What reaches the observer

Apparent brightness.

\(b = \dfrac{L}{4 \pi d^{2}}\)
  • \(b\): the power received per unit area, in \(\mathrm{W} \,\mathrm{m}^{-2}\)
  • The power \(L\) spreads over a sphere of area \(4 \pi d^{2}\)
  • An inverse square law: twice as far, a quarter as bright
THE SAME POWER, A LARGER SPHERE
B.1 THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFERS · SL + HL

Luminosity and brightness are different things. Luminosity belongs to the star: it is how much power the star gives out, and it is the same wherever you stand. Apparent brightness belongs to the observer: it is how much of that power lands on each square metre where you are. A faint-looking star may be a feeble one nearby or a brilliant one far away. You cannot tell which without knowing the distance.

Where the sphere comes from. The star radiates equally in all directions and nothing is absorbed on the way. By the time the light has travelled a distance \(d\), all of it is passing through the surface of an imaginary sphere of radius \(d\) centred on the star. Share the power equally over that surface and you have the brightness.

The same inverse square pattern appears for gravitational and electric field strength in D.1 and D.2, and for the same geometric reason.

Confusing \(A\) in \(L = \sigma A T^{4}\) (the surface of the star, radius \(R\)) with \(4 \pi d^{2}\) (a sphere reaching to the observer).

Worked example

Sunlight at the Earth.

\(L = 3.9 \times 10^{26} \,\mathrm{W}\) · Earth–Sun distance \(d = 1.50 \times 10^{11} \,\mathrm{m}\) \(b = \dfrac{L}{4 \pi d^{2}}\) \(b = \dfrac{3.9 \times 10^{26}}{4 \pi \times (1.50 \times 10^{11})^{2}}\) \(b = \class{res}{1.4 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{W} \,\mathrm{m}^{-2}}\)
  • This is the solar constant, \(S\): 1.36 × 10³ W m⁻² in the data booklet
  • At Mars, 1.5 times as far: \(\dfrac{1}{1.5^{2}} = 0.44\) of this

Working backwards. Astronomers do this calculation in reverse. The brightness of sunlight can be measured directly, and the distance to the Sun is known, so \(L = 4 \pi d^{2} b\) gives the Sun’s luminosity. With the Sun’s radius, the Stefan–Boltzmann law then gives its surface temperature. That chain of reasoning is what Stefan used in 1879.

What the number means. 1.4 kW falls on every square metre facing the Sun at the top of the atmosphere: about the power of a small electric heater. Less reaches the ground, because some is reflected and absorbed on the way down. B.2 starts from this number to work out the temperature of the Earth.

Reading the graph. The curves never cross: a hotter black body emits more than a cooler one at every wavelength. The area under a curve is the total power from each square metre, \(\sigma T^{4}\), so the 6000 K curve encloses five times the area of the 4000 K one, since \(1.5^{4} = 5.1\).

Colour as a thermometer. A piece of iron in a fire glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white as it heats, because the peak is moving from the infrared towards the visible. Stars work the same way: Betelgeuse, at about 3500 K, is red, the Sun is yellow-white, and Rigel, at about 12 000 K, is blue-white. Measuring the peak wavelength of a star gives its surface temperature without going there.

The shape is not symmetric. The curve falls sharply on the short-wavelength side and has a long tail on the other. A body at 4000 K emits a little blue light, but it emits far more infrared than a quick look at the peak suggests.

Reading “m K” as millikelvin. It is the metre kelvin: wavelength in metres times temperature in kelvin.

Worked example

A temperature from a peak wavelength.

Sunlight is most intense at 500 nm \(\lambda_\mathrm{max} T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3} \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{K}\) \(T = \dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{\lambda_\mathrm{max}} = \dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{500 \times 10^{-9}}\) \(T = \class{res}{5.8 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{K}}\)
A lamp filament at 2800 K \(\lambda_\mathrm{max} = \dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{T} = \dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{2800}\) \(\lambda_\mathrm{max} = \class{res}{1.0 \times 10^{-6} \,\mathrm{m}}\) In the infrared: most of its output is not light

Why filament lamps were replaced. The filament’s peak is at 1000 nm, beyond the red end of the visible spectrum at about 700 nm. Only the short-wavelength edge of its curve falls in the visible, so roughly 95% of the electrical energy leaves as infrared. An LED emits only in the visible and needs about a tenth of the power for the same light.

You are a black body too. Skin at 305 K has \(\lambda_\mathrm{max} = \frac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{305} = 9.5 \times 10^{-6}\) m, far into the infrared. Thermal cameras are built to detect wavelengths around 10 μm for this reason. The Earth’s surface, at about 288 K, peaks at almost the same wavelength, which is the starting point for the greenhouse effect in B.2.

Wavelengths are usually given in nm or μm. Convert to metres before using the constant.
