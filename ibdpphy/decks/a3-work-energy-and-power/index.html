A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Why energy is so useful

Forces and accelerations change from moment to moment and have directions. Energy is a single number that stays the same from start to finish. If you know the energy of a system at the beginning and can account for what enters and leaves, you know its energy at the end, without following the motion in between. Many problems that are long with Newton’s laws take two lines with energy.

Choosing the system

“Conserved” applies to a system that nothing enters or leaves. A falling ball alone is not isolated: the Earth pulls on it. The ball and the Earth together are, so their total energy is constant. Whenever energy seems to appear or vanish, the system has been drawn too small.

Energy is a scalar, measured in joules. There are no components to resolve, which is much of its appeal.

Transferring energy

Work done by a force.

\(W = Fs \cos \theta\)
  • For a constant force
  • \(\theta\): the angle between the force and the displacement
  • Work done = energy transferred. Unit: \(1 \,\mathrm{J} = 1 \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{m}\)
PULLING A BLOCK
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Two ways to read the equation

\((F \cos \theta) \times s\): the component of the force along the motion, times the distance moved. Or \(F \times (s \cos \theta)\): the whole force, times the distance moved in the direction of the force. They give the same number. Use whichever is easier to see in the diagram.

What the rest of the force does

The component at right angles to the motion, \(F \sin \theta\), does no work. In the diagram it reduces the normal force, but the block stays on the table: it moves no distance in the direction of this component.

Work in physics is not effort

Holding a heavy bag still, you tire, but the force on the bag moves through no distance, so no work is done on the bag. Your muscles are transferring energy internally, which is a different matter.

Leaving out \(\cos \theta\) when the force is not along the motion, or using \(\sin \theta\) because the angle was given from the vertical.

The sign of the work

Positive, negative and zero work.

\(\theta\) less than 90°

Positive work

The force has a component along the motion. It gives energy to the body

Example: gravity on a falling ball

\(\theta\) equal to 90°

No work

The force is at right angles to the motion

Examples: the normal force on a sliding block, the centripetal force in a circle

\(\theta\) more than 90°

Negative work

The force has a component against the motion. It takes energy from the body

Example: friction, \(\cos 180^{\circ} = -1\)

A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Why a satellite needs no engine

In a circular orbit, gravity is always at right angles to the velocity. It does no work, so the satellite’s kinetic energy never changes, and it can orbit for centuries without fuel. The same is true of the tension in the string of a conical pendulum and of the magnetic force on a moving charge.

Negative work is still a transfer

When friction does −50 J of work on a sliding box, the box loses 50 J of kinetic energy. That energy has not gone: it has been transferred to thermal energy of the box and the floor.

Total work on a body = the sum of the work done by each force = the work done by the resultant force.

Worked example

Pulling a sledge across snow.

Rope at \(30^{\circ}\) to the ground · tension \(F = 120 \,\mathrm{N}\) The sledge moves \(s = 25 \,\mathrm{m}\) \(W = Fs \cos \theta\) \(W = 120 \times 25 \times \cos 30^{\circ}\) \(W = \class{res}{2.6 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{J}}\)
Friction 104 N, at constant velocity \(W_\mathrm{f} = F_\mathrm{f} s \cos 180^{\circ}\) \(W_\mathrm{f} = 104 \times 25 \times (-1) = \class{res}{-2.6 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{J}}\) Total work: zero
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Why the total is zero

At constant velocity the resultant force is zero, so the forward component of the tension, \(120 \cos 30^{\circ} = 104 \,\mathrm{N}\), equals the friction. The rope puts 2600 J into the sledge and friction takes 2600 J out. The kinetic energy of the sledge does not change, and the energy ends up as thermal energy in the snow and the runners.

The forces that do nothing

The weight and the normal force are both at right angles to the motion and do no work, however large they are.

Set your calculator to degrees, and check with a value you know: \(\cos 60^{\circ} = 0.5\).

When the force is not constant

Work from a force–displacement graph.

  • Work done = the area under a force–displacement graph
  • Constant force: a rectangle, \(F \times s\)
  • Varying force: count squares, or split into simple shapes
  • Use the component of force along the displacement
A VARYING FORCE

Why the area

Cut the displacement into thin strips. Across one strip the force hardly changes, so the work done is the height of the strip times its width: its area. Adding the strips gives the whole area under the curve. The same argument gave displacement from a velocity–time graph in A.1 and impulse from a force–time graph in A.2.

Check the axes first

Area under force against displacement is work, in joules. Area under force against time is impulse, in newton seconds. The two graphs can look alike and mean quite different things.

Counting squares: work out what one square is worth, for example \(5 \,\mathrm{N} \times 0.2 \,\mathrm{m} = 1 \,\mathrm{J}\), then count, taking part squares in pairs.

A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Energy of motion

Kinetic energy.

\(E_\mathrm{k} = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} = \dfrac{p^{2}}{2m}\)
  • Depends on the square of the speed
  • A scalar: never negative, whatever the direction
  • \(p = mv\) is the momentum
A resultant force \(F\) acts over a distance \(s\), from rest \(W = Fs = mas\) \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\)  with  \(u = 0\):  \(as = \dfrac{v^{2}}{2}\) \(W = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}\)
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

What the square means

Double the speed and the kinetic energy is four times as large. A car at 100 km h⁻¹ has four times the kinetic energy it has at 50 km h⁻¹, and with the same braking force it needs four times the distance to stop. Speed matters far more than mass.

The momentum form

Put \(v = \frac{p}{m}\) into \(\frac{1}{2} m v^{2}\) and you get \(\frac{p^{2}}{2m}\). It is the quickest route when a question gives momentum, as after a collision or an explosion. Two bodies with the same momentum do not have the same kinetic energy: the lighter one has more. That is why the bullet, not the rifle, carries nearly all the energy.

Squaring the whole of \(\frac{1}{2} m v\). Only the speed is squared.

Work done by the resultant force

Work and the change in kinetic energy.

  • The work done by the resultant force equals the change in kinetic energy
  • \(F s = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} - \dfrac{1}{2} m u^{2}\)
  • No times, no accelerations: just the start and the end
A 1200 kg car at \(20 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) · braking force 6000 N Work done by the brakes = kinetic energy lost \(F s = \dfrac{1}{2} m u^{2}\)  so  \(s = \dfrac{m u^{2}}{2F}\) \(s = \dfrac{1200 \times 20^{2}}{2 \times 6000} = \class{res}{40 \,\mathrm{m}}\)
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

The same answer the long way

With Newton’s second law: \(a = \frac{6000}{1200} = 5.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\), then \(v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as\) gives \(s = \frac{20^{2}}{2 \times 5.0} = 40 \,\mathrm{m}\). The energy method reaches it in one step, and it still works when the force is not constant, where the equations of motion do not.

Why braking distance goes as speed squared

From \(s = \frac{m u^{2}}{2F}\): at 40 m s⁻¹ the same car needs 160 m. Where the energy goes: the brake discs get hot. The brakes have done −240 kJ of work on the car, and 240 kJ of thermal energy has appeared.

Use the resultant force. If the car were also on a slope, the component of its weight along the slope would do work too.

Energy of position

Gravitational potential energy.

\(\Delta E_\mathrm{p} = mg \Delta h\)
  • The work done against gravity in raising a mass
  • Only the change in height matters, not the route
  • Valid close to the Earth’s surface, where \(g\) is constant
TWO ROUTES TO THE SAME HEIGHT
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Why the route does not matter

Lifted straight up, the block needs a force \(mg\) over a distance \(\Delta h\): work \(mg \Delta h\). On a smooth ramp at angle \(\theta\), the force needed is smaller, \(mg \sin \theta\), but the distance along the ramp is longer, \(\frac{\Delta h}{\sin \theta}\). Multiply them and \(\sin \theta\) cancels: \(mg \Delta h\) again. A ramp trades force for distance; it does not save energy.

Why only changes

The equation gives a change in potential energy, not an amount. You may choose any level as zero, the floor or the bench or the ground outside, because only differences in height affect the motion. Choose the lowest point in the problem and every value is positive.

Far from the surface \(g\) is not constant, and \(E_\mathrm{p} = -\frac{G M m}{r}\) must be used: D.1, higher level.

Energy stored in a spring

Elastic potential energy.

\(E_\mathrm{H} = \dfrac{1}{2} k (\Delta x)^{2}\)
  • \(k\): spring constant. \(\Delta x\): extension or compression
  • The force grows from 0 to \(k \Delta x\): the average force is half of that
  • The same for stretching and for squashing
FORCE AGAINST EXTENSION
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Where the half comes from

The force needed to stretch a spring is not constant. It starts at zero and rises steadily to \(k \Delta x\) (Hooke’s law, A.2). The work done is the area under the graph, a triangle: \(\frac{1}{2} \times \Delta x \times k \Delta x\). Or think of the average force, \(\frac{1}{2} k \Delta x\), acting over the distance \(\Delta x\).

The square again

Double the extension and the stored energy is four times as large. Pulling a bowstring back twice as far stores four times the energy.

Using \(F \times \Delta x\) with the final force. That gives twice the right answer: it is the rectangle, not the triangle.

\(\Delta x\) is measured from the natural length of the spring, not from wherever it happened to start.

Worked example

A spring launcher.

\(k = 200 \,\mathrm{N} \,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\) · \(\Delta x = 0.15 \,\mathrm{m}\) · ball 0.050 kg \(E_\mathrm{H} = \dfrac{1}{2} k (\Delta x)^{2}\) \(E_\mathrm{H} = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 200 \times 0.15^{2} = \class{res}{2.25 \,\mathrm{J}}\) \(\dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} = E_\mathrm{H}\)  so  \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 E_\mathrm{H}}{m}}\) \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 2.25}{0.050}} = \class{res}{9.5 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)

Why energy is the only sensible method here

The force of the spring on the ball falls as the spring expands, so the acceleration is not constant and the equations of motion cannot be used. Energy does not care how the force varies on the way: all 2.25 J stored in the spring ends up as kinetic energy of the ball, if friction and the mass of the spring are ignored.

Fired straight up instead

The same 2.25 J would become gravitational potential energy at the top: \(\Delta h = \frac{E_\mathrm{H}}{mg} = \frac{2.25}{0.050 \times 9.8} = 4.6 \,\mathrm{m}\) above the release point.

Compress the spring twice as far and the ball leaves at twice the speed: the energy is four times as large, and speed goes as the square root of energy.

A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Without resistive forces

Mechanical energy is conserved.

A CART ON A FRICTIONLESS TRACKKINETIC + POTENTIAL = CONSTANT
Total mechanical energy = \(E_\mathrm{k} + E_\mathrm{p} + E_\mathrm{H}\). With no frictional or resistive forces, it stays the same.
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

What to watch

As the cart drops, the teal bar shrinks and the apricot bar grows by exactly the same amount. At the bottom of the dip nearly all the energy is kinetic and the cart is at its fastest. On the second hill, which is lower than the first, the cart still has some kinetic energy left at the top, so it gets over. It could never climb a hill higher than the one it started from.

The speed depends only on the height

The shape of the track between two points makes no difference to the speed at the second one. Two tracks from the same height to the same level, one steep and one gentle, deliver the cart at the same speed, though not after the same time.

Work, in this picture, is the amount of energy changed from one mechanical form to another: gravity does positive work on the way down, turning potential energy into kinetic.

Worked example

A roller coaster drops 20 m.

From rest at the top · no resistive forces Potential energy lost = kinetic energy gained \(mg \Delta h = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}\) \(v = \sqrt{2 g \Delta h} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 20}\) \(v = \class{res}{20 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
  • The mass cancels: every car reaches the same speed
  • The shape of the track does not matter
  • If it starts at \(5.0 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\): \(v = \sqrt{u^{2} + 2 g \Delta h} = 20.4 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\)
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

A method for every problem of this kind

Pick two moments, a start and an end. Write the total mechanical energy at each: kinetic, gravitational and elastic. Set them equal. Cross out anything that is zero or the same on both sides. What is left is usually one line of algebra.

Why starting at 5.0 m s⁻¹ adds so little

Energies add, not speeds. The starting kinetic energy, proportional to \(5.0^{2} = 25\), is small beside the \(2 g \Delta h = 392\) gained in the fall. The final speed is \(\sqrt{25 + 392} = 20.4\), barely more than before.

Using the equations of motion along a curved track. The acceleration is not constant there. Energy is the right tool.

When mechanical energy is not conserved

With frictional and resistive forces.

  • Friction and drag do negative work: mechanical energy falls
  • Change in mechanical energy = work done by the non-conservative forces
  • The “lost” energy becomes thermal energy
Total energy is still conserved.
A 70 kg skier descends 30 m, from rest, and arrives at \(18 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) \(W = \Delta E = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} - mg \Delta h\) \(W = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 70 \times 18^{2} - 70 \times 9.8 \times 30\) \(W = 11\,340 - 20\,580 = \class{res}{-9.2 \times 10^{3} \,\mathrm{J}}\)
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Reading the answer

The skier started with 20 580 J of potential energy and finished with 11 340 J of kinetic energy. The difference, 9240 J, is the work done against friction and air resistance. It is negative because those forces act against the motion and take mechanical energy away.

Finding the force

If the slope is 120 m long, the average resistive force is \(\frac{9240}{120} = 77 \,\mathrm{N}\). Work done by friction depends on the length of the path, unlike the work done by gravity, which depends only on the height. That difference is what “non-conservative” means.

Conservative forces (gravity, the force of a spring) store energy that can be got back. Non-conservative forces (friction, drag) turn it into thermal energy that cannot.

Power and efficiency.

How fast energy is transferred, how much of it is useful, and how much a fuel holds

A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

The rate of doing work

Power.

\(P = \dfrac{\Delta W}{\Delta t} = Fv\)
  • The rate of doing work, or of transferring energy
  • Unit: the watt, \(1 \,\mathrm{W} = 1 \,\mathrm{J} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\)
  • \(P = Fv\) for a force along the velocity
A lift raises 800 kg through 15 m in 12 s \(P = \dfrac{\Delta W}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{mg \Delta h}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{800 \times 9.8 \times 15}{12} = \class{res}{9.8 \,\mathrm{kW}}\) A car at a steady \(30 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) against 600 N of drag \(P = Fv = 600 \times 30 = \class{res}{18 \,\mathrm{kW}}\)
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Where \(P = Fv\) comes from

In a time \(\Delta t\) a body moving at speed \(v\) covers a distance \(v \Delta t\). A force \(F\) along the motion does work \(F v \Delta t\). Divide by \(\Delta t\): \(P = Fv\). It gives the power at one instant, even while the speed is changing.

Why cars have a top speed

At a steady speed the driving force equals the resistive force, and the engine’s power is \(Fv\). Drag rises steeply with speed, roughly as \(v^{2}\), so the power needed rises roughly as \(v^{3}\). Doubling the top speed would take about eight times the power.

Energy = power × time. A kilowatt-hour is 1 kW for one hour: \(1000 \times 3600 = 3.6 \times 10^{6} \,\mathrm{J}\). It is a unit of energy, not of power.

How much is useful?

Efficiency and Sankey diagrams.

\(\eta = \dfrac{E_\mathrm{output}}{E_\mathrm{input}} = \dfrac{P_\mathrm{output}}{P_\mathrm{input}}\)
  • The useful output as a fraction of the total input
  • No unit. Always less than 1
  • Sankey diagram: the width of each arrow shows the energy
A PETROL ENGINE\(\eta = 0.30\)
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Reading a Sankey diagram

Energy flows from left to right. The input arrow is 100 units wide. The useful output carries straight on; the wasted energy peels away, usually downwards. The widths of all the outputs must add up to the width of the input: that is conservation of energy, drawn to scale. Here \(30 + 70 = 100\), and the efficiency is \(\frac{30}{100} = 0.30\), or 30%.

Why it is never 1

Some energy always goes to thermal energy through friction, electrical resistance or hot exhaust gases. That energy still exists, but it has spread out into the surroundings and cannot be gathered up to do useful work. B.4 explains at higher level why engines that run on heat have a limit well below 100%.

When drawing one: choose a scale such as 1 mm for 1 J, keep the widths in proportion and label each arrow with its form of energy.

Giving an efficiency greater than 1, or 100%. If that happens, the input and output have been swapped.

Worked example

The efficiency of an electric motor.

Input power 500 W It lifts 20 kg at a steady \(1.5 \,\mathrm{m} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) \(P_\mathrm{output} = Fv = mgv\) \(P_\mathrm{output} = 20 \times 9.8 \times 1.5 = 294 \,\mathrm{W}\) \(\eta = \dfrac{P_\mathrm{output}}{P_\mathrm{input}} = \dfrac{294}{500} = \class{res}{0.59}\)
  • At a steady speed the lifting force equals the weight
  • As a percentage: 59%
  • Wasted: \(500 - 294 = 206 \,\mathrm{W}\), as thermal energy
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Machines in a chain

When one machine feeds another, the efficiencies multiply. A power station at 0.40, transmission lines at 0.95 and this motor at 0.59 give \(0.40 \times 0.95 \times 0.59 = 0.22\) overall. Less than a quarter of the energy of the fuel ends up as potential energy of the load.

Working backwards

How much power must a motor of efficiency 0.80 take in to deliver 2.0 kW? \(P_\mathrm{input} = \frac{P_\mathrm{output}}{\eta} = \frac{2.0}{0.80} = 2.5 \,\mathrm{kW}\). The input is always the larger number.

Efficiency can be given as a decimal or a percentage. In a calculation, use the decimal.

A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

Why both numbers matter

An aircraft cares most about mass: every kilogram of fuel has to be lifted. A car or a ship cares about volume as well: the fuel has to fit in a tank. Petrol does well on both counts, which is a large part of why it has been so hard to replace.

The trouble with gases

A kilogram of hydrogen releases three times the energy of a kilogram of petrol, but at atmospheric pressure it fills about 11 cubic metres. To be useful as a fuel it must be compressed to hundreds of atmospheres or cooled to a liquid at −253 °C, and both take energy and heavy tanks.

Nuclear fuel

Fission releases energy from the nucleus, not from the electrons, so the scale is different altogether: one kilogram of uranium-235 gives as much as about two thousand tonnes of petrol.

Energy released = specific energy × mass, or energy density × volume. Check which of the two a question gives you by its unit.

Worked example

How much coal does a power station burn?.

Electrical output 500 MW · efficiency 0.35 Specific energy of coal \(30 \,\mathrm{MJ} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\) \(P_\mathrm{input} = \dfrac{P_\mathrm{output}}{\eta} = \dfrac{500}{0.35} = 1430 \,\mathrm{MW}\) Mass each second \(= \dfrac{P_\mathrm{input}}{\text{specific energy}}\) \(= \dfrac{1430 \times 10^{6}}{30 \times 10^{6}} = \class{res}{48 \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\)
  • Power is energy each second, so this gives mass each second
  • In a day: \(48 \times 86\,400 = 4.1 \times 10^{6} \,\mathrm{kg}\)
  • About 4000 tonnes: several long trains of coal, every day
A.3 WORK, ENERGY AND POWER · SL + HL

The order of the steps

Always go from the useful output back to the input first, by dividing by the efficiency. Only then bring in the fuel. Dividing the 500 MW output directly by the specific energy gives 17 kg s⁻¹, the amount an impossible, perfectly efficient station would burn.

The same station on uranium

With \(8 \times 10^{7} \,\mathrm{MJ} \,\mathrm{kg}^{-1}\), the rate would be \(\frac{1430}{8 \times 10^{7}} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5} \,\mathrm{kg} \,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) of uranium-235: about 1.5 kg a day.

Mixing MW with J. Either keep everything in MJ and MW, as here, or convert everything to J and W.
