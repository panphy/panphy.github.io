THE CASE (2011)

The loose cable that broke physics

  • Neutrinos timed over 730 km
  • Arrived ~60 ns before light?
  • Thousands of repeats, very consistent
  • Cause: a loose cable connection
  • Precise, but not accurate
Mission 1: Trust the data?

Why did thousands of repeats not reveal the problem? The loose cable delayed the timing signal by the same amount on every run. Each repeat carried the same mistake, so the results agreed perfectly and the mean was just as wrong.

It was found by checking the equipment, and by a different experiment that timed the neutrinos another way.

Repeating tells you how consistent your readings are. It cannot tell you whether they are right.

What the data can tell you. Precision shows in the numbers themselves: A and C are tightly grouped, B is spread out.

01 Key idea

Precise ≠ accurate

PreciseRepeats close to each other
AccurateClose to the true value
Consistent does not mean correct: a systematic error can shift every reading.
Mission 1: Trust the data?

Each has its own cause. Random error spreads readings out, so it spoils precision. Systematic error moves all the readings together, so it spoils accuracy and leaves the precision untouched.

Why precise-but-wrong is the dangerous one. Scattered results warn you that something is off. A tight group looks trustworthy, so nobody checks it.

How do you check accuracy? Measure something whose value you already know, such as a 100 g mass on the balance.

Mission 1: Trust the data?

Why divide by 2? Largest − smallest is the full width of the spread. The mean sits near the middle, so the readings reach about half that width above it and half below.

What is the ± for? Comparing results. You have 1.22 ± 0.03 s, which covers 1.19 s to 1.25 s. Another group has 1.26 ± 0.03 s, which covers 1.23 s to 1.29 s. The intervals overlap, so the results agree even though the means differ.

01 Two errors

Only one is fixed by repeating

Random error

  • Spreads readings both ways
  • e.g. reaction time with a stopwatch
  • Fix: repeat + mean, light gates

Systematic error

  • Shifts every reading the same way
  • e.g. balance reads 0.2 g when empty (zero error)
  • Fix: check zero, calibrate; repeats don’t help
Mission 1: Trust the data?

How to tell which kind you have. Ask: would this make the reading wrong by the same amount, in the same direction, every time? Yes: systematic. Up one time and down the next: random.

Why does a mean help with one and not the other? Random highs and lows partly cancel when you add them up. A systematic shift has nothing to cancel with: if the balance adds 0.2 g to every reading, it adds 0.2 g to the mean.

A known systematic error can be corrected: subtract 0.2 g from every reading.

01 What can the instrument see?

Resolution

ResolutionThe smallest change an instrument can detect
Ruler, mm marks1 mm
Stopwatch0.01 s
Balance0.1 g
Better resolution → finer readings, but it does not remove a systematic error
Mission 1: Trust the data?

Match the instrument to the job. A millimetre ruler is fine for a desk, where 1 mm is a tiny fraction of the length. It is poor for the thickness of a coin, where 1 mm could be half the measurement.

A way round it: measure many at once. One sheet of paper is too thin for a ruler, but 100 sheets are about 10 mm, so one is about 0.1 mm.

A stopwatch shows hundredths of a second, but your reaction time is about 0.2 s. Timing by hand, the display is far finer than the measurement.

What went wrong, exactly? The groups differed in two ways: what they drank and when they were tested. People are often more alert in the morning, so that group might have scored higher with no drink at all.

02 Take the question apart

Change. Measure. Control.

  • Independent: the one thing I change
  • Dependent: the thing I measure
  • Control: everything I keep the same
  • Change only one thing at a time
How does length affect a pendulum’s period?
Mission 2: Variables & graphs

Why only one change? Suppose you test a longer pendulum and also use a heavier bob. If the swing takes longer, was it the length or the mass? You cannot say.

You control what might matter. The mass of the bob turns out to make almost no difference to the time of a swing. You only know that because someone tested it fairly. Until you have evidence, anything that could plausibly affect the result is kept the same.

“Keep it a fair test” earns nothing. Name the variable and how you keep it the same: “use the same bob each time”.

02 Two kinds of data

Continuous or categoric?

  • Continuous: any value on a scale
  • length, time, temperature, mass
  • Categoric: names or types
  • material, colour, surface, brand
  • Test: is there a value in between?
Mission 2: Variables & graphs

Why does the difference matter? With continuous data, the values in between mean something. If you tested 10 cm and 20 cm, you can ask about 15 cm. There is nothing between sponge and cardboard to ask about.

The same thing can be recorded either way. “Hot, warm, cold” is categoric. “60 °C, 40 °C, 20 °C” is continuous, and tells you much more. When you have the choice, measure.

02 Key rule

Which graph?

RuleContinuous → line graph
Categoric → bar chart

“My results are numbers, so it’s a line graph.”

Check the independent variable.

The dependent variable is usually a number either way.
Mission 2: Variables & graphs

Why a line for continuous data? A line says something about the values between your points, which only makes sense if in-between values exist.

Why bars for categoric data? A line from “sponge” to “cardboard” would suggest something halfway between them. Separate bars show separate things.

Why look at the independent variable? It goes along the x-axis, and that is where the choice shows: a number scale, or a row of labels. The y-axis is a number scale in both.

Which material absorbs the shock?

THE CASE

Why helmets are made of foam

  • Foam is crushed in a crash
  • Head stops over a longer time
  • → smaller force on the skull

Mission 3: The drop test

Why does a longer stopping time help? If the head stops in an instant against something hard, the force is very large. Foam crushes, so the head slows over a longer time and the force is much smaller.

The link to your experiment. A ball that bounces back high has got most of its energy back. A ball that barely bounces has left its energy in the material. So the lowest bounce marks the best shock absorber.

03 Variables first

The drop test

QuestionHow does material affect bounce height?
  • Independent: material (categoric)
  • Dependent: bounce height / cm
  • Control: same ball, drop height, release, ruler position
  • Lowest bounce = best shock absorber
Mission 3: The drop test

Why each control variable matters. A ball dropped from higher lands faster, so it bounces higher on any material. A different ball bounces differently. A push gives the ball extra energy, and never the same amount twice.

State control variables with their values in a plan: “dropped from 50 cm each time”, not just “same height”.

03 Method + safety

How to run it

  1. Choose 4–5 materials
  2. Ruler vertical, 0 cm at the surface
  3. Drop from 50 cm, no push
  4. Read the first bounce at eye level
  5. Repeat: 3 trials per material
  6. Calculate each mean

Safety

  • Drop, never throw
  • Stable surface for materials
  • Keep clear of the drop zone
Odd reading? Check it now, while the kit is out.
Mission 3: The drop test

The reason for each step.

Ruler vertical: a leaning ruler makes every height read too long.

0 cm at the surface: otherwise every height is wrong by the same amount, a zero error.

Eye level: looking down at the ball lines it up with the wrong mark on the ruler behind it.

First bounce only: each bounce is lower than the last, so compare the same one every time.

Why a mean? Each trial is a little off, some high and some low. Those differences partly cancel, so the mean is a better estimate than any single trial.

Use the trials as a check. Look across each row before you calculate. Cardboard reads 30, 32, 28: close together. If one read 52, you would repeat it before working out a mean.

03 Present it

A bar chart that survives a marker

  • Title: what against what
  • Material on the x-axis
  • Mean height / cm on the y-axis

Mission 3: The drop test

Why must the y-axis start at zero? The height of a bar is its value. If the axis started at 10 cm, the rubber mat’s bar would look about nine times taller than the sponge’s, when 38 cm is really about three times 13 cm.

Why gaps between the bars? The materials are separate things. Touching bars would suggest a continuous scale along the bottom.

Does the error change your conclusion? A ruler with its zero 1 cm too high makes every bar 1 cm too short, so the ranking of the materials stays the same. The values are wrong; the order is not. Random error is different: it could swap two materials whose means are close.

04 Set it up

The ramp

  • Independent: ramp height / cm (continuous)
  • Dependent: distance travelled / cm
  • Control: same trolley, ramp, start line, floor
  • 5 heights: 5 → 25 cm
  • 3 trials each → 15 runs

Safety

Stable ramp, clear lane, never aim at people

Mission 4: Ramp line graph

Why five heights? Two points can always be joined by a straight line, so two tell you nothing about the shape. Five show whether the pattern is straight or curved.

Why evenly spaced? Bunched points leave a long stretch where you are only guessing what the line does.

Why would a higher ramp send the trolley further? It is moving faster when it reaches the floor. A reason is what turns a guess into a prediction.

04 Before you plot

Choose the scale first

RuleLargest value ÷ big squares → round up to 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 or 50
ExampleLargest mean 80 cm, 9 big squares → 80 ÷ 9 ≈ 8.9 → use 10 cm per square
Mission 4: Ramp line graph

Why 1, 2, 5 or 10? A big square has ten small squares along each side. If a big square is worth 10, each small square is worth 1. If it is worth 5, each small square is 0.5; if 2, each is 0.2. All are easy to count in. If a big square is worth 3, each small square is 0.3, and plotting 7 means counting 23⅓ small squares.

Why fill the grid? On a bigger graph a small slip of the pencil is a smaller error.

Writing your results along the axis as if they were a scale: 20, 37, 52, 66, 80. An axis goes up in equal steps.

04 Plot it

Plot precisely

  • Independent on x
  • Dependent on y
  • Units on both axes
  • Plot the means
  • Small crosses, not blobs
  • No bars, no dot-to-dot
Mission 4: Ramp line graph

Why crosses and not dots? The centre of a small × is an exact point. A blob covers several small squares.

Why no dot-to-dot? Every point has some uncertainty. Zig-zags treat each one as exactly right and make the pattern follow the errors.

After plotting, read each cross back off the graph and compare it with the table. It catches most plotting slips.

  • Straight or curved

Mission 5: Best fit & anomalies

What is the line for? Each plotted point is a little off, some high and some low. The best-fit line averages them all at once, the way a mean averages three trials. That is why it may pass through none of the points.

Straight or curved? Hold a ruler along the points. If they sit either side of it at random, draw a straight line. If they are above it in the middle and below at both ends, they follow a curve.

The reason behind each number. One independent variable, so there is only one possible cause. Three or more controls, because several things could change the result. Four or five values, because fewer cannot show the shape of a pattern. Three trials, so you can spot a reading that is badly out.

Why a sign-off first? A flaw in the plan is free to fix on paper. Found afterwards, the results cannot be used.

Three words that are easy to blur.

Prediction: what you expect before you start. “Longer wings will give a longer fall time.”

Result: what you measured. “At 8 cm the mean fall time was 1.88 s.”

Conclusion: what the results show overall. “As wing length increased, fall time increased.”
