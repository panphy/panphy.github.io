01 Mission 1: Measurement detective

Can this data be trusted?

precision vs accuracyuncertainty from repeatsrandom & systematic errorresolution
THE CASE (2011)

The loose cable that broke physics

  • Neutrinos timed over 730 km
  • Arrived ~60 ns before light?
  • Thousands of repeats, very consistent
  • Cause: a loose cable connection
  • Precise, but not accurate
Mission 1: Trust the data?
  • The neutrino team repeated their timing thousands of times and got almost the same answer each time. Their results were precise.
  • Every result was wrong by the same amount, because of a loose cable. Their results were not accurate.
  • The lesson for this mission: results that agree with each other can still all be wrong.

The scientists did not hide the strange result. They published it and asked other teams to find the fault. Checking each other’s work is how science corrects itself.

01 Key idea

Precise ≠ accurate

PreciseRepeats close to each other
AccurateClose to the true value
Consistent does not mean correct: a systematic error can shift every reading.
Mission 1: Trust the data?
  • Precise: repeat readings are close to each other.
  • Accurate: a reading is close to the true value.
  • The two are separate. Results can be precise but not accurate, like darts in a tight group that is far from the bullseye.
  • A systematic error moves every reading the same way, so the readings still agree with each other.

Using “accurate” when you mean “precise”. Close to each other is precise; close to the true value is accurate.

Mission 1: Trust the data?
  • No measurement is exact. The uncertainty says how far the true value could be from your result.
  • From repeat readings: uncertainty = (largest reading − smallest reading) ÷ 2.
  • Write the result as mean ± uncertainty, with the unit: 1.22 ± 0.03 s.
  • That means the true value is likely to lie between 1.19 s and 1.25 s.

Give the mean to the same number of decimal places as the readings, and round the uncertainty to match: 0.025 s becomes 0.03 s.

A smaller uncertainty means more precise readings. It does not show that they are accurate.

01 Two errors

Only one is fixed by repeating

Random error

  • Spreads readings both ways
  • e.g. reaction time with a stopwatch
  • Fix: repeat + mean, light gates

Systematic error

  • Shifts every reading the same way
  • e.g. balance reads 0.2 g when empty (zero error)
  • Fix: check zero, calibrate; repeats don’t help
Mission 1: Trust the data?
  • A random error makes readings scatter both above and below the true value, differently each time. Example: reaction time with a stopwatch.
  • Taking repeats and a mean reduces the effect of random error, because the highs and lows partly cancel.
  • A systematic error shifts every reading the same way by the same amount. Example: a balance that reads 0.2 g with nothing on it (a zero error).
  • Repeats do not fix a systematic error. Fix the instrument or the method, or correct every reading.

“Repeat the experiment to remove the zero error.” Every repeat has the same shift, so the mean has it too.

01 What can the instrument see?

Resolution

Ruler, mm marks1 mm

Mission 1: Trust the data?
  • Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can detect: 1 mm for a ruler with millimetre marks.
  • Choose an instrument whose resolution is small compared with what you are measuring.
  • Better resolution gives finer readings. It does not make the instrument accurate: a ruler with a worn end still reads wrong, however fine its marks.

Record readings to the resolution of the instrument. With a millimetre ruler write 12.0 cm, not 12 cm.

A stopwatch shows 0.01 s, but your reaction time is about 0.2 s. The display is finer than the measurement you can actually make by hand.

Mission 1: Trust the data?
  • These are the exact meanings examiners use. Learn them as pairs that are easy to mix up.
  • Precise / accurate: close to each other / close to the true value.
  • Repeatable / reproducible: the same person gets the same results again / a different person or method gets the same results.

In science, range means the maximum and minimum values of a variable, such as “from 10 cm to 50 cm”. It is not the largest reading minus the smallest.

02 Mission 2: Decode any investigation

THE CASE

The study that proved nothing

  • Energy-drink group scored higher in a test
  • Drinkers: tested in the morning
  • Non-drinkers: tested after lunch
  • Two things changed → no conclusion
Mission 2: Variables & graphs
  • In the energy-drink study, two things were different between the groups: the drink and the time of day.

02 Take the question apart

Change. Measure. Control.

  • Independent: the one thing I change
  • Dependent: the thing I measure
  • Control: everything I keep the same
  • Change only one thing at a time
How does length affect a pendulum’s period?
Mission 2: Variables & graphs
  • Independent variable: the one thing you choose to change.
  • Dependent variable: the thing you measure, which may change as a result.
  • Control variables: everything you keep the same, so that only the independent variable can explain the result.
  • In “How does X affect Y?”, X is the independent variable and Y is the dependent variable.

For the pendulum: independent = length, dependent = time period, control = mass of the bob, size of the swing, how it is released.

02 Two kinds of data

Continuous or categoric?

  • Continuous: any value on a scale
  • length, time, temperature, mass
  • Categoric: names or types
  • material, colour, surface, brand
  • Test: is there a value in between?
Mission 2: Variables & graphs
  • Continuous data can take any value on a scale: length, time, temperature, mass.
  • Categoric data are names or types: material, colour, surface, brand.
  • The test: can there be a value in between? Between 10 cm and 20 cm there is 15 cm. Between “sponge” and “cardboard” there is nothing.

03 Mission 3: Bouncing balls → evidence

Which material absorbs the shock?

THE CASE

Why helmets are made of foam

  • Foam is crushed in a crash
  • Head stops over a longer time
  • → smaller force on the skull
  • Found by drop tests, not guesses
Mission 3: The drop test
  • Foam in a helmet crushes in a crash, so the head takes longer to stop and the force on it is smaller.
  • Engineers found the best materials by testing and measuring, not by guessing.
  • You will do the same on a small scale: the material that gives the lowest bounce has absorbed the most energy.

03 Variables first

The drop test

QuestionHow does material affect bounce height?
  • Independent: material (categoric)
  • Dependent: bounce height / cm
  • Control: same ball, drop height, release, ruler position
  • Lowest bounce = best shock absorber
Mission 3: The drop test
  • Write the three variables before you touch the equipment.
  • Independent: the material (categoric). Dependent: bounce height in cm.
  • Control: the same ball, the same drop height, the same way of releasing it, the ruler in the same position.
  • A lower bounce means the material absorbed more of the ball’s energy.

Listing “the material” as a control variable. The material is what you are changing, so it is the independent variable.

03 Method + safety

How to run it

  1. Choose 4–5 materials
  2. Ruler vertical, 0 cm at the surface
  3. Drop from 50 cm, no push
  4. Read the first bounce at eye level
  5. Repeat: 3 trials per material
  6. Calculate each mean

Safety

  • Drop, never throw
  • Stable surface for materials
  • Keep clear of the drop zone
Odd reading? Check it now, while the kit is out.
Mission 3: The drop test
  • A good method is a list of steps someone else could follow and get the same results.
  • Details that improve the measurement: ruler vertical, zero at the surface, eye level with the top of the bounce, release without pushing.
  • Three trials for each material, then a mean.
  • If one reading looks odd, check and repeat it straight away, while the equipment is still set up.

Reading the ruler from above or below the mark gives a parallax error. Having your eye level with the mark removes it.

  • An evaluation names a specific problem, says what it does to the results, and gives a fix that matches.

05 Key idea

Show the trend

Do

  • One thin, smooth line
  • Straight or curved
  • Points balanced above and below

Don’t

  • Zig-zag point to point
  • Force it through the ends
  • Let an anomalous result drag it
Mission 5: Best fit & anomalies
  • A best-fit line shows the overall trend. It does not have to pass through every point.
  • It can be a straight line or a smooth curve. Look at the points and decide which they follow.
  • Draw one thin line with about as many points above it as below it.
  • Ignore an anomalous result when you place the line.

Forcing the line through the first and last points, or through the origin. Only go through (0, 0) if the points lead there.

Use a clear ruler for a straight line, so you can see the points on both sides while you place it.

  • Control variables must be things that would really change the fall time: the paper, the number of paperclips, the drop height, the way you release it.

Drop from as high as you safely can. A longer fall time makes your reaction time a smaller part of the measurement.

Mission 6: Solo flight
  • A best-fit line lets you predict values you did not measure.
  • To read a value: go up from the x-axis to the line, then across to the y-axis. Draw these construction lines on the graph.
  • Predictions inside the range you tested can be trusted more than predictions beyond it, where the pattern might change.

Reading between your points is called interpolation. Extending the line beyond them is extrapolation. A helicopter with 20 cm wings might just flop over, so the line cannot be trusted that far.

Mission 6: Solo flight
  • Write an evaluation as a chain: problem → effect on the results → matching fix.
  • Reaction time varies each time you start and stop the stopwatch, so the times are scattered. This is a random error.
  • It matters most when the time measured is short, because the error is then a bigger fraction of the reading.
  • A matching fix removes the cause: slow-motion video, electronic timing, or a greater drop height.

“Be more careful” or “use better equipment” are not improvements. Say what you would use and what problem it solves.

08 Defend it

Conclude. Evaluate.

Conclusion

Line: As increased, increased / decreased.
Bar: The highest / lowest was .
Evidence: I know because (values + units).

Evaluation

  • One strength
  • One weakness
  • A random error and a systematic error
  • A matching improvement
  • How confident? What extra evidence?
Missions 7–8: Your investigation
  • Conclusion: state the pattern, then give the evidence with values and units.
  • Evaluation: one strength, one weakness, the errors (random and systematic), and an improvement that matches the weakness.
  • End by saying how confident you are, and what extra evidence would make you more confident: more repeats, a wider range, or another group getting the same result.

If another group gets the same pattern with their own equipment, the result is reproducible, which is strong evidence that it is right.
