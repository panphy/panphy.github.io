01 What a circuit needs

A complete loop

  • A source of energy: cell or battery
  • A closed loop of conducting wire
  • Wires are already full of charge
  • Close the switch → all the charge moves at once
  • A gap anywhere → no current everywhere
Mission 1: Build & measure
  • A current needs two things: a source of energy (a cell or battery) and a complete loop of conductor.
  • The wires are full of charge before the cell is connected. The cell does not supply the charge; it makes the charge that is already there move.
  • All the charge in the loop starts moving at the same moment, which is why a lamp lights as soon as the switch closes.
  • A break anywhere stops the current everywhere in that loop.

“The battery sends charge down the wire to the lamp.” The charge is already in the lamp and the wires; closing the switch sets all of it moving together.

01 Learn to draw all of these

Circuit symbols

Cell: the longer line is the positive terminal
Battery: two or more cells joined together
Mission 1: Build & measure
  • Learn to draw and recognise every symbol here: switch, cell, battery, lamp, fuse, resistor, variable resistor, diode, LED, thermistor, LDR, ammeter and voltmeter.
  • Cell: the long line is positive, the short line is negative. A battery is two or more cells joined together.
  • Look for the extra feature on similar symbols: an arrow through a resistor makes it variable, a line through makes a thermistor, arrows pointing in make an LDR, arrows pointing out make an LED.

Cover the names and test yourself both ways: name to symbol, then symbol to name. Exam questions ask for both.

01 Anyone can build it

Draw it properly

  • Pencil and ruler
  • Straight wires, right-angle corners
  • No gaps: every wire joins at both ends
  • Wire goes into each symbol
  • Label values: 6 V, 10 Ω
Mission 1: Build & measure
  • A circuit diagram shows how components are connected, not what the circuit looks like on the bench.
  • Use a pencil and ruler: straight wires, right-angle corners, no gaps.
  • Wires stop at the edge of each symbol and carry on from the other side. Put symbols along the sides, not on the corners.
  • Label the values you know, with units: 6 V, 10 Ω.

A gap in a drawn wire is a broken circuit. An examiner cannot give the mark for a circuit that would not work.

01 Measuring current

Ammeter in series

  • Current in amperes (A)
  • Ammeter in the same loop as the component
  • Same charge flows through both
  • Ideal ammeter: almost zero resistance
One loop = a series circuit
Mission 1: Build & measure
  • Current is measured in amperes (A) with an ammeter.
  • An ammeter goes in series: in the same loop as the component, so the same charge flows through both.
  • An ammeter has almost zero resistance, so adding it does not change the current it is measuring.

Connecting an ammeter across a component. With almost no resistance it gives the charge an easy path round the component, and the reading is wrong.

Small currents are given in milliamps: 1 A = 1000 mA. Convert to amps before you use an equation.

02 Current

A rate of flow

  • Current = rate of flow of charge
  • Charge Q in coulombs (C)
  • Current I in amperes (A)
  • 1 A = 1 C passing a point each second
  • In metals: free electrons move
  • Needs a closed loop + a p.d.
Mission 2: Charge on the move
  • Learn the definition: electric current is the rate of flow of electrical charge.
  • Charge Q is measured in coulombs (C). Current I is measured in amperes (A).
  • 1 A means 1 C of charge passes a point every second.
  • In a metal the moving charges are free electrons. They only flow steadily if there is a closed loop and a source of potential difference.

“Rate of flow of charge” gets the mark. “Flow of electricity” or “how fast the electrons go” does not.

02 Which way?

Two directions

  • Electrons: − terminal → + terminal
  • Conventional current: + → −
  • Circuit arrows use conventional current
  • Same current, opposite arrows
Mission 2: Charge on the move
  • Conventional current goes from the positive terminal round to the negative terminal. Arrows on circuit diagrams always show this direction.
  • Electrons are negatively charged, so they move the other way: negative terminal to positive terminal.
  • Both describe the same current; only the direction of the arrow differs.

The + to − direction was chosen long before the electron was discovered. Physicists kept the convention because every rule still works with it.

02 Common mistake

Current is not used up

  • One loop → same current everywhere
  • Charge is conserved: it goes round
  • Components take energy, not charge

“The lamp uses up some current.”

The lamp transfers energy. Current stays the same.

Mission 2: Charge on the move
  • In a single loop the current is the same everywhere. Ammeters before and after a lamp read the same.
  • Charge is conserved: every coulomb that goes into a component comes out the other side.
  • What a component takes from the charge is energy. The energy is transferred to the surroundings as light and heating.

“The lamp uses up current.” Say instead: the lamp transfers energy; the current is the same on both sides.

Think of a bicycle chain. Every link keeps going round and none is used up, yet the chain carries energy from the pedals to the wheel.

Mission 3: Energy per coulomb
  • Potential difference (p.d.) is the energy transferred per coulomb of charge that passes. It is measured in volts (V).
  • 1 V = 1 J per coulomb. A p.d. of 6 V across a resistor means each coulomb transfers 6 J as it passes through.
  • E = Q V: energy transferred (J) = charge flow (C) × p.d. (V).
  • If you are given current and time, find the charge first with Q = I t, then use E = Q V.

Two-step questions do not tell you there are two steps. List what you are given; if you have I and t but need Q, that is the first step.

03 Measuring p.d.

Voltmeter across

  • p.d. is between two points
  • Voltmeter in parallel, across the part
  • Very high resistance
  • Both leads on one wire → about 0 V

“The voltage at the resistor.”

“The p.d. across the resistor.”

Mission 3: Energy per coulomb
  • A p.d. is always between two points, so a voltmeter has one lead on each side of the component.
  • A voltmeter goes in parallel, across the component.
  • A voltmeter has a very high resistance, so almost no current goes through it and the circuit is hardly changed.
  • Both leads on the same piece of wire read about 0 V: almost no energy is transferred in a connecting wire.

Writing “the p.d. through the lamp” or “the voltage at the lamp”. Current goes through; p.d. is across.

03 Source and load

Energy in. Energy out.

  • Source (battery): energy to the charge
  • Load (lamp, resistor): energy from the charge
  • Series: component p.d.s add up to the supply
  • 6 J supplied = 3 J + 3 J transferred
Charge is conserved and energy is conserved
Mission 3: Energy per coulomb
  • The source (cell or battery) transfers energy to the charge. Each load (lamp, resistor, motor) transfers energy from the charge.
  • In a series circuit the p.d.s across the components add up to the supply p.d.
  • This is energy conservation: every joule given to a coulomb by the battery is transferred by the components on the way round.

Two things are conserved in every circuit. Charge is conserved, which gives the rules for current. Energy is conserved, which gives the rules for p.d.

04 Ohm’s law

Ohmic conductors

  • I directly proportional to V
  • Only at constant temperature
  • Straight line through the origin
  • Resistance stays constant
Double the p.d. → double the current
Mission 4: Resistance
  • For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, the current is directly proportional to the p.d. across it.
  • Its I–V graph is a straight line through the origin, and its resistance stays constant.
  • “Directly proportional” means: double the p.d. and the current doubles.

A straight line is not enough. To show direct proportion, the line must also pass through the origin. Say both.

A fixed resistor and a metal wire are ohmic only while their temperature stays the same. Let the wire heat up and the line starts to curve.

04 Inside the metal

Why metals resist

  • Electrons collide with metal ions
  • Collisions oppose the current
  • Energy transferred → wire heats up
  • Hotter → ions vibrate more → more collisions
  • So R increases with temperature
Mission 4: Resistance
  • A metal is a lattice of ions with free electrons moving between them.
  • The electrons collide with the ions. The collisions make it harder for charge to flow: this is resistance.
  • Each collision transfers energy to the ions, so the wire heats up.
  • In a hotter metal the ions vibrate more, there are more collisions, and the resistance increases.

For “explain why the resistance increases”, give the chain: hotter → ions vibrate more → more frequent collisions with electrons → greater resistance.

04 The standard circuit

Measure R

  1. Ammeter in series
  2. Voltmeter across the component
  3. Variable resistor → change I
  4. Record V and I for several pairs
  5. R = V ÷ I, then a mean or a graph
Mission 4: Resistance
  • You cannot read resistance from a meter in this circuit. Measure V and I, then calculate R = V ÷ I.
  • Ammeter in series with the component; voltmeter in parallel across it.
  • The variable resistor changes the current, so you can take several pairs of readings.
  • Several pairs give a mean value of R, or a graph, which is more reliable than one pair.

Asked to “describe how to measure the resistance”? Draw this circuit, say which meter measures what, and finish with R = V ÷ I.

RP Resistance: Part A

Length of a wire

  • DV: resistance / Ω
  • Control: material, diameter, temperature
  1. 10 → 100 cm, in steps
  2. Read V and I
  3. R = V ÷ I

Required practical: Resistance
  • Independent variable: length of wire. Dependent variable: resistance. Control variables: material, diameter and temperature of the wire.
  • For each length, read the p.d. and the current, then calculate R = V ÷ I.
  • Take readings over a wide span of lengths, in equal steps, and repeat them.
  • Switch off between readings so the wire does not heat up. A hot wire has a higher resistance, which would spoil the control of temperature.

A method question wants: what you change, what you measure and with what, what you keep the same, and how you make the results more accurate.

RP Results and evaluation

R length

  • Straight line through origin → R L
  • Longer wire → more collisions
  • Wire heats → R rises → keep current small
  • Measure to the same contact point of the clip

“Human error.”

“The wire heated up, so R increased.”

Required practical: Resistance
  • The graph of R against length is a straight line through the origin, so resistance is directly proportional to length.
  • Why: in a longer wire the electrons make more collisions with ions on the way through.
  • Keep the current small and switch off between readings, so the wire stays cool.
  • The line may miss the origin slightly. A clip that does not sit exactly at 0 cm, or contact resistance at the clips, adds the same amount to every reading: a zero error.

“Human error” earns nothing. Name the cause and its effect: “the wire heated up, so its resistance increased.”

05 Mission 5: Crack any network

Series & parallel

series rulesparallel rulesRtotal = R₁ + R₂
KEY RULE

Parallel: same p.d., currents add

Mission 5: Series & parallel

05 One path

Series rules

  • Same current through every part
  • Supply p.d. shared between parts
  • Bigger R → bigger share of p.d.
Rtotal = R₁ + R₂total resistance in series
Mission 5: Series & parallel
  • Series: one path. The current is the same through every component.
  • The supply p.d. is shared between the components. The component with the bigger resistance gets the bigger share.
  • Rtotal = R1 + R2. Adding a resistor in series always increases the total resistance.

Equal resistors share the p.d. equally. Two identical lamps on a 6 V supply get 3 V each.

One break stops everything in a series circuit, which is why house lights are not wired in series.

05 More than one path

Parallel rules

  • Same p.d. across each branch
  • Branch currents add up to the total
  • Smaller R → bigger branch current
  • Rtotal less than the smallest resistor
Each branch gets the full supply p.d.
Mission 5: Series & parallel
  • Parallel: more than one path. The p.d. across each branch is the same, and equal to the supply p.d.
  • The current from the supply splits at a junction. The branch currents add up to the total current.
  • The branch with the smaller resistance carries the bigger current.
  • The total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistor.

“Each branch gets a share of the p.d.” That is the series rule. In parallel, every branch gets the full supply p.d.

Each branch works on its own, so one lamp can be switched off without the others going out. This is how house lights and sockets are wired.

RP Resistance: Part B

Another path, less R

  • Series: add a resistor → Rtotal up
  • Parallel: add a branch → more paths
  • More current at the same p.d.
  • So Rtotal = V ÷ I goes down
Two 10 Ω resistors
Series20 Ω
Parallel5 Ω
Mission 5: Series & parallel
  • Adding a resistor in series increases the total resistance: the same charge now has to pass through both.
  • Adding a resistor in parallel decreases the total resistance: there is another path, so more current flows from the supply at the same p.d.
  • Since Rtotal = V ÷ I, a bigger I at the same V means a smaller Rtotal.

At GCSE you only calculate total resistance for series circuits. For parallel you must explain why it decreases; no calculation is asked for.

“More resistors means more resistance.” True in series only.

06 Mission 6: A component’s fingerprint

Mission 6: I–V characteristics

06 Reading the graph

Current against p.d.

  • p.d. on x, current on y
  • Negative values: supply reversed
  • Straight through origin → ohmic
  • Curve → R changes
Fixed resistor: same shape both ways
Mission 6: I–V characteristics
  • An I–V characteristic is a graph of current (y-axis) against p.d. (x-axis) for one component.
  • Straight line through the origin: current is directly proportional to p.d., so the component is ohmic and its resistance is constant.
  • A curve means the resistance changes as the current changes.
  • The negative half shows what happens with the supply connected the other way round.

To find the resistance at one point, read V and I at that point and use R = V ÷ I.

With current on the y-axis, a steeper line means a lower resistance: more current for the same p.d.

06 Non-ohmic

Lamp & diode

Filament lamp

  • More current → filament hotter
  • Hotter → R increases
  • Graph gets less steep

Diode (and LED)

  • Current in one direction only
  • Very high R in reverse
  • Forward: conducts above ~0.6 V
Mission 6: I–V characteristics
  • Filament lamp: as the current increases, the filament gets hotter, so its resistance increases. The graph curves and gets less steep.
  • Diode: current flows in one direction only. In the reverse direction its resistance is very high, so the current is almost zero.
  • Neither is ohmic: their resistance is not constant.
  • An LED is a diode that emits light when current flows through it in the forward direction.

Learn to sketch all three shapes: resistor (straight line through the origin), lamp (S-shaped curve through the origin), diode (flat, then rising steeply on one side only).

06 Components that sense

Thermistor & LDR

Thermistor

  • Temperature ↑ → R ↓
  • Thermostats, fire alarms

Light-dependent resistor

  • Light intensity ↑ → R ↓
  • Streetlights, night lights
Mission 6: I–V characteristics
  • Thermistor: temperature increases → resistance decreases.
  • LDR (light-dependent resistor): light intensity increases → resistance decreases.
  • Both graphs are curves: the resistance falls quickly at first, then more slowly.
  • Uses: thermistors in thermostats and fire alarms; LDRs in lights that switch on when it gets dark.

Mixing a thermistor up with a metal wire. A wire’s resistance increases when it gets hotter; a thermistor’s decreases.

A memory aid for the LDR: Light Up, Resistance Down.

06 Sensor circuit

Brighter → more current

  1. Light gets brighter
  2. LDR resistance decreases
  3. Total resistance decreases
  4. I = V ÷ R → current increases
6.0 V supplyBright: 6.0 ÷ 600 Ω = 0.010 A
Dark: 6.0 ÷ 1500 Ω = 0.0040 A
Mission 6: I–V characteristics
  • Explain a sensor circuit as a chain, one link at a time: light gets brighter → LDR resistance decreases → total resistance decreases → current increases (I = V ÷ R).
  • The supply p.d. stays the same; only the resistance changes.
  • A thermistor circuit works the same way, with temperature in place of light intensity.

In series with a fixed resistor, the LDR’s share of the p.d. falls as its resistance falls, so the fixed resistor’s share rises. That changing p.d. is the signal that switches a lamp or an alarm.

RP I–V characteristics

  • Plot current (y-axis) against p.d. (x-axis) and draw a line or curve of best fit.

The diode is tested with a protective resistor in series and a milliammeter, because the currents are small and too large a current would damage it.

RP Get the negative half

Reverse & read

Required practical: I–V

  • Plot the negative readings in the bottom-left quarter of the graph.
  • A resistor and a lamp behave the same in both directions. A diode does not: it only conducts in the direction its arrow points.

Giving up on the diode because the meters read zero. Zero current in reverse is the result; record it.

Two power equations

Mission 7: Power & the Grid
  • Power is the energy transferred each second. 1 W = 1 J/s, and 1 kW = 1000 W.
  • P = V I: use it when you know the p.d. and the current.
  • P = I² R: use it when you know the current and the resistance.
  • Square the current first, then multiply by R. Doubling the current gives four times the power.

Working out (I × R)² or forgetting the square. For 2.0 A through 15 Ω: 2.0² = 4.0, then 4.0 × 15 = 60 W.

The two equations are the same idea: put V = I R into P = V I and you get P = I × I R = I² R.

  • E = P t: energy transferred (J) = power (W) × time (s).
  • Convert first: kW × 1000 → W, minutes × 60 → s.
  • The energy an appliance transfers depends on its power and how long it is on.
  • Say where the energy goes: a kettle heats the water (thermal store); a motor makes things move (kinetic store).

E = Q V and E = P t both give energy in joules. Choose by what you are given: charge and p.d., or power and time.

07 Power station to home

The National Grid

  • Cables + transformers
  • Step-up: p.d. up for transmission
  • High p.d. → small current
  • Small current → less heating in cables
  • Step-down: p.d. down for homes
WhyAn efficient way to transfer energy
Mission 7: Power & the Grid
  • The National Grid is a system of cables and transformers that links power stations to consumers.
  • Step-up transformers increase the p.d. from the power station for transmission.
  • For the same power, a high p.d. means a small current, and a small current means less heating of the cables, so less energy is wasted.
  • Step-down transformers decrease the p.d. to a safer value for homes.

The usual 3-mark answer: high p.d. → low current → less energy lost by heating the cables → more efficient.

Power wasted in a cable is P = I² R, so making the current ten times smaller cuts the waste a hundred times.

Mission 8: Mains & safety
  • Direct current (dc) goes in one direction only. Cells and batteries give a direct p.d.
  • Alternating current (ac) keeps reversing direction. It comes from an alternating p.d.
  • UK mains is ac at about 230 V with a frequency of 50 Hz.
  • 50 Hz means 50 complete cycles every second.

Learn both numbers with their units: 230 V and 50 Hz. They are asked for directly.

On an oscilloscope, dc is a flat line and ac is a wave that goes above and below zero.

Mission 8: Mains & safety
  • Live (brown): carries the alternating p.d. from the supply, about 230 V.
  • Neutral (blue): completes the circuit. It is at, or close to, 0 V.
  • Earth (green and yellow stripes): a safety wire at 0 V. It only carries a current if there is a fault.
  • The p.d. between live and earth is about 230 V; between neutral and earth it is about 0 V.

Learn the colour, the p.d. and the job for all three. Questions often give you two and ask for the third.

08 Staying safe

Why live is dangerous

  • Body at 0 V, live at 230 V
  • Touch live → current through you: shock
  • Still live with the switch off
  • Live–earth link → huge current, fire risk
  • Metal case is earthed: fault current → earth

“The earth wire is at 230 V.”

Earth is at 0 V.

Mission 8: Mains & safety
  • Your body is at 0 V. Touching the live wire puts about 230 V across you, so a current passes through you to earth: an electric shock.
  • The live wire is dangerous even when the switch is off: the wire up to the switch is still at 230 V.
  • If a fault lets the live wire touch a metal case, the earth wire gives a low-resistance path to earth, so the case cannot stay live.

“The earth wire is at 230 V” or “the earth wire always carries current.” It is at 0 V and carries current only when there is a fault.

The large fault current through the earth wire melts the fuse, which is in the live wire, and that disconnects the appliance from the supply.
