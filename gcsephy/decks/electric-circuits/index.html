01 What a circuit needs

A complete loop

  • A source of energy: cell or battery
  • A closed loop of conducting wire
  • Wires are already full of charge
  • Close the switch → all the charge moves at once
  • A gap anywhere → no current everywhere
Mission 1: Build & measure

Why does the lamp light at once?

Picture a bicycle chain. Push one pedal and every link moves at the same moment, because the chain is already full of links. The wires are already full of charge, and the cell sets all of it moving together.

The charges themselves drift slowly, less than a millimetre each second. The lamp does not wait for charge to arrive from the cell.

Why does one gap stop everything?

A steady current needs a complete conducting path. Charge cannot keep flowing up to a gap with nowhere to go, so the flow stops all the way round, like a chain held at one link.

01 Learn to draw all of these

Circuit symbols

Cell: the longer line is the positive terminal
Battery: two or more cells joined together
Mission 1: Build & measure

Read the symbols as pictures

Two small arrows stand for light

Pointing towards a resistor: light arriving, so it is an LDR. Pointing away from a diode: light leaving, so it is an LED.

A diagonal line through a resistor means its resistance can change

With an arrowhead: a variable resistor, which you adjust by hand. With a short flat end: a thermistor, which changes with temperature.

A diode’s triangle points the way conventional current can pass.

In a cell, the long thin line is the positive terminal. If you draw the cell the wrong way round, every current arrow in your diagram points the wrong way.

01 Anyone can build it

Draw it properly

  • Pencil and ruler
  • Straight wires, right-angle corners
  • No gaps: every wire joins at both ends
  • Wire goes into each symbol
  • Label values: 6 V, 10 Ω
Mission 1: Build & measure

A circuit diagram is a map of connections, not a picture

Like a tube map, it shows what is joined to what. The length of a wire and where a component sits on the bench do not matter, which is why a messy bench can match a neat diagram.

Test your own diagram

put your pencil on the positive terminal and trace round to the negative terminal without lifting it. If you have to jump a gap, so would the charge.

01 Measuring current

Ammeter in series

  • Current in amperes (A)
  • Ammeter in the same loop as the component
  • Same charge flows through both
  • Ideal ammeter: almost zero resistance
One loop \(=\) a series circuit
Mission 1: Build & measure

Why in series?

An ammeter measures the charge passing through itself each second. To measure the current in a lamp, all the charge that goes through the lamp must also go through the ammeter.

Why almost zero resistance?

An instrument should not change what it measures. An ammeter with a large resistance would reduce the current and then report the reduced value.

An ammeter connected across a component gives the charge an easy route round it. A very large current flows and the reading means nothing.

02 Current

A rate of flow

  • Current = rate of flow of charge
  • Charge \(Q\) in coulombs (C)
  • Current I in amperes (A)
  • 1 A \(=\) 1 C passing a point each second
  • In metals: free electrons move
  • Needs a closed loop + a p.d.
Mission 2: Charge on the move

Picture standing at one point in the wire and counting

Current is how much charge goes past you each second. It is not how fast the charges move, and not how much charge is in the wire.

Why does it need a p.d.?

The free electrons in a metal are always jiggling in random directions, which gives no overall flow. The cell gives them a steady push one way round the loop.

One coulomb is about six million million million electrons. At 1 A, that many pass a point every second.

Mission 2: Charge on the move

The equation is the definition of current, rearranged

Current is the charge passing each second. So the total charge that passes is the current multiplied by the number of seconds.

That is why time must be in seconds

One ampere is one coulomb per second. Put minutes in and the answer is 60 times too small.

Know the sizes

A phone charging at 2 A for an hour: \(2 \times 3600 = 7200\) C. If you get 0.002 C for a kettle, look for a unit you did not convert.

02 Which way?

Two directions

  • Electrons: − terminal → + terminal
  • Conventional current: + → −
  • Circuit arrows use conventional current
  • Same current, opposite arrows
Mission 2: Charge on the move

Why two directions?

Scientists agreed that current goes from positive to negative long before anyone knew what was moving. The electron turned out to be negative, so it is pushed away from the negative terminal: the opposite way.

Nobody changed the convention because nothing depends on it. Every rule in this unit gives the same answer either way.

Draw current arrows from + round to −. Mention electrons going − to + only when a question asks about the particles.

02 Common mistake

Current is not used up

  • One loop → same current everywhere
  • Charge is conserved: it goes round
  • Components take energy, not charge

“The lamp uses up some current.”

The lamp transfers energy. Current stays the same.

Mission 2: Charge on the move

If the current is the same on both sides, what changes?

The energy each coulomb carries. Energy is transferred to the lamp; it is not used up either. Think of delivery vans on a one-way loop. The same number of vans pass every point each minute. They leave the depot full and come back empty. The vans are the charge; the parcels are the energy.

Why must it be so?

If less charge left the lamp than entered it, charge would pile up inside the lamp for as long as it was on. It does not.

Saying a second lamp is dim because the first “took some of the current”. Both have the same current. They share the energy.

Mission 3: Energy per coulomb

What does “6 V” mean?

Joules per coulomb. Each coulomb that passes through a 6 V battery picks up 6 J. Each coulomb that passes through a lamp with 6 V across it gives up 6 J.

Why “difference”?

A coulomb entering the lamp carries more energy than one leaving it. The p.d. is that difference, so it always involves two places.

Where \(E = Q\,V\) comes from

if each coulomb transfers \(V\) joules, then \(Q\) coulombs transfer \(Q \times V\) joules.

03 Measuring p.d.

Voltmeter across

  • p.d. is between two points
  • Voltmeter in parallel, across the part
  • Very high resistance
  • Both leads on one wire → about 0 V

“The voltage at the resistor.”

“The p.d. across the resistor.”

Mission 3: Energy per coulomb

Why across, not in line?

A voltmeter compares the energy each coulomb has just before the component with the energy it has just after. To compare two places, it needs one lead connected on each side of the component.

Why a very high resistance?

So that almost no charge goes through the meter and the circuit carries on as if it were not there. An ammeter is the opposite: very low resistance, in the main loop.

Why 0 V along a wire?

A wire has almost no resistance, so a coulomb gives up almost no energy passing along it.

03 Source and load

Energy in. Energy out.

  • Source (battery): energy to the charge
  • Load (lamp, resistor): energy from the charge
  • Series: component p.d.s add up to the supply
  • 6 J supplied \(=\) 3 J \(+\) 3 J transferred
Charge is conserved and energy is conserved
Mission 3: Energy per coulomb

Follow one coulomb round the loop

At the 6 V battery it picks up 6 J. At the first lamp it gives up 3 J. At the second it gives up the other 3 J. It arrives back with nothing left, picks up 6 J and goes round again.

That is why the p.d.s add up to the supply

A coulomb cannot give out more energy than it was given, and it does not come back with any left over.

The shares need not be equal. The component with more resistance takes more. Mission 4 explains why.

Mission 4: Resistance

Read resistance as “volts needed per amp”

A 15 Ω resistor needs 15 V for each amp through it. For 0.20 A it needs \(0.20 \times 15 = 3.0\) V, which is Example 1.

Predict before you calculate

Same p.d. and double the resistance: the current must halve. If your answer goes the other way, the equation was rearranged wrongly.

Mixing components. \(V\), \(I\) and \(R\) must all belong to the same component: the p.d. across the lamp, the current through the lamp, the resistance of the lamp.

04 Ohm’s law

Ohmic conductors

  • \(I\) directly proportional to \(V\)
  • Only at constant temperature
  • Straight line through the origin
  • Resistance stays constant
Double the p.d. → double the current
Mission 4: Resistance

How to test for “directly proportional” in a results table

Work out \(V \div I\) for each row. If it is the same every time, the current is directly proportional to the p.d., and that constant value is the resistance.

What the slope tells you

Current is on the y-axis, so a steeper line means more current for the same p.d.: a lower resistance.

A straight line alone does not show direct proportion. It must also pass through the origin.

04 Inside the metal

Why metals resist

  • Electrons collide with metal ions
  • Collisions oppose the current
  • Energy transferred → wire heats up
  • Hotter → ions vibrate more → more collisions
  • So \(R\) increases with temperature
Mission 4: Resistance

This one picture explains three other results

A longer wire has more resistance

the electrons pass more ions, so they make more collisions.

A wire gets hot

each collision hands energy to an ion. The ions vibrate more, and more vibration is what a higher temperature is.

A filament lamp is not ohmic

a bigger current makes the filament hotter, and a hotter filament means more collisions. So its resistance rises as the current rises.

04 The standard circuit

Measure \(R\)

  1. Ammeter in series
  2. Voltmeter across the component
  3. Variable resistor → change I
  4. Record \(V\) and \(I\) for several pairs
  5. \(R = V \div I\), then a mean or a graph
Mission 4: Resistance

Why not take one pair of readings and stop?

One pair gives one value of \(R\) with no way of knowing whether you misread a meter. Several pairs show whether \(V \div I\) comes out the same each time.

A graph does better still

A line of best fit averages all the readings at once, shows up any that do not fit, and shows whether the component is ohmic.

Keep the current small. A warm component has a different resistance, so you would be measuring something that changes as you measure it.

RP Resistance: Part A

Length of a wire

  • IV: length / cm
  • DV: resistance / Ω
  • Control: material, diameter, temperature
  1. 10 → 100 cm, in steps
  2. Read \(V\) and \(I\)

Required practical: Resistance

Why each control variable matters

A thicker wire has a lower resistance, a different metal has a different resistance, and a hotter wire has a higher resistance. Change any of them along with the length and you cannot say which caused the change in R.

Temperature is the hard one, because the experiment itself heats the wire. That is the reason for switching off between readings.

The crocodile clip is a few millimetres wide. Decide which edge marks the length and use the same edge every time.

RP Results and evaluation

\(R \propto\) length

  • Straight line through origin → \(R \propto L\)
  • Longer wire → more collisions
  • Wire heats → \(R\) rises → keep current small
  • Measure to the same contact point of the clip

“Human error.”

“The wire heated up, so \(R\) increased.”

Required practical: Resistance

What “directly proportional” lets you do

You can predict without measuring. If 20 cm of this wire has a resistance of 2.0 Ω, then three times the length, 60 cm, has three times the resistance: 6.0 Ω.

What if the line misses the origin?

If every point sits too high by the same amount, something is adding the same extra resistance each time, such as the contact between clip and wire. That is a systematic error.

What if the points scatter either side of the line?

That is random error, from reading the meters or placing the clip.

Mission 5: Series & parallel

05 One path

Series rules

  • Same current through every part
  • Supply p.d. shared between parts
  • Bigger \(R\) → bigger share of p.d.
\(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2\)total resistance in series
Mission 5: Series & parallel

Why the same current?

There is one path. Charge that passes through the first component has nowhere else to go.

Why does the bigger resistor get more p.d.?

Both carry the same current, and \(V = I\,R\). Twice the resistance needs twice the p.d.

Check it on the slide’s circuit

The current is 0.40 A in both resistors, so the p.d.s are \(0.40 \times 5 = 2\) V and \(0.40 \times 10 = 4\) V. The 10 Ω resistor has twice the resistance and takes twice the p.d., and the two add to the 6 V supply.

05 More than one path

Parallel rules

  • Same p.d. across each branch
  • Branch currents add up to the total
  • Smaller \(R\) → bigger branch current
  • \(R_{\text{total}}\) less than the smallest resistor
Each branch gets the full supply p.d.
Mission 5: Series & parallel

Why the same p.d. across every branch?

Trace the wires. The top of every branch joins the same point, and so does the bottom. Each branch is connected straight across the supply.

Check it on the slide’s circuit

Each branch has 12 V across it, so the currents are \(12 \div 4 = 3\) A and \(12 \div 6 = 2\) A: 5 A in total.

Now the total resistance

\(R = V \div I = 12 \div 5 = 2.4\) Ω, less than either resistor.

RP Resistance: Part B

Another path, less \(R\)

  • Series: add a resistor → \(R_{\text{total}}\) up
  • Parallel: add a branch → more paths
  • More current at the same p.d.
  • So \(R_{\text{total}} = V \div I\) goes down
Two 10 Ω resistors
Series20 Ω
Parallel5 Ω
Mission 5: Series & parallel

Think of supermarket checkouts

Open a second checkout and more shoppers get through each minute, though each checkout is no faster. Another branch is another route for charge, so more flows each second from the same supply.

The first branch is not changed at all

It has the same p.d. across it and carries the same current as before. The total resistance fell because the total current rose.

You have to explain this, not calculate it: another path, so more total current for the same p.d., so a smaller total resistance.

The method works for any circuit problem

1

Make a table: a row for each component, columns \(V\), \(I\) and \(R\). Fill in what you are given.

2

Mission 6: \(I\)–\(V\) characteristics

Getting resistance from the graph

Pick a point on the line, read its p.d. and current, and calculate \(R = V \div I\). On a curve the answer is different at different points.

What the negative half is for

It shows the component with the supply connected the other way round. A resistor gives the same shape in both halves. A diode does not, and you only find that out by testing both directions.

Using the gradient of a curve to find resistance. Read \(V\) and \(I\) at the point and divide.

06 Non-ohmic

Lamp & diode

Filament lamp

  • More current → filament hotter
  • Hotter → \(R\) increases
  • Graph gets less steep

Diode (and LED)

  • Current in one direction only
  • Very high \(R\) in reverse
  • Forward: a diode conducts above ~0.6 V (an LED needs more)
Mission 6: \(I\)–\(V\) characteristics

The lamp’s curve comes from electrons colliding with ions (Mission 4)

At low current the filament is cool, its resistance is low and the line is steep. As it heats up, each extra volt gives less extra current, so the line bends over.

A diode is a one-way valve for charge

It is used to turn ac into dc, and to protect circuits from a battery connected the wrong way round.

A cold filament has a much lower resistance than a hot one, so the current is largest at switch-on. That is when old filament lamps usually failed.

The 0.6 V is a typical value for a silicon diode; you do not need to learn it. The AQA specification says: “The current through a diode flows in one direction only. The diode has a very high resistance in the reverse direction.” (Physics 4.2.1.4, Combined Science 6.2.1.4)

06 Components that sense

Thermistor & LDR

Thermistor

  • Temperature ↑ → \(R\) ↓
  • Thermostats, fire alarms

Light-dependent resistor

  • Light intensity ↑ → \(R\) ↓
  • Streetlights, night lights
Mission 6: \(I\)–\(V\) characteristics

These go the opposite way to a metal wire

A hot wire has more resistance. A hot thermistor has less. Exam questions put the two side by side.

Reading the curves

Where a graph is steep, a small change in temperature or light makes a big change in resistance. That is where the component is most sensitive.

Thermistors and LDRs are semiconductors. Heat or light frees extra electrons to carry charge, so the resistance falls. The AQA specification asks only how the resistance changes: “The resistance of a thermistor decreases as the temperature increases.” “The resistance of an LDR decreases as light intensity increases.” (Physics 4.2.1.4, Combined Science 6.2.1.4)

Mission 6: \(I\)–\(V\) characteristics

Why does the total resistance change?

The LDR is in series with a fixed resistor, and series resistances add. When the LDR’s resistance falls, the total falls by the same amount.

Why does that change the current?

The supply stays at 6.0 V. The same p.d. across a smaller total resistance drives a bigger current, so the ammeter reading is a measure of brightness.

The two components also share the supply p.d., and the LDR’s share is large in the dark and small in bright light. Real sensor circuits use that changing p.d. to switch a lamp or an alarm.

RP \(I\)–\(V\) characteristics

Test each component

  1. Build the circuit
  2. Change the p.d. with the variable resistor
  3. Record \(V\) and \(I\) for several values
  4. Reverse the supply, then repeat
  5. Plot \(I\) against \(V\)
  6. Repeat: resistor, lamp, diode
Required practical: \(I\)–\(V\)

Why these three components?

They show three behaviours: constant resistance (resistor), resistance that rises with current (lamp), and resistance that depends on direction (diode).

Why does the diode need extra care?

Once it conducts its resistance is very low, so the current would shoot up and destroy it. A protective resistor in series limits the current, and a milliammeter reads the small currents.

Take diode readings in small steps near 0.6 V, where the current starts to rise sharply.

RP Get the negative half

Reverse & read

Required practical: \(I\)–\(V\)

What reversing does

Swapping the two leads at the supply sends the current round the other way. Nothing else in the circuit is changed. The meters now read negative, and those readings go in the bottom-left of the graph.

Which way round is the diode?

The triangle in its symbol points the way conventional current can pass. On a real diode, a stripe marks the end the triangle points to.

Deciding the diode is broken because the ammeter reads zero. In reverse, almost zero current is the correct result. Record it, with the negative p.d. from the voltmeter.

07 Know and apply

Two power equations

\(P = V\,I\)\(\text{power} = \text{p.d.} \times \text{current}\)\(P\) in W, \(V\) in V, \(I\) in A
Example230 V, 4.0 A → \(P = 230 \times 4.0 =\) 920 W
\(P = I^2\,R\)\(\text{power} = \text{current}^2 \times \text{resistance}\)\(P\) in W, \(I\) in A, \(R\) in Ω
Example2.0 A, 15 Ω → \(P = 2.0^2 \times 15 =\) 60 W
Square the current first: double \(I\) → \(4 \times\) the power
Mission 7: Power & the Grid

Where \(P = V\,I\) comes from

Volts are joules per coulomb. Amps are coulombs per second. Multiply them and the coulombs cancel: joules per second, which is power.

Why does doubling the current give four times the power?

To push twice the current through the same resistor you need twice the p.d. In \(P = V\,I\) both have doubled, and \(2 \times 2 = 4\). \(P = I^2\,R\) says the same in one step.

  • Charger → chemical store of battery

A powerful appliance used briefly can transfer less energy than a weak one left on all day.

Why does a high p.d. help?

The power station has a fixed power to send, and \(P = V\,I\). Raise the p.d. and the same power needs a smaller current.

Why does a small current matter so much?

The power wasted heating the cables is \(P = I^2\,R\). The current is squared: make the p.d. 10 times bigger, the current is 10 times smaller and the waste is 100 times smaller.

Saying a high p.d. makes the electricity travel faster. The point is the smaller current.

08 Two kinds of supply

dc vs ac

  • ac: keeps reversing direction

p.d.about 230 V

50 Hz = 50 complete cycles every second
Mission 8: Mains & safety

What do the electrons do in ac?

They move a tiny distance one way, then back, 50 times a second. They do not travel from the power station to your home. Energy is still transferred, because the electrons in the appliance are pushed back and forth too. In a heater, each push makes collisions that heat the element.

50 Hz on a graph

each complete cycle lasts \(1 \div 50 = 0.02\) s.

Mains is ac because transformers only work with a changing current, and the National Grid depends on them.

Why two wires for the circuit?

Current needs a complete loop. Live brings the alternating p.d. to the appliance and neutral is the way back, so the same current is in both.

Why is neutral near 0 V if it carries current?

It is connected to the ground at the local substation, which holds it close to 0 V.

Why a third wire?

Earth is not part of the working circuit. It joins the metal case to the ground, ready to carry current away if a fault makes the case live.

08 Staying safe

Why live is dangerous

  • Body at 0 V, live at 230 V
  • Touch live → current through you: shock
  • Still live with the switch off
  • Live–earth link → huge current, fire risk
  • Metal case is earthed: fault current → earth

“The earth wire is at 230 V.”

Earth is at 0 V.

Mission 8: Mains & safety

Why is it dangerous with the switch off?

The switch is a gap part-way along the live wire. The wire on the supply side of the gap is still connected to the mains at 230 V.

How earthing protects you, step by step

A loose live wire touches the metal case. The earth wire gives a very low-resistance path to the ground, so a very large current flows. That current melts the fuse in the live wire, which disconnects the appliance.

Double-insulated appliances, which usually have plastic cases, need no earth wire. No part that you can touch can become live.

09 Rub two insulators

Charging by rubbing

  • Friction moves electrons from one material to the other
  • Gains electrons → negative
  • Loses electrons → equally positive
  • Works for insulators: the charge cannot flow away

“Positive charges move onto the cloth.”

Only electrons move.

Mission 9: Static electricity

Why only electrons?

The positive charge in a material is in the nuclei of its atoms, held deep inside. Electrons on the outside of the atoms are the only charged particles loose enough to be rubbed off.

Why “equally”?

Charge is not created by rubbing, only moved. Every electron the rod gains is one the cloth has lost, so the rod’s negative charge and the cloth’s positive charge are the same size.

Which way do they go?

It depends on the pair of materials. Polythene rubbed with a cloth gains electrons and becomes negative; acetate rubbed with a cloth loses electrons and becomes positive.

A metal rod held in the hand cannot be charged this way: electrons flow through it and through you to earth as fast as they are moved.

09 Forces between charges

Attract or repel

  • Like charges repel
  • Unlike charges attract
  • A non-contact force
  • Closer together → bigger force
Charged balloon on a wall, hair standing on end, dust on a screen
Mission 9: Static electricity

Why does a charged balloon stick to an uncharged wall?

The negative balloon repels electrons in the wall’s surface a little way back. The surface nearest the balloon is left slightly positive, so the balloon is attracted to it. The repelled electrons are further away, so their push is weaker than the pull.

Testing a charged rod

Hang a rod whose charge you know and bring the unknown one near. If they repel, the charges are the same sign. Attraction does not prove the opposite sign, because a charged object also attracts an uncharged one.

Coulomb hung a light rod from a very thin thread, with a charged ball on one end. A second charged ball pushed it away and twisted the thread. The bigger the force, the bigger the twist.

Answer to the question

the forces between small charged balls are tiny, far smaller than the weight of a feather. A thin thread twists a long way for a very small force, so the twist was big enough to read on the scale at the top of the glass.

The slightest draught would have moved the rod more than the electric force did.

For GCSE, only that charges closer together push or pull harder. The exact “four times” rule is for A-level.

09 The space around a charge

Electric fields

  • A charged object has an electric field around it
  • Another charge in the field feels a force
  • Field is strongest close to the object
  • Lines show the force on a positive charge
Mission 9: Static electricity

Reading the diagram

The lines are closest together at the surface of the sphere and spread out further away. Where the lines are close the field is strong; where they spread, it is weaker.

Drawing one

Draw straight lines that start on the surface, at right angles to it, evenly spaced all the way round. Put an arrowhead part-way along each: pointing away from a positive sphere, towards a negative one.

How a field explains a force without contact

The first object does not need to touch the second. Its field fills the space around it, and the second object feels a force from the field where it is.

09 When the charge escapes

Why sparks jump

  • Charge builds up on an insulated object
  • The p.d. to earth and the field grow
  • Strong enough to ionise the air
  • Air conducts: electrons jump as a spark
Lightning and car-door shocks are sparks. Aircraft are earthed before refuelling
Mission 9: Static electricity

What the field does to the air

Air is normally an insulator. In a very strong field the electrons in air molecules are pulled hard one way and the nuclei the other, until electrons are torn off. The air is now full of ions and free electrons, which can carry charge, so a current flows across the gap.

Why earth a fuel tanker?

Fuel rushing through a pipe rubs against it and charge builds up. A wire to earth lets that charge flow away steadily, so the p.d. never gets high enough for a spark near the fuel vapour.
