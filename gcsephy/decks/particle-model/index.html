01 The particle model

Same particles, three states

Lesson 1: Solids, liquids and gases

What is the same in all three boxes?

The particles. A water molecule in ice is identical to one in steam: the same size, the same mass. Only two things differ, how the particles are arranged and how they move.

Why does a solid keep its shape?

Strong forces hold each particle in its place. The particle can vibrate, but it cannot swap places with a neighbour, so the solid cannot flow.

Why can a liquid flow but not be squashed?

Its particles can slide past each other, so it takes the shape of its container. But they are still touching, so there is no empty space to push them into.

In a particle diagram of a liquid, the particles touch. If you draw gaps between them, you have drawn a gas.

Lesson 1: Solids, liquids and gases

How to earn full marks on a “describe the particles” question

Give two things for each state: the arrangement and the motion. “The particles in a solid are close together” is half an answer. “They are close together in a regular pattern and vibrate about fixed positions” is a whole one.

Why “vibrate about fixed positions”?

Particles in a solid are never still. They shake to and fro around one spot. “They do not move” loses the mark.

Seal a syringe of air and push: the plunger moves in. Fill it with water and push: it does not move. That is the evidence for the last row of the table.

01 Six names to know

Changes of state

  • Boiling: at the boiling point, all through the liquid
  • Evaporating: at any temperature, from the surface
  • Sublimating: solid straight to gas
Orange arrows: heated. Blue arrows: cooled.
Lesson 1: Solids, liquids and gases

Why does a puddle dry up at 10 °C?

The particles in a liquid have a range of speeds. A few at the surface are moving fast enough to escape, even when the liquid is far below its boiling point. That is evaporation. Boiling is different: bubbles of gas form inside the liquid, and that happens only at the boiling point.

Where have you seen sublimation?

Solid carbon dioxide (“dry ice”) at a concert or in a science show. It never makes a puddle: it turns straight into cold gas.

01 A physical change

Mass is conserved

  • No particles added, none taken away
  • Same particles, rearranged
  • 50 g of ice → 50 g of water → 50 g of steam

“A new substance is made.”

Physical change: no new substance.

“It cannot be undone.”

Reverse it and the material recovers its original properties.

Lesson 1: Solids, liquids and gases

Then why does a boiling pan lose mass?

The pan is open, so particles leave it as steam. The mass is still conserved: the mass of the water left plus the mass of the steam in the room equals the mass you started with. In a sealed container the balance reading would not change.

How is this different from a chemical change?

Burn a piece of wood and you get ash and gases, which are new substances, and cooling them does not give you the wood back. Melt wax and cool it, and you have wax again, with the same properties.

02 Try it: change the temperature

Hotter means faster

Lesson 2: Gas particles and pressure

What does “random” mean here?

The molecules move in every direction, and at a range of speeds. At any moment some are fast and some are slow. That is why we talk about their average kinetic energy.

What is temperature, to a particle?

The temperature of a gas is related to the average kinetic energy of its molecules. Heating the gas transfers energy to the molecules, so on average they move faster. Nothing else about them changes: they do not grow, and in a sealed box there are no more of them.

About the model

Real molecules are far smaller and far faster than these. In the air around you they travel at about 500 m/s and collide with each other billions of times a second.

02 Billions of tiny pushes

What causes pressure?

  1. Molecules collide with the walls
  2. Each collision gives the wall a small force
  3. Billions of collisions every second
  4. Together: a steady force on each square metre, the pressure
Lesson 2: Gas particles and pressure

Why is the push steady, if each collision is a separate knock?

Think of heavy rain on an umbrella. Each drop is a separate tap, but there are so many that you feel one steady push. A gas does the same to a wall, with billions of molecules every second.

Is there pressure on you now?

Yes. The air presses on every square metre of your skin with a force of about 100 000 N. You are not crushed because the fluids inside your body push back with the same pressure.

Pressure on the walls comes from collisions with the walls. Collisions between molecules do not push on the container.

02 A chain of reasoning

Heat a sealed gas

  1. Gas heated: molecules gain kinetic energy, move faster
  2. They hit the walls more often
  3. Each collision has a bigger force
  4. The pressure increases
Lesson 2: Gas particles and pressure

Why does “constant volume” matter?

If the container could grow, like a balloon, the gas would spread out instead and the pressure would hardly change. A rigid, sealed container traps the same molecules in the same space, so faster molecules must mean more pressure.

So what about the aerosol can?

The can is rigid and sealed. In a fire, or on a sunny windowsill, the pressure inside climbs until the can splits. The warning on the label is this slide in one sentence.

Three marks, three links: faster molecules, more frequent collisions, bigger force in each collision. Then state the result: the pressure increases.

03 Know and apply

Mass per unit volume

\(\rho\) is the Greek letter rho
Example5400 kg of aluminium has a volume of 2.0 m³
Equation
\(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}\)
Substitute
\(\rho = \dfrac{5400}{2.0}\)
Answer
\(\rho = 2700\) kg/m³
Lesson 3: Density

What does 2700 kg/m³ tell you?

Every cubic metre of aluminium has a mass of 2700 kg. Density is the mass of one unit of volume, so it lets you compare materials fairly, whatever the size of the piece.

Does a bigger piece have a bigger density?

No. Cut the block in half and each half has half the mass and half the volume. The ratio is the same. Density belongs to the material, not to the object.

“Dense” does not mean “heavy”. The air in a classroom has a mass of about 200 kg, far more than a gold ring, yet air is the least dense thing in the room.

03 Explain it with particles

Why gases are so light

  • Solid and liquid: particles close together → high density
  • Gas: particles far apart → very low density
  • Water is about 800 times denser than air
Lesson 3: Density

Count the particles

Each box is the same size. The solid and the liquid boxes hold about the same number of particles, which is why a solid and its liquid have similar densities. The gas box holds only a few, so the same volume has far less mass.

Is a solid always denser than its liquid?

Nearly always, and only slightly: the particles pack a little closer in the solid. Water is the odd one out. In ice the molecules lock into an open pattern that takes up more room, so ice is less dense than water.

AQA asks you to “explain the differences in density between the different states of matter in terms of the arrangement of atoms or molecules” (Physics 4.3.1.1, Combined Science 6.3.1.1). You do not need to explain why ice is less dense than water.

Lesson 3: Density

Why multiply by 1000, when a kilogram is 1000 grams?

Two things change. A cubic metre is a cube 100 cm along each side, so it holds \(100 \times 100 \times 100 = 1\,000\,000\) cm³. One cubic metre of water is therefore a million grams, which is 1000 kg. So 1 g/cm³ is 1000 kg/m³.

A quick check on any answer

Most solids and liquids are between 0.5 and 20 g/cm³, or 500 and 20 000 kg/m³. If your metal comes out at 0.0027, you divided where you should have multiplied.

Lesson 3: Density

How to rearrange without a triangle

Start from \(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}\). Multiply both sides by \(V\) and you have \(m = \rho\,V\). Then divide both sides by \(\rho\) and you have \(V = \dfrac{m}{\rho}\). The same two moves work for every equation of this shape, including \(E = m\,L\) later in the unit.

Does the answer to Example 2 make sense?

Nearly a kilogram of gold fits in 50 cm³, about a quarter of a mug. That is the meaning of a very high density: a lot of mass in a small space.

BIG QUESTION

How do you find the volume of a stone?

Required practical: Density

RP Density: Part A

A regular solid

  1. Measure length, width, height: ruler or vernier callipers
  2. volume = length × width × height
  3. Measure the mass: balance
  4. \(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{64.8}{24} = 2.7\) g/cm³
Required practical: Density

Ruler or callipers?

A ruler reads to the nearest millimetre. On a side of 20 mm, being 1 mm out is a 5% error, and the volume uses three sides multiplied together, so the errors add up. Vernier callipers read to 0.1 mm, so they have a better resolution and the error is ten times smaller.

Why measure each side more than once?

A real block is rarely a perfect cuboid. Measuring each side in two or three places and taking a mean evens out a slightly uneven face.

RP Density: Part B

An irregular solid

Volume of stone = rise in the water level

Too big for the cylinder: catch the water it pushes out

Required practical: Density

Why does displacement work?

Water cannot be squashed, and two things cannot be in the same place. When the stone goes under, it pushes aside exactly its own volume of water, which has nowhere to go but up the cylinder or out of the spout.

Which should be measured first, the mass or the volume?

The mass. A wet stone carries drops of water, which would add to the balance reading and make the density too high.

Fill a displacement can until water runs from the spout, and wait for the last drip before you put the measuring cylinder under it.

RP Density: Part C

A liquid

  1. Mass of the empty cylinder
  2. Pour in the liquid; read its volume
  3. Mass of cylinder and liquid
  4. mass of liquid = 112.0 − 62.0 = 50.0 g
  5. \(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{50.0}{50} = 1.0\) g/cm³
Required practical: Density

Why not pour the liquid out to weigh it?

Some would stay behind, clinging to the glass. Weighing the cylinder empty and then full measures every drop that is in the cylinder when you read the volume.

Would a bigger volume give a better answer?

Yes. The scale can be read to about 1 cm³. That is 10% of a 10 cm³ sample but only 1% of a 100 cm³ sample.

RP Evaluate

Getting it right

  • Read at eye level, at the bottom of the meniscus
  • Balance reads zero before you start
  • Mass before the object gets wet
  • No splashes; no air bubbles on the object
  • Narrow cylinder: scale lines further apart

“The volume of the stone is 68 cm³.”

It is the change: 68 − 50 = 18 cm³.

“Human error.”

“An air bubble made the volume too big, so the density came out too small.”

Required practical: Density

Which way does each error push the answer?

Density is mass divided by volume. Anything that makes the volume too big (a bubble, reading the top of the meniscus on the second reading only) makes the density too small. Anything that makes the mass too big (a wet stone, a balance that did not start at zero) makes the density too big.

Why eye level?

Looking down on the scale makes the level seem higher than it is; looking up makes it seem lower. This is called parallax error.

Lesson 5: Internal energy

05 Definition

Internal energy

internal energytotal kinetic energy + total potential energy
of all the particles in the system
  • Kinetic: the particles are moving or vibrating
  • Potential: depends on how far apart the particles are
  • Heating → particles gain energy → internal energy increases
Lesson 5: Internal energy

Why do particles have potential energy?

Particles attract each other. To pull two of them apart you have to do work against that attraction, just as you do work to stretch a spring. The energy you supply is stored as potential energy. So particles that are further apart have more potential energy.

Is internal energy the same as temperature?

No. Temperature tells you only about the average kinetic energy of the particles. Internal energy is the total of both kinds, for every particle. A bath of warm water has far more internal energy than a red-hot spark, because it has so many more particles.

Equation\(\Delta E = m\,c\,\Delta\theta\) Lesson 7\(E = m\,L\) Lesson 6
Lesson 5: Internal energy

Why can it not do both at once?

At the melting point, any energy supplied is taken up at once by particles breaking free of their fixed positions. Until every particle has broken free, none of the energy is left over to make them move faster. So the temperature waits until the change of state is complete.

And when cooling?

The same, in reverse. As a liquid freezes, its particles move closer into fixed positions and their potential energy falls. That energy is transferred to the surroundings, and the temperature stays constant until all of it is solid.

05 Ice to steam, heated steadily

Slopes and flats

  • Slope: one state, getting hotter
  • Flat: changing state
  • First flat: melting point
  • Second flat: boiling point
On a flat, the heater is still on
Lesson 5: Internal energy

Why is the second flat so much longer?

The heater supplies energy at a steady rate, so a longer flat means more energy. Boiling needs far more energy than melting, because the particles must be pulled completely apart, not just freed to slide past each other.

Why are the slopes different?

Ice, water and steam need different amounts of energy to warm by each degree. Water needs the most, so its section is the least steep. Lesson 7 gives this a name: specific heat capacity.

To find a melting or boiling point, read the temperature of the flat section from the vertical axis.

05 The same story, backwards

Cooling curve

  • Liquid cools: particles slow down
  • Flat: freezing. Potential energy falls
  • Energy still leaves, temperature constant
  • Then the solid cools
Lesson 5: Internal energy

The practical: stearic acid

Melted stearic acid, a waxy solid, is left to cool in a tube while you read its temperature every minute. The temperature falls, holds steady at about 69 °C for several minutes, then falls again. The steady section is its freezing point, and it is the same temperature as its melting point.

Why does the curve get less steep at the end?

A hot object loses energy quickly to a cool room. As it gets closer to room temperature it loses energy more slowly, so the last part of the curve flattens out.

06 Definition

Energy to change state

specific latent heatthe energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance with no change in temperatureUnit: J/kg
  • Latent means hidden
  • Energy goes in; a thermometer shows no change
  • Internal energy still increases: potential energy of the particles
Lesson 6: Specific latent heat

Why “specific”?

In physics, “specific” means “for each kilogram”. Latent heat is the energy for whatever amount you have; specific latent heat is the energy for exactly 1 kg, which lets you compare one substance with another.

Where does the energy go, if not into temperature?

Into pulling particles away from their neighbours. That raises their potential energy. Their kinetic energy, and so the temperature, stays the same.

A definition question is worth two marks: one for “energy to change the state of 1 kg”, one for “with no change in temperature”.

Lesson 6: Specific latent heat

Why is there no temperature in the equation?

Because the temperature does not change during a change of state. All that matters is how much substance changes state (\(m\)) and how much energy each kilogram needs (\(L\)).

The equation is the definition, scaled up

\(L\) is the energy for 1 kg. For 2 kg you need twice as much, for 0.5 kg half as much. Multiplying by the mass is all the equation does.

Masses are often given in grams. \(L\) is per kilogram, so convert first: 60 g is 0.060 kg.

Lesson 6: Specific latent heat

Why is vaporisation so much bigger?

Melting only loosens the particles: they stay touching. Boiling pulls every particle away from all its neighbours and out into a gas. That takes nearly seven times as much energy for each kilogram of water.

The steam scald

Steam at 100 °C that lands on your skin first condenses, giving out 2 260 000 J for every kilogram. Only then is it water at 100 °C, which goes on to cool and transfer more. Boiling water gives you only that second part.

Why does sweating cool you?

To evaporate, sweat must take in latent heat, and it takes it from your skin.

06 Practical: melting ice

Measure \(L\) for ice

  1. Two funnels, same mass of ice. One has the heater
  2. Heater and joulemeter on for 5 minutes
  3. mass melted by heater = 42.0 − 6.0 = 36.0 g
  4. \(L = \dfrac{E}{m} = \dfrac{12\,000}{0.0360} = 333\,000\) J/kg
Lesson 6: Specific latent heat

What is the second funnel for?

It is a control. The room is warmer than the ice, so some ice melts with no heater at all. The control shows how much. Subtract it, and what is left is the ice melted by the energy the joulemeter measured.

Why must the ice be at 0 °C to begin with?

Ice straight from a freezer is at about −18 °C. Some of the heater's energy would go into warming it to 0 °C before any melted, and \(L\) would come out too big. Ice that is already wet and melting is at 0 °C.

Lesson 7: Specific heat capacity

What does 4200 J/kg °C mean?

It takes 4200 J to make 1 kg of water 1 °C hotter. The same 4200 J would make 1 kg of copper nearly 11 °C hotter. Water is hard to heat, and for the same reason it is slow to cool.

Why that matters

Water carries a lot of energy for a small temperature change, so it is used in central heating and to cool car engines. The sea warms slowly in summer and cools slowly in winter, which keeps places near the coast mild.

A high specific heat capacity does not mean “heats up quickly”. It means the opposite: more energy is needed for every degree.

\(\Delta E = m\,c\,\Delta\theta\)

Answer

Lesson 7: Specific heat capacity

Build the equation from the definition

\(c\) is the energy for 1 kg and 1 °C. For 2.0 kg you need twice as much, so multiply by the mass. For 80 °C you need 80 times as much, so multiply by the temperature change. That is all \(m\,c\,\Delta\theta\) is.

What the symbols mean

\(\Delta\) (delta) means “change in”. \(\theta\) (theta) is temperature. So \(\Delta\theta\) is the final temperature minus the starting temperature, and \(\Delta E\) is the change in the energy stored.

Is 672 000 J sensible?

A 3000 W kettle supplies 3000 J each second, so this takes \(672\,000 \div 3000 = 224\) s: nearly four minutes for a full kettle. That is about right.

Lesson 7: Specific heat capacity

One rule covers every rearrangement

The three quantities on the right are multiplied together. To get one of them alone, divide \(\Delta E\) by the other two.

On the calculator

Work out the bottom of the fraction first: \(0.50 \times 390 = 195\), then \(7800 \div 195\). Or put brackets round the bottom. Typing \(7800 \div 0.50 \times 390\) gives a wrong answer.

If the question asks for the final temperature

\(\Delta\theta\) is only the rise. Copper that starts at 20 °C and rises by 40 °C ends at 60 °C.

Lesson 7: Specific heat capacity

A two-step question

A kettle heats 0.20 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C, then boils it all away. The first step is a temperature change: \(\Delta E = m\,c\,\Delta\theta = 0.20 \times 4200 \times 80 = 67\,200\) J. The second is a change of state: \(E = m\,L = 0.20 \times 2\,260\,000 = 452\,000\) J. Add them: 519 200 J.

What that shows

Boiling the water away took nearly seven times as much energy as heating it from room temperature. That is why a kettle that fails to switch off wastes so much energy.

Look for a temperature change in the question. Two temperatures: use \(c\). “Melts”, “boils”, “freezes” or “condenses” at one temperature: use \(L\).

Required practical: Specific heat capacity

The method in order

Measure the mass of the block on a balance. Wrap it in insulation, and put the heater and the thermometer in their holes. Record the starting temperature. Switch on for a measured time and find the energy supplied. Record the highest temperature reached. Then \(c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m\,\Delta\theta}\).

Where does \(E = V\,I\,t\) come from?

From Electric Circuits. Power is \(P = V\,I\), and energy is \(E = P\,t\). Put the first into the second: \(E = V\,I\,t\). A joulemeter does this multiplication for you.

Why an electric heater and not a Bunsen burner?

You can measure the energy an electric heater supplies. Most of the energy from a flame goes into the air, and there is no simple way to measure how much reaches the block.

RP Results

From the results

  • 1.00 kg of aluminium, 12.0 V, 4.0 A, 600 s

Or: \(c = \dfrac{1}{m \times \text{gradient}}\)
Required practical: Specific heat capacity

Why does the graph curve at the start?

For the first minute the heater is warming itself up, and energy takes time to spread through the metal to the thermometer. After that, equal amounts of energy give equal temperature rises, and the graph is a straight line.

Why the gradient gives a better value

The gradient of the straight part is the temperature rise for each joule: \(\dfrac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta E}\). Since \(c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m\,\Delta\theta}\), that makes \(c = \dfrac{1}{m \times \text{gradient}}\). Here the gradient is 0.00106 °C/J, which gives 940 J/kg °C. The line uses every reading and leaves out the slow start, so it is closer to the accepted 900 J/kg °C than the 993 from the totals.

“The results were inaccurate.”

“Energy was transferred to the surroundings, so \(\Delta\theta\) was too small.”

Required practical: Specific heat capacity

What kind of error is this?

A systematic error: it pushes every result the same way, always too high. Repeating the experiment and taking a mean does not remove it. Only changing the method, with better insulation, makes it smaller.

What does the oil do?

Air is a poor conductor. Without oil there is a layer of air between the block and the thermometer bulb, and the thermometer reads lower than the block. Oil fills the gap, so the thermometer reaches the temperature of the metal.

Why does the temperature keep rising after switching off?

The heater and the metal next to it are hotter than the rest of the block. Energy carries on spreading outwards for a minute or so until the block is the same temperature all through.

Smaller volume, bigger pressure

Triple Science: Pressure and volume

Why does the pressure rise, if the molecules are no faster?

The temperature is constant, so the average speed is the same. But the same molecules are now in a smaller space. Each one has less distance to travel between walls, so it hits them more often. More collisions each second on each square centimetre means a bigger pressure.

Which way does the gas push?

At right angles to every surface it touches. Molecules arrive from all directions, so the sideways parts of their pushes cancel out and only the push straight into the wall is left. That is why a balloon is round.

Compare this with Lesson 2. There the gas was heated at constant volume: harder and more frequent collisions. Here it is squeezed at constant temperature: only more frequent.

Triple Science: Pressure and volume

What does “constant” mean?

Multiply the pressure of the gas by its volume and you get a number. Squeeze or release the gas, and the pressure and volume both change, but their product is the same number as before. In the example it is \(100 \times 60 = 6000\) before and \(300 \times 20 = 6000\) after.

Do I have to use Pa and m³?

Not when you compare before with after. Use the same unit for both pressures and the same unit for both volumes, and the answer comes out in those units.

The two conditions

“Fixed mass” means no gas gets in or out. “Constant temperature” means the gas is squeezed slowly, so it stays at the temperature of the room. If either changes, the equation does not hold.

Triple Science: Pressure and volume

Inversely proportional

Halve the volume and the pressure doubles. Make the volume three times bigger and the pressure falls to a third. Whatever you multiply one by, you divide the other by.

The rising bubble

Deep under water the pressure on a bubble is high. As it rises, the pressure on it falls, so its volume grows. A bubble released where the pressure is 300 kPa has three times the volume when it reaches the surface, where the pressure is 100 kPa.

Will the curve ever touch an axis?

No. However big the volume, the gas still has some pressure, and however great the pressure, the gas still takes up some space.

Doing work on a gas

  1. A force pushes the piston in: work is done on the gas
  2. Energy is transferred to the gas
  3. Its internal energy increases
  4. Its temperature rises
Triple Science: Pressure and volume

What is “work”?

Work is the transfer of energy by a force. When your force moves the piston, energy is transferred from you to the trapped air.

How does that make the molecules faster?

A molecule that hits a wall which is standing still bounces off at the same speed. A molecule that hits a piston moving towards it bounces off faster, like a tennis ball off a swinging racket. Every such collision adds a little kinetic energy, so the average kinetic energy, and the temperature, go up.

Why did the temperature stay constant on the earlier slides?

There the gas was squeezed slowly, so the extra energy had time to escape through the walls to the room. Pump up a bicycle tyre quickly and there is no time: the end of the pump gets hot.
