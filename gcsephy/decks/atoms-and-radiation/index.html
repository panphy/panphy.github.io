Why “relative”? The real charges and masses are awkwardly tiny, so everything is compared with a proton, which counts as mass 1 and charge +1.

Why does only the nucleus count for mass? An electron has about 1/2000 of the mass of a proton. Even all the electrons in a large atom add almost nothing. That is why the mass number counts protons and neutrons and ignores electrons.

Writing the electron’s mass as 0. It is very small, not zero.

How empty is “mostly empty”? Scale an atom up to a football stadium and the nucleus is a pea on the centre spot.

Using the numbers. One ten-thousandth of 1 × 10−10 m is 1 × 10−14 m. Dividing by 10 000 lowers the power of ten by 4, from −10 to −14.

Why can you not push your hand through a table? The electrons on the outside of your atoms repel the electrons on the outside of the table’s.

Why do the protons decide the element? The number of protons sets how many electrons the neutral atom holds, and that sets how it behaves chemically. Change the protons and it is a different element. Change the neutrons and it is the same element with a different mass.

Inside the atom

Isotopes.

  • Same element: same protons
  • Different numbers of neutrons
  • So different mass numbers
  • Some are unstable: carbon-14
ISOTOPES OF CARBONNOT TO SCALE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

Why are they the same element? All three have 6 protons and 6 electrons, so they react in exactly the same way. Only the mass of the nucleus differs.

Why are some unstable? A nucleus needs a suitable balance of protons and neutrons. Carbon-14 has too many neutrons for its 6 protons, so sooner or later it decays.

Living things stop taking in carbon-14 when they die. Measuring how much is left tells archaeologists how old a bone is.

Inside the atom

Atoms and ions.

  • Atom: electrons = protons
  • So no overall charge
  • Lose outer electron(s) → positive ion
  • The nucleus does not change
LITHIUMNOT TO SCALE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

Why is it electrons that move, and never protons? The outer electrons are far from the nucleus and weakly held. Protons are locked inside the nucleus. So an atom can only become charged by losing or gaining electrons.

Losing an electron gives a positive ion. Lithium has 3 protons and 3 electrons: +3 and −3 make zero. Take one electron away: +3 and −2 make +1. The atom has not gained anything positive. It has lost something negative.

Gaining an electron gives a negative ion. Fluorine has 9 protons and 9 electrons. Add one electron: +9 and −10 make −1.

Mixing up ions and isotopes. An ion has a different number of electrons. An isotope has a different number of neutrons.

The same thing happens at every step. A model explains the evidence of its day. An experiment gives a result it cannot explain. A new model is built that explains both the old evidence and the new.

Each model keeps what still works. The nuclear model kept the electrons and moved the positive charge to the centre. Bohr kept the nucleus and organised the electrons. The earlier scientists were not foolish; each model was the best explanation of what was then known.

Why did Thomson suggest this? He had shown that atoms contain negative electrons. Atoms are neutral, so there had to be positive charge to balance them. Nobody knew where it was, so he took the simplest idea: spread through the whole atom.

What it predicted. With charge and mass spread thinly, nothing is concentrated enough to push a fast particle off course. The next experiment tested exactly that.

Rutherford, Geiger & Marsden (1909–1911)

The alpha scattering experiment.

  • Fire alpha particles (+) at thin gold foil
  • Count them at all angles
  • Plum pudding predicts: all pass through
  • Only tiny deflections expected
ALPHA SCATTERINGNOT TO SCALE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

Why alpha particles? They are positive, fast and heavy. Positive charge inside the atom would push on them, and how far they were pushed would show how that charge is arranged.

Why gold? It can be beaten into an extremely thin foil, so an alpha particle is unlikely to be pushed off course more than once.

Why count at every angle, even back towards the source? Nobody expected anything there. Checking anyway led to the discovery.

Why do the few that bounced back tell us the most? To turn a fast, heavy alpha particle right round takes a large force from something much more massive than it. And because it happened so rarely, that something must be a very small target, seldom hit head-on.

Rutherford said it was as surprising as firing a shell at tissue paper and having it come back and hit you.

Check that the new model explains all three results.

Straight through: most alpha particles pass through empty space.

Deflected: one passing near the positive nucleus is repelled.

Bounced back: one heading straight at the nucleus is repelled so strongly that it reverses. The nucleus is tiny, so this is rare.

The plum pudding model could explain only the first.

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

What problem was Bohr solving? In the nuclear model, a circling electron should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. Atoms would collapse. They do not.

His idea: electrons can only be at certain fixed distances, like the rungs of a ladder. You can stand on a rung, not between rungs.

Why was it believed? Hot hydrogen gives out light of a few exact colours. Bohr calculated what those colours should be, and his numbers matched the measurements.

Why does this slide matter? The Bohr diagrams you draw show protons and neutrons. Bohr could not have drawn that: in 1913 the nucleus was just a small positive lump.

How were protons found? The charge on any nucleus turned out to be a whole number of equal units, which pointed to identical positive particles inside it.

Why did neutrons take until 1932? They have no charge, so the methods for detecting charged particles missed them.

Energy levels

Electrons can change level.

  • Absorb EM radiation → move further out
  • Emit EM radiation → move closer in
HYDROGEN ATOMNOT TO SCALE
− Electron+ Proton~ EM radiation
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

Think of the ladder. Climbing to a higher rung takes energy; stepping down gives it back. An electron that absorbs electromagnetic radiation moves up a level. One that drops down gives out the difference as electromagnetic radiation.

Why does “further out” mean “more energy”? The electron is attracted to the nucleus, so pulling it away takes energy, like lifting something off the ground.

The colours of fireworks come from electrons dropping back down. Each element has its own levels, so each gives its own colours.

Why did anyone suspect another particle? The masses did not add up. A helium nucleus has the charge of 2 protons but the mass of 4. Something with mass and no charge had to be in there.

What the neutron explains. Mass number, and isotopes: atoms of one element can have different masses because they have different numbers of neutrons.

Working scientifically

Why models change.

  • A model must explain the evidence
  • It makes predictions we can test
  • New evidence can change or replace it
  • Results are published and peer reviewed
THE SCIENCE CYCLE

The atom is the standard example. The plum pudding model predicted that alpha particles would pass almost straight through gold foil. Some bounced back. The model could not explain that, so it was replaced by one that could.

Is the current model “proved”? No model is. It is accepted because it explains all the evidence so far, and stays accepted until evidence turns up that it cannot explain.

Unstable nuclei

Radioactive decay.

  • Unstable nuclei give out radiation
  • They become more stable
  • Decay is random
  • Activity: decays per second, in becquerel (Bq)
  • Count rate: decays a detector records per second

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

What does “random” really mean? Nothing you do to the sample, heating, cooling or squashing it, makes a particular nucleus decay sooner. Each has the same chance of decaying in the next second as any other of its kind.

Why is count rate less than activity? Radiation leaves the source in all directions and the detector sits on one side, so most of it misses.

Move the detector further away and the count rate falls. The activity has not changed.

See where the numbers come from. An alpha particle carries away 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Four particles gone, so the mass number falls by 4. Two were protons, so the atomic number falls by 2.

Why do the checks work? No protons or neutrons are created or destroyed. They are shared between the new nucleus and the alpha particle. So the top numbers on the right add up to the top number on the left, and the bottom numbers do the same.

Where does the electron come from? There are no electrons in a nucleus. A neutron changes into a proton, and an electron is created at that moment and thrown out.

Why the −1? The bottom number tracks charge. A neutron (0) becomes a proton (+1) and an electron (−1), which still add up to 0.

Compare with electrons. An electron dropping to a lower energy level gives out electromagnetic radiation. A nucleus can do the same kind of thing: settle into a lower-energy arrangement and give out the difference. The energy is far larger, so the radiation is gamma, not light.

When does it happen? Often straight after an alpha or beta decay, when the new nucleus has energy to spare.

You can work the table out from what each one is.

Alpha is big with a charge of +2. It pulls electrons off almost every atom it passes, and each ionisation costs it energy, so it runs out within centimetres.

Beta is a tiny, fast electron. It ionises fewer atoms, loses energy more slowly and gets further.

Gamma has no charge or mass. It passes most atoms without affecting them, so it goes a very long way.

Why does paper stop alpha when lead struggles with gamma? Stopping radiation means taking its energy away. Alpha gives its energy to every atom it meets, so a thin sheet is enough. Gamma hardly interacts, so it takes a lot of dense material to absorb even some.

Ask why the other two types would fail.

Smoke alarm: beta and gamma would pass through smoke as if it were not there, and out through the case into the room.

Thickness gauge: alpha would be stopped by any paper; gamma would pass through any thickness unchanged.

Tracer: alpha and beta would be absorbed inside the body, doing damage and never reaching the detector.

Half-life

Half-life.

  • Time for the number of unstable nuclei to halve
  • Or: time for the count rate (activity) to halve
  • Same time for every halving
Iodine-131: half-life ≈ 8 days
DECAY CURVE: IODINE-131
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

Why does it halve, and not fall by the same amount each time? Every unstable nucleus has the same chance of decaying in the next second. So a fixed fraction of whatever is left decays in a given time, and half always takes the same time to go.

Why not a “full life”? Halving never reaches zero: a half, a quarter, an eighth. There is no definite moment when the last nucleus goes.

“After two half-lives it has all gone.” A quarter is left.

Every question is the same three numbers: a start value, an end value and a number of half-lives. Given two, find the third.

Working backwards. A count rate falls from 80 to 10 in 6 hours. Count the halvings: 80 → 40 → 20 → 10 is three. Three half-lives take 6 hours, so one takes 2 hours.

Dividing by the number of half-lives. Three half-lives is ÷ 2 three times, which is ÷ 8, not ÷ 3.

Random, but predictable

Random decay.

  • Can’t predict which nucleus or when
  • Large samples → predictable half-life
  • Small samples → counts vary

How can something random be predictable? Think of dice. You cannot say what one will show. Throw six thousand and close to a thousand will show a six. A sample has far more nuclei than that, so the time for half to decay comes out the same every time.

Why do small samples vary? Throw six dice and you might get no sixes, or three. A weak source jumps about in the same way, which is why count rates are measured over a longer time.

Staying safe

Contamination vs irradiation.

Gloves and tongsKeep your distanceShort exposureLead-lined storageDosimeter badge
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

A way to picture it. Irradiation is like standing in front of a heater. Walk away and it stops, and you have not become a heater. Contamination is like glitter on your clothes. You carry it with you until it is cleaned off.

Why each precaution works. Gloves and tongs keep radioactive material off your skin: they prevent contamination. Distance, a short exposure and lead-lined storage cut the radiation that reaches you: they reduce irradiation.
