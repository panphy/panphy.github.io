Inside the atom

Mostly empty space.

  • Atom radius ≈ 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m
  • Nucleus radius < 1/10 000 of the atom’s
  • Almost all the mass is in the nucleus
  • Electrons at different distances: energy levels
AN ATOMNOT TO SCALE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • The radius of an atom is about 1 × 10−10 m.
  • The radius of the nucleus is less than 1/10 000 of the radius of the atom.
  • Almost all the mass of the atom is in the nucleus. The rest of the atom is mostly empty space.
  • Electrons are at different distances from the nucleus, in different energy levels.

If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot.

1 × 10−10 m is 0.1 nm. Be ready to use standard form in a calculation with these sizes.

Inside the atom

Reading the symbol.

  • Atomic number = number of protons
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons
  • Neutrons = mass no. − atomic no.
  • Protons decide the element
Try: 2311Na → 11 p, 12 n, 11 e
NUCLEAR NOTATION
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Atomic number (bottom number) = number of protons.
  • Mass number (top number) = number of protons + number of neutrons.
  • Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
  • In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons.
  • The number of protons decides which element it is: every atom with 11 protons is sodium.

Reading the top number as the number of neutrons. The top number counts protons and neutrons together.

Inside the atom

Isotopes.

  • Same element: same protons
  • Different numbers of neutrons
  • So different mass numbers
  • Some are unstable: carbon-14
ISOTOPES OF CARBONNOT TO SCALE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
  • They have the same number of protons, so the same atomic number, but different mass numbers.
  • Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 all have 6 protons. They have 6, 7 and 8 neutrons.
  • Some isotopes have unstable nuclei and are radioactive, such as carbon-14.

A 2-mark definition needs both halves: same number of protons, different number of neutrons.

Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons, so they react chemically in the same way.

Inside the atom

Atoms and ions.

  • Atom: electrons = protons
  • So no overall charge
  • Lose outer electron(s) → positive ion
  • The nucleus does not change
LITHIUMNOT TO SCALE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • An atom is neutral: number of electrons = number of protons.
  • If an atom loses one or more outer electrons, it becomes a positive ion.
  • Only the electrons change. The nucleus, and so the element, stays the same.

“It gained a proton, so it is positive.” Protons do not move in or out when an ion forms. A positive ion has fewer electrons.

Atoms can also gain electrons to become negative ions. Radiation that knocks electrons out of atoms is called ionising radiation.

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Before the electron was discovered, atoms were thought to be tiny spheres that could not be divided.
  • The discovery of the electron showed that atoms contain smaller particles.
  • Plum pudding model: the atom is a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
  • There is no nucleus and no empty space in this model.

To describe the model for full marks, say all three: a ball of positive charge, electrons embedded in it, and the charge spread throughout.

Rutherford, Geiger & Marsden (1909–1911)

The alpha scattering experiment.

  • Fire alpha particles (+) at thin gold foil
  • Count them at all angles
  • Plum pudding predicts: all pass through
  • Only tiny deflections expected
ALPHA SCATTERINGNOT TO SCALE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Alpha particles (positively charged) were fired at a very thin sheet of gold foil.
  • Detectors counted how many came out at each angle.
  • The plum pudding model predicted that nearly all would go straight through, with only tiny deflections, because its positive charge is spread thinly through the atom.
  • The experiment was a test of a prediction. The results did not match it.

The foil had to be extremely thin, so that an alpha particle met few atoms on its way through. The apparatus was in a vacuum, because air would stop the alpha particles.

Rutherford (1911)

The nuclear model.

  • A tiny, dense nucleus at the centre
  • The nucleus is positive
  • Almost all the mass is in it
  • Electrons outside the nucleus
  • Replaced the plum pudding model

  • Nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre, with the electrons outside it.
  • Almost all the mass of the atom is in the nucleus.
  • It replaced the plum pudding model because it explained the alpha scattering results and the plum pudding model could not.

A problem remained: why do the negative electrons not fall into the positive nucleus? Bohr’s model, two years later, was an answer to that question.

  • Niels Bohr adapted the nuclear model: electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances.
  • These fixed orbits are the energy levels.

“Why was Bohr’s model accepted?” The answer is the agreement between his calculations and the results of experiments.

The experiments were measurements of the light given out by hydrogen. Bohr’s energy levels predicted its colours correctly.

Energy levels

Electrons can change level.

  • Absorb EM radiation → move further out
  • Emit EM radiation → move closer in
HYDROGEN ATOMNOT TO SCALE
− Electron+ Proton~ EM radiation
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Electrons can move between energy levels.
  • Absorbing electromagnetic radiation moves an electron to a higher energy level, further from the nucleus.
  • Emitting electromagnetic radiation moves an electron to a lower energy level, closer to the nucleus.

Mixing the directions. Energy in → further out. Energy out → closer in.

If an atom absorbs enough energy, an outer electron can leave the atom completely. The atom is then a positive ion.

Filling in the nucleus

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • The nucleus was accepted before anyone knew what it was made of.
  • Later experiments led to the idea that the positive charge of a nucleus can be divided into a whole number of smaller particles, each with the same positive charge. These were named protons.
  • James Chadwick’s experiments (1932) gave evidence for the neutron, about 20 years after the nucleus became accepted.

Neutrons were hard to find because they have no charge. Electric and magnetic fields do not deflect them, and they do not ionise atoms directly.

Working scientifically

Why models change.

  • A model must explain the evidence
  • It makes predictions we can test
  • New evidence can change or replace it
  • Results are published and peer reviewed
THE SCIENCE CYCLE
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • A scientific model is used to explain observations and to make predictions that can be tested.
  • If new evidence does not match the model, the model is changed or replaced.
  • Results are published and peer reviewed: other scientists check the method and the conclusions, and try to repeat the results.

The atom is the standard example. Plum pudding made a prediction; alpha scattering did not match it; the nuclear model replaced it.

Nuclear radiation.

Alpha, beta, gamma, half-life

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION

Unstable nuclei

Radioactive decay.

  • Unstable nuclei give out radiation
  • They become more stable
  • Decay is random
  • Activity: decays per second, in becquerel (Bq)
  • Count rate: decays a detector records per second
DECAY AND DETECTIONNOT TO SCALE
α Alphaβ Betaγ Gamman Neutron
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Some atomic nuclei are unstable. An unstable nucleus gives out radiation as it changes to become more stable: this is radioactive decay.
  • Decay is random: you cannot predict which nucleus will decay next, or when.
  • Activity is the rate at which a source of unstable nuclei decays. It is measured in becquerel (Bq): 1 Bq is 1 decay per second.
  • Count rate is the number of decays recorded each second by a detector, such as a Geiger–Müller tube.

Treating count rate and activity as the same thing. A detector only picks up the radiation that reaches it, so the count rate is less than the activity.

The radiation may be an alpha particle, a beta particle, a gamma ray or a neutron.

  • In beta decay, a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton and an electron. The high-speed electron leaves the nucleus: this is the beta particle.
  • The mass number stays the same: one neutron fewer, one proton more.
  • The atomic number goes up by 1, so it becomes a different element.
  • The beta particle is written with 0 on top and −1 below.

“The beta particle is one of the electrons from the energy levels.” It comes from the nucleus, made when a neutron changes into a proton.

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • A gamma ray is electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus.
  • It has no mass and no charge, so emitting it changes neither the mass number nor the atomic number.
  • The nucleus only loses energy. It stays the same element.

Gamma emission often follows alpha or beta decay, when the new nucleus is left with extra energy. The “m” in technetium-99m marks a nucleus in that higher-energy state.

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Alpha: helium nucleus, charge +2, strongly ionising, a few centimetres in air, stopped by paper or skin.
  • Beta: fast electron, charge −1, moderately ionising, about a metre in air, stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.
  • Gamma: electromagnetic wave, no charge, weakly ionising, travels a long way in air, reduced by thick lead or concrete.

The order alpha, beta, gamma runs from most ionising to least ionising, and from least penetrating to most penetrating.

Alpha is the most ionising because it has the largest charge and mass, so it knocks electrons out of many atoms in a short distance.

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • More ionising means less penetrating.
  • Each time radiation ionises an atom, it loses some energy. Alpha ionises so strongly that it loses all its energy in a few centimetres of air.
  • Gamma ionises weakly, so it keeps its energy and travels much further.

To identify a source, put absorbers in front of a detector one at a time. Count rate falls with paper → alpha. Falls with aluminium but not paper → beta. Only falls with thick lead → gamma.

“Lead stops gamma.” Thick lead reduces it; some gamma always gets through.

ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Choose a source by three properties: penetrating power, ionising power and half-life.
  • Smoke alarm: alpha. It ionises the air well, cannot get out of the casing, and a long half-life means the source lasts for years.
  • Thickness gauge: beta. Thicker paper absorbs more, so the count rate shows the thickness. Alpha would all be stopped; gamma would all pass through.
  • Medical tracer: gamma. It passes out of the body to a detector and is weakly ionising. A short half-life means the activity soon falls.

For “explain why this source is suitable”, give each property and why it matters for that job.

Half-life

Half-life.

  • Time for the number of unstable nuclei to halve
  • Or: time for the count rate (activity) to halve
  • Same time for every halving
Iodine-131: half-life ≈ 8 days
DECAY CURVE: IODINE-131
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Learn both forms of the definition. Half-life is the time it takes for the number of nuclei of the isotope in a sample to halve.
  • Or: the time it takes for the count rate (or activity) from a sample to fall to half its starting level.
  • The half-life of an isotope is constant. Every halving takes the same time, whatever value you start from.

“After two half-lives it has all gone.” After two half-lives a quarter is left: a half, then half of that.

Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years. A source with a short half-life is very active at first, but its activity falls quickly.

  • From a graph: read the starting value, halve it, read across to the curve and down to the time axis.
  • From numbers: number of half-lives = total time ÷ half-life. Then halve the starting value that many times.

Check a half-life from a graph twice: 400 → 200 and 200 → 100 should take the same time.

Dividing the count by the number of half-lives. Three half-lives means ÷ 2, ÷ 2, ÷ 2, which is ÷ 8, not ÷ 3.

Random, but predictable

Random decay.

  • Can’t predict which nucleus or when
  • Large samples → predictable half-life
  • Small samples → counts vary
64 NUCLEI
Undecayed nucleus Decayed nucleus
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Random means you cannot predict which nucleus will decay, or when a particular nucleus will decay.
  • With a very large number of nuclei the overall pattern is predictable: half of them decay in each half-life.
  • With a small sample, or a low count rate, the readings vary noticeably from one count to the next.

Dice are a good model. You cannot say which dice will show a six on the next throw, but with a thousand dice about one in six will, every time.

This is why count rates are measured over a longer time, or repeated and averaged.

Staying safe

Contamination vs irradiation.

Gloves and tongsKeep your distanceShort exposureLead-lined storageDosimeter badge
ATOMS & NUCLEAR RADIATION
  • Irradiation: an object is exposed to nuclear radiation. The object does not become radioactive, and the exposure stops when the source is moved away or shielded.
  • Contamination: unwanted radioactive atoms get onto or into an object. The object stays hazardous for as long as those atoms keep decaying.
  • Precautions: gloves and tongs prevent contamination; distance, shielding and a short exposure time reduce irradiation.

Outside the body, gamma and beta are the bigger hazard because alpha is stopped by skin. Inside the body, an alpha source is the most dangerous, because it is so strongly ionising.

“Irradiated food is radioactive.” Irradiation does not make an object radioactive.
