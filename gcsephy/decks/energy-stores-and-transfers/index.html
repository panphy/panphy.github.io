Lesson 1: Stores and systems

Why the bars matter. Each bar is the energy in one store at that instant. As the ball drops, height turns into speed and back again, but the three bars always add up to the same total. That one fact, that the total never changes, is the whole of this unit.

Where does the "lost" height go? With friction on, the ball and the track get very slightly warmer. That warmth is energy in the thermal store. Nothing has vanished: it has moved to a store we cannot easily get back.

Lesson 1: Stores and systems

A store is a place to keep count. Physicists track energy like money in bank accounts: each store is an account, and a change moves joules from one account to another. The total in all the accounts never changes.

Thermal is the store everything ends up in. Hot things have faster-moving particles. Almost every real process leaves a little extra energy in the thermal store of the surroundings, which is why it turns up in nearly every answer in this unit.

02

03

04

Light, sound and electricity are not stores. They are ways energy travels between stores.
Lesson 1: Stores and systems

Why is light not a store? You cannot keep light in a box. The moment it is made, it travels away and is absorbed, warming whatever it hits. Light is the delivery van, not the warehouse.

"Electrical energy" is a trap. Write "energy is transferred electrically". The battery's chemical store empties and some other store fills; the current is just the route.

01 What are we looking at?

Closed systems

  • A system: the object or group of objects we choose to study
  • A closed system: no energy gets in or out
  • In a closed system the total energy stays the same
  • Energy only moves between stores inside it

Example

Ball + Earth

A falling ball and the Earth form a closed system. The energy that leaves the gravitational potential store arrives in the kinetic store. Nothing enters from outside.

Lesson 1: Stores and systems

Why choose a system at all? Choosing what is "inside" decides what counts as a transfer. Draw a boundary round a hot drink and the air: no energy leaves, the drink cools and the air warms. Draw it round the drink alone and energy leaves it by heating. Both are right; they are different systems.

The calculator trap. Typing \(0.5 \times 1200 \times 15\) and then pressing \(x^2\) squares everything: you get 81 000 000, not 135 000. Work out \(15^2 = 225\) on its own line first, then multiply.

Lesson 2: The kinetic store

Why it matters on the road. To stop, the brakes must transfer all of the kinetic energy to their thermal store. At 60 mph a car carries four times the energy it has at 30 mph, so with the same braking force it needs about four times the distance to stop.

Why mass and speed are not equal partners. Mass appears once in the equation; speed appears twice (squared). Speed always wins.

Lesson 3: Gravitational store

03 Watch

Lift it up

  • A 2.0 kg book is lifted onto a shelf
  • Only the vertical lift, \(h\), fills the G.P.E. store
  • Sliding it across onto the shelf adds no height
  • Let it fall: the energy goes to the kinetic store
Lift the bookinteractive
Lesson 3: Gravitational store

Why only the vertical part? Gravity pulls straight down, so energy goes into the gravitational potential store only when the book moves up against that pull. Sliding it sideways along the shelf does not raise it, so it adds nothing to the store.

Same lift, any route: lift the book straight up, or carry it up a slope, and it gains the same energy, because it ends at the same height.

Talk about itWhat two things would you need to know to work out how much energy the lake stores?

Lesson 3: Gravitational store

Answer to the question: the mass of water and its height above the turbines (with \(g\), which is given). Every cubic metre of water has a mass of 1000 kg; falling 500 m, it gives up \(1000 \times 9.8 \times 500 \approx 4.9\) million joules.

Why build it? Power stations cannot start up in seconds; falling water can. The lake is filled when electricity is cheap and spare, and emptied when it is needed fast.

Where does the energy come from? The hiker's chemical store (food). The equation gives only the energy that ends up in the gravitational store; the hiker also warms up, so they used more than 161 700 J.

Lesson 4: Stretching springs

04 Changing shape

Two forces

  • To stretch a spring and keep it still: two forces, one at each end
  • Here: the clamp pulls the top up; the load pulls the bottom down
  • Elastic deformation: goes back to its original length
  • Inelastic deformation: stays stretched
Lesson 4: Stretching springs

Why two forces? Pull one end of a spring lying on a table and it just slides towards you, still the same length. Something must hold the other end back. A spring stays stretched only while it is pulled both ways at once.

Each force acts where it touches. The clamp's pull acts at the top hook; the load's pull acts at the bottom hook. That is why the arrows start at the ends of the spring.

04 Measure the stretch

Extension, not length

Extension= stretched length − original length
  • No load: 5.0 cm long
  • With a load: 9.0 cm long
  • \(\text{Extension} = 9.0 - 5.0 =\) 4.0 cm
Lesson 4: Stretching springs

Why not the length? Hooke's law says the extension is proportional to the force. The length is not: double the force on a 5.0 cm spring and it goes from 9.0 cm to 13.0 cm, not to 18 cm. Plot lengths and your line will not pass through the origin.

04 Know and apply

Lesson 4: Stretching springs

What does 50 N/m mean? It would take 50 N to stretch this spring by a whole metre, if it did not break first. More usefully: 0.5 N per centimetre. A stiffer spring has a bigger \(k\).

Why metres? \(k\) is in newtons per metre. Use 4.0 instead of 0.040 and you get 0.5 N/m, a hundred times too small, and every energy you calculate from it in Lesson 5 is wrong too.

Lesson 4: Stretching springs

Mass is not a force. Mass is how much stuff there is (kg); force is a push or pull (N). On the Moon the same 100 g mass would pull the spring with only 0.16 N, and the spring would stretch far less.

The rounding is a small systematic error. Each 100 g really pulls with 0.98 N, not 1 N, so every force we record is 2% too big and our \(k\) comes out 2% too big. It is the same error every time, just like a ruler with a worn end.

Lesson 5: Elastic store

Why a half? The force is not constant while you stretch: it starts at zero and rises to \(k\,e\). On average you pull with half the final force, \(\tfrac{1}{2}k\,e\), through the distance \(e\). Average force \(\times\) distance \(=\) \(\tfrac{1}{2}k\,e \times e = \tfrac{1}{2}k\,e^2\).

The centimetre disaster. Put \(e = 10\) instead of 0.10 into example 2 and you get 1500 J, ten thousand times too big. A lab spring stores fractions of a joule.

Why squared? The further you stretch, the harder you have to pull. The second centimetre needs more force than the first, so it takes more work. Double the stretch and you pull twice as hard on average for twice the distance: \(2 \times 2 = 4\) times the energy.

Back to the big question: pulling the bow twice as far gives the arrow about four times the energy, not twice.

The explanation. To climb a hill as high as the first, the train would need all of its starting energy back in its gravitational potential store. Friction and air resistance dissipate some energy on every metre of track, so it never has enough. Every later hill must be lower, or the train stops part-way up.

Lesson 8: Wasted energy

08 Every real transfer

Some is always dissipated

  • Some energy always goes to the thermal store of the surroundings
  • It is spread out: dissipated. We call it "wasted"
  • It is not destroyed. We just cannot use it again

"The energy is lost."

"The energy is dissipated to the thermal store of the surroundings."

"Insulation keeps the cold out."

"Insulation slows the energy transfer from the warm house to the cold outside."

Lesson 8: Wasted energy

Why can't we use it again? Dissipated energy is spread very thinly: a cup of tea's thermal energy ends up warming the whole room by a fraction of a degree. There is still the same number of joules, but spread so thin that it can no longer do anything useful.

There is no "cold" to keep out. Energy always moves from hot to cold. In winter it flows out of your house; insulation slows that flow.

08 Fight the waste

Reduce unwanted transfers

Friction

Lubricate

Oil or grease lets parts slide past each other easily, so less energy goes to the thermal store.

Air resistance

Streamline

A smooth shape means the air pushes back less, so less energy is dissipated in the air.

Conduction

Insulate

Materials with a low thermal conductivity slow the transfer through walls and roofs.

Lesson 8: Wasted energy

Feel it. Rub your hands together hard: they warm up. That is friction transferring energy to the thermal store. In a dry bicycle chain the same thing happens at every link, and the energy comes from your legs. Oil lets the links slide, so more of your effort goes into moving the bike.

08 Conduction

Thermal conductivity

  • Higher thermal conductivity → energy conducted faster
  • Pins drop off copper first, glass last
  • Air, wool, foam: low conductivity, good insulators
  • A house cools more slowly with thicker walls of lower conductivity
Wax-and-pin rodsdemo
Lesson 8: Wasted energy

Why trapped air works. Air has a very low thermal conductivity, but loose air moves and carries energy away with it. Wool, foam, bubble wrap and loft insulation trap the air in tiny pockets so it cannot move. That is why so many insulators are fluffy.

Why metal feels cold. A metal bench and a wooden bench in the same park are at the same temperature. The metal feels colder because its high conductivity takes energy from your hand faster.

The one-second check. Efficiency can never be more than 1 (100%): that would mean more useful energy out than went in, and energy cannot be created. If you get 1.25, you have divided the wrong way up.

Finding the input: a motor 0.80 efficient gives 400 J usefully. \(\text{Total} = \dfrac{400}{0.80} = 500\) J. Check: the input must be bigger than the useful output.

Two equations have a square. \(E_k\) and \(E_e\) both square one quantity, so doubling the speed or the extension gives four times the energy. In this unit you only use them forwards, to find the energy.

Choosing an equation: list what you are given and what you want, then pick the equation that has exactly those quantities. If none fits, it is a two-step question: the first step finds the missing quantity, such as \(k\) from \(F = ke\).
