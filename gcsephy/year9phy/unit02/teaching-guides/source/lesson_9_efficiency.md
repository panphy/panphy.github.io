# Lesson 9: How Much Is Useful?

## Unit
Energy: Stores and Transfers

## Lesson focus
Students calculate efficiency from energy or power, as a decimal and a percentage, find the useful output from a given efficiency, measure the efficiency of a bouncing ball, and draw and read Sankey diagrams.

## Specification
AQA GCSE Physics 4.1.2.2 Efficiency. The specification asks students to recall and apply both efficiency equations; both are also on the AQA Physics equation sheet. Sankey diagrams are not in the specification; they are used here to picture efficiency.

## Scope
Students calculate efficiency, and rearrange to find the useful output or the total input.

## Learning objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

1. Calculate efficiency from energy or power, as a decimal and as a percentage.
2. Explain why no real device is 100% efficient.
3. Calculate the useful output or the total input from the efficiency.
4. Draw and interpret a Sankey diagram.
5. Describe ways to increase the efficiency of an energy transfer.

## Key vocabulary

| Keyword | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|
| Useful output | The energy (or power) transferred to the store we want |
| Wasted output | The energy (or power) dissipated to stores we do not want, usually thermal |
| Total input | All the energy (or power) supplied |
| Efficiency | The fraction of the input that is useful |
| Sankey diagram | A diagram in which the width of each arrow shows the amount of energy |

## The equations

> efficiency = useful output energy transfer ÷ total input energy transfer  
> efficiency = useful power output ÷ total power input

Efficiency has no unit. As a decimal it is between 0 and 1; multiply by 100 for a percentage.

Rearranged (with efficiency as a decimal): useful output = efficiency × total input, and total input = useful output ÷ efficiency.

Also:

> total input = useful output + wasted output

## Equipment and resources

- Per group: two or three different balls, metre rule clamped vertically, a smartphone to film the bounce (optional)
- Sankey-diagram grid in the workbook; rulers, coloured pencils
- Calculators

## Starter: Where does the 100 J go?

A lamp is supplied with 100 J. 20 J is transferred by light, 80 J heats the surroundings.

Ask: how much is useful? How much is wasted? Is any destroyed? (20 J; 80 J; none.)

## Main teaching: Calculating efficiency

Worked example 1 (energy, as a decimal and a percentage):

> A motor is supplied with 500 J and transfers 400 J to the kinetic store of the load. Calculate its efficiency.  
> efficiency = useful output ÷ total input  
> efficiency = 400 ÷ 500  
> efficiency = 0.80, or 80%

Worked example 2 (power):

> A 2000 W kettle has a useful power output of 1800 W, heating the water. Calculate its efficiency.  
> efficiency = useful power output ÷ total power input  
> efficiency = 1800 ÷ 2000  
> efficiency = 0.90, or 90%

Worked example 3 (finding the useful output):

> A 60 W lamp has an efficiency of 25%. Calculate its useful power output.  
> efficiency = 25% = 0.25  
> useful output = efficiency × total input  
> useful output = 0.25 × 60  
> useful output = 15 W

Key check: efficiency can never be greater than 1 (100%). If an answer is, the numbers are upside down.

## Practical: How efficient is a bounce?

1. Drop a ball from 1.00 m, measured from the bottom of the ball.
2. Measure the height of the first bounce (filming against the metre rule helps). Repeat three times; find the mean.
3. Repeat for other balls.

At the top of each bounce, all the useful energy is in the gravitational potential store, and the mass and g are the same, so:

> efficiency = Eₚ after ÷ Eₚ before = m g h₂ ÷ m g h₁ = h₂ ÷ h₁

Sample: drop height 1.00 m, mean bounce height 0.65 m, efficiency = 0.65 ÷ 1.00 = 0.65, or 65%.

Ask: where did the other 35% go? (Thermal store of the ball and floor; sound.) This links back to the shock absorbers of Unit 1, Lesson 3: a good shock absorber is an inefficient bounce.

## Main teaching: Sankey diagrams

Draw the lamp from the starter on squared paper: a 100 J arrow 10 squares wide, splitting into a 20 J useful arrow (2 squares) going straight on and an 80 J wasted arrow (8 squares) bending downwards.

Rules:

1. The width of each arrow is proportional to the energy.
2. Useful energy goes straight on; wasted energy bends away (usually down).
3. Input width = useful width + wasted width.

Students then read efficiency from a given Sankey diagram, and draw one for the motor in worked example 1.

## Increasing efficiency

| Way | Reduces |
|---|---|
| Lubricate moving parts | Energy transferred by friction to the thermal store |
| Streamline the shape | Energy transferred to the thermal store of the air by air resistance |
| Insulate | Energy transferred by heating to the surroundings |
| Use an LED instead of a filament lamp | Energy transferred by heating to the surroundings |

## Check for understanding

1. A device transfers 300 J usefully from 1200 J supplied. What is its efficiency? (0.25, or 25%)
2. Why can efficiency never be more than 100%?
3. A 400 W motor is 75% efficient. Calculate its useful power output. (300 W)
4. A lamp is 0.20 efficient and gives 12 J by light. How much energy was supplied? (60 J)
5. On a Sankey diagram, how do you know which arrow is the wasted energy?

## Plenary

Students complete: "A device can never be 100% efficient, because …" (some energy is always dissipated, usually to the thermal store of the surroundings).

## Possible prep

Complete the Sankey diagram page. Stretch: a car engine is about 30% efficient. Draw its Sankey diagram, and suggest two ways to increase its efficiency.

## Teacher notes

1. Students put the numbers upside down. Check every answer is at most 1.
2. Students forget to convert a percentage to a decimal before multiplying.
3. Students give efficiency a unit (J or W). It has none.
4. In the bounce practical, ask why the mass "cancels". Only stronger students will see it; the rest can use h₂ ÷ h₁ as given.
5. Week 5's second lesson is set aside for revision and catch-up before the test in week 6.
