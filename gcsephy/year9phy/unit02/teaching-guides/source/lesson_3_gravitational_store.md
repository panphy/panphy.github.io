# Lesson 3: How High Can You Go?

## Unit
Energy: Stores and Transfers

## Lesson focus
Students meet gravitational field strength as "newtons per kilogram", calculate the energy in the gravitational potential store with Eₚ = m g h, and rearrange it to find a height.

## Specification
AQA GCSE Physics 4.1.1.2 Changes in energy. The equation is on the AQA Physics equation sheet, and g is always given in calculations. This unit uses g = 9.8 N/kg throughout.

## Scope
Students calculate Eₚ, and rearrange to find h or m.

Weight (W = m g) is taught later, in the Forces unit. This lesson only needs the idea that gravity pulls each kilogram down with 9.8 N. That one sentence is reused in Lesson 4.

## Learning objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

1. Explain what the gravitational field strength g means.
2. Calculate the energy in the gravitational potential store using Eₚ = m g h.
3. Use the change in height, not the height above the ground, when an object moves between two points.
4. Rearrange the equation to find a height or a mass.

## Key vocabulary

| Keyword | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|
| Gravitational potential store | The energy an object has because it is raised in a gravitational field |
| Gravitational field strength (g) | The force of gravity on each kilogram; 9.8 N/kg on Earth |
| Newton (N) | The unit of force |
| Height (h) | The vertical distance an object is raised, in metres (m) |

## The equation

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Gravitational potential energy | Eₚ | joule (J) |
| Mass | m | kilogram (kg) |
| Gravitational field strength | g | newton per kilogram (N/kg) |
| Height | h | metre (m) |

> Eₚ = m g h  
> gravitational potential energy = mass × gravitational field strength × height

## What g means

> On Earth, gravity pulls every kilogram down with a force of 9.8 newtons. That is what 9.8 N/kg means.

Hand round a 1 kg mass. That pull is 9.8 N. On the Moon it would be 1.6 N; on Jupiter, about 25 N. So lifting the same object through the same height stores less energy on the Moon.

Do not introduce W = m g as an equation. Students will meet it in Forces.

## Equipment and resources

- A 1 kg mass; a set of everyday objects with masses labelled
- Metre rules or a tape measure
- Calculators

## Starter: Which stores more?

Rank by energy in the gravitational potential store:

1. A 1 kg bag of sugar on a 1 m table
2. The same bag on a 2 m shelf
3. A 2 kg bag on the 1 m table
4. The 1 kg bag on a 1 m table on the Moon

Students should reach: 2 and 3 are equal and largest; 1 next; 4 smallest. Ask what the three things are that matter (mass, height, strength of gravity).

## Main teaching: Calculating Eₚ

Write the equation, substitute, calculate.

Worked example 1:

> A 2.0 kg book is lifted onto a shelf 1.5 m high. Calculate the energy transferred to its gravitational potential store. (g = 9.8 N/kg)  
> Eₚ = m g h  
> Eₚ = 2.0 × 9.8 × 1.5  
> Eₚ = 29.4 J

Worked example 2 (change in height):

> A 55 kg hiker climbs from a car park 120 m above sea level to a summit 420 m above sea level. Calculate the increase in the energy in their gravitational potential store.  
> h = 420 − 120 = 300 m  
> Eₚ = m g h  
> Eₚ = 55 × 9.8 × 300  
> Eₚ = 161 700 J

Point out that the car park's height above sea level does not matter; only the height gained does.

## Main teaching: Finding the height

Rearrange in symbols first, then substitute.

> Eₚ = m g h  
> Divide both sides by m g:  
> h = Eₚ ÷ (m g)  
> In the same way, m = Eₚ ÷ (g h)

Worked example 3:

> A 60 kg climber gains 17 640 J in their gravitational potential store. How high did they climb?  
> h = Eₚ ÷ (m g)  
> h = 17 640 ÷ (60 × 9.8)  
> h = 17 640 ÷ 588  
> h = 30 m

Common error: dividing by 60 and then multiplying by 9.8. Teach students to work out m g first, or to use brackets on the calculator.

## Activity 1: Calculation page

Workbook page in three sections:

1. Straight substitution (masses in kg, then in g).
2. Change in height (stairs, lifts, a ski slope).
3. Finding h or m, including one in kilojoules (convert to joules first).

## Activity 2: Lift it

Pairs choose three objects, measure the mass and the height of a shelf or bench, and calculate the energy transferred when each object is lifted onto it. Then: how high would you have to lift the lightest object for it to store the same energy as the heaviest? (Uses h = Eₚ ÷ (m g).)

## Check for understanding

1. What does g = 9.8 N/kg mean?
2. Calculate Eₚ for a 5.0 kg bag lifted 2.0 m. (98 J)
3. Why would the same lift store less energy on the Moon?
4. A 0.50 kg ball gains 24.5 J. How high was it lifted? (5.0 m)
5. A crate lifted 2.0 m gains 392 J. What is its mass? (20 kg)

## Plenary

Show a photograph of a reservoir behind a dam. Ask: where is the energy stored, and what would you need to know to calculate it? (The mass of water and its height above the turbines.) Link forward: in Lesson 6, that energy is transferred to the kinetic store as the water falls.

## Possible prep

Your school bag has a mass of about 5 kg. Calculate the energy transferred to its gravitational potential store when you carry it up one flight of stairs (about 3 m). Then calculate it for the whole school day's stair climbing.

## Teacher notes

Common mistakes:

1. Using height above the ground instead of the change in height.
2. Using 10 N/kg out of habit. AQA gives 9.8 N/kg; use it.
3. Mass in grams.
4. In rearranging, dividing by only one of m and g.

Mass and weight are often confused. Students may say "the book weighs 2 kg". Do not make this the focus of the lesson, but model the correct language: "its mass is 2 kg; gravity pulls it down with 19.6 N".
