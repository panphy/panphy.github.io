THOMSON · 1904

A positive sphere, with electrons inside.

Negative electrons are embedded in a spread of positive charge, like fruit in a pudding. There is no nucleus in this model.

🔎 Your observation mission

Zoom inside the sphere. The blue electrons sit inside it, not on an outer shell, and can only vibrate about fixed places. The + marks show positive charge spread through the whole atom.

Why did the idea change?

Some alpha particles bounced back in the gold-foil experiment. Spread-out positive charge could not explain these large deflections.

A model is an explanation, not a photograph.

Colours, particle sizes and distances are chosen to help you see the ideas. The nucleus and electrons are enlarged to make them visible; “Show true scale” in the Rutherford view shows how tiny the nucleus really is. Particle sizes are illustrative: they do not represent a mass ratio or actual relative sizes. Motion is illustrative, not a simulation of real electron trajectories. Real atoms are mostly empty space, and electrons do not follow neat planetary paths.

The alpha beam arrives evenly across the atom, as in the real experiment, where the beam was millions of atoms wide. Dashed paths are extra shots aimed straight at the centre so that a bounce-back appears quickly; they are never included in the counts. Because the nucleus is enlarged, even the counted rates are not real ones: in Geiger and Marsden’s experiment only about 1 in 8000 alpha particles bounced back. The electron cloud is drawn from hydrogen-like orbitals, a simplification for carbon.

These are simplified classroom versions of historical models. Each view uses six electrons for comparison; the Bohr view shows a carbon arrangement (2, 4). Rutherford’s nucleus is one positive region. Our Bohr view is a modern classroom adaptation: carbon-12 with six protons and six neutrons.
